# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Two — Saturday Morning

At nine fifty-seven on Saturday morning, Jamie Morgan sat in the passenger seat outside Ashcroft House holding a toolbox he was reasonably certain had been packed by somebody preparing him for military service.

His father had put everything in it.

Hammer. Screwdrivers. Pliers. Tape measure. Sandpaper. Scraper. Dust mask. Work gloves. A pencil sharpened at both ends for reasons Jamie had not been able to establish.

There was also a packed lunch in his rucksack, a flask, two chocolate bars and strict instructions not to behave like an idiot.

The last item had not physically fit in the toolbox, but Gareth had repeated it enough times that Jamie felt he was carrying it anyway.

“You can still come in,” Jamie said.

Gareth leaned across the steering wheel and looked at the house through the windscreen.

“No.”

“Why not?” Jamie asked.

“Because I’m going home.”

“You said you’d show me what to do.”

“I showed you how to sand wood yesterday,” Gareth said.

“For six minutes.”

“That was five minutes longer than it needed.”

Jamie looked towards the iron gates.

They were open again.

The rain had stopped during the night, but everything still looked wet. Drops hung from the laurel leaves. Water ran in a slow silver line along the gutter beside the lane.

The house waited above him.

“Dad.”

Gareth smiled.

“Ten o’clock.”

“It’s three minutes to.”

“Then you’re early. Evelyn will be delighted.”

Jamie turned sharply in his seat.

“Evelyn?”

Gareth shrugged.

“That’s her name.”

“You’re on first-name terms already?”

“She rang last night.”

Jamie stared at him.

“What for?”

“To make sure you were coming,” Gareth said.

“Did you tell her I’d died?”

“No.”

“Could have.”

Gareth pointed towards the passenger door.

“Out.”

Jamie climbed reluctantly from the car.

Gareth lowered the window.

“And Jamie?”

Jamie bent down to the open window.

“What?”

“Don’t do anything you don’t know how to do. If she asks you to climb on the roof, ring me.”

Jamie glanced up at Ashcroft House.

One of the chimneys leaned just enough to be concerning.

“What if she asks me to go in the cellar?”

Gareth considered that.

“Depends.”

“On what?”

“Whether she’s got a shovel.”

Gareth drove away before Jamie could answer.

“Brilliant.”

Jamie picked up the toolbox.

It was heavier than it had any right to be.

He started up the path.

The front door opened before he reached it.

Mrs Ashcroft stood there with her walking stick in one hand and a mug in the other.

She checked the watch on her wrist.

“Nine fifty-nine.”

Jamie stopped on the top step.

“You said ten.”

“I know what I said,” Mrs Ashcroft replied.

“So I’m early.”

“By approximately forty seconds.”

“Good start, then.”

“We shall see.”

She stepped aside.

Jamie entered.

The house felt different in daylight.

Not brighter, exactly. Ashcroft House seemed determined to resist brightness. But strips of pale morning sun reached through the tall windows and revealed things Jamie had missed on Tuesday evening: dust floating above the staircase, tiny cracks in the plaster, the deep red pattern in the hall rug and the carved faces hidden in the wooden banister posts.

The broken stained-glass window had been covered temporarily with clear plastic and a sheet of board around the worst of the hole.

The coloured fragments were gone.

Jamie looked up at it.

“Someone took the glass?”

“The restorer collected the loose pieces yesterday,” Mrs Ashcroft said.

“Can it definitely be fixed?”

She watched him for a moment.

“Mostly.”

Jamie nodded.

Mostly was not the same as yes.

“Tea?” she asked.

“Yeah, please.”

She handed him the mug she was holding.

Jamie stared at it.

“You made that for me?”

“No. I made it for a burglar, but you arrived first.”

He took it.

“Thanks.”

He drank.

His face tried to fold inwards.

Mrs Ashcroft noticed.

“Problem?”

“No.”

“Good.”

It was the strongest tea Jamie had ever encountered.

Not tasted.

Encountered.

It felt less like a drink and more like something that should have been issued a warning label.

He swallowed with difficulty.

“Lovely.”

“Liar.”

Jamie looked at her.

For the first time, she smiled properly.

Only briefly.

Then she pointed her stick towards a doorway on the left.

“Come along.”

---

His first job was not glamorous.

Jamie had expected something important.

Maybe replacing floorboards. Fixing the gate. Repairing one of the damaged doors.

Instead Mrs Ashcroft led him into a long room at the front of the house and pointed at the skirting board.

“Paint.”

Jamie looked at it.

“What about it?”

“It is on the wood.”

“That’s normally where paint goes.”

“Not twelve layers of it,” she said.

She handed him a scraper.

Jamie looked from the scraper to the skirting.

“You want me to take all that off?”

“I want you to take the loose material off without destroying the timber beneath it.”

“That’s going to take ages.”

“Yes.”

Jamie waited for the rest of the explanation.

There wasn’t one.

Mrs Ashcroft began to leave.

“Hang on.”

She looked back.

“How much of it?” Jamie asked.

“The room.”

Jamie stared around.

The room was enormous.

Skirting ran along three walls, disappeared behind a pair of heavy cabinets and continued beneath a bay window.

“You’re joking.”

“I rarely joke before lunch.”

“What happens after lunch?”

Mrs Ashcroft's mouth twitched.

“I become hilarious.”

She left.

Jamie looked down at the scraper.

Then at the skirting.

Then at the doorway.

“This is bullshit,” he whispered.

A voice behind him said, “She can hear through walls, you know.”

Jamie jumped so hard he nearly dropped the scraper.

The girl from the upstairs window was sitting on the broad sill of the bay window.

He had not heard her come in.

Up close, she was definitely older than him.

Maybe sixteen. Seventeen at most.

Her dark hair was tied messily behind her head, although several strands had escaped around her face. She wore black jeans, an oversized green jumper and thick socks but no shoes.

She was holding an apple.

Jamie recovered what remained of his dignity.

“How long have you been there?”

“Long enough,” the girl said.

“You walk quietly.”

“You scream quietly.”

“I didn’t scream.”

She bit into the apple.

“Mm.”

Jamie pointed the scraper at her.

“You were at the window Tuesday.”

“You were at the window first.”

“Technically the stone was.”

She smiled.

“That was impressive.”

“It was an accident.”

“Even more impressive.”

Jamie crouched by the skirting and attacked a bubble of paint with unnecessary force.

A strip peeled away.

The girl watched.

“You’re doing that wrong.”

Jamie stopped.

“You’re related to her, aren’t you?”

“What gave it away?” she asked.

“Immediate criticism.”

She laughed.

Jamie tried the scraper at a shallower angle.

Another piece lifted cleanly.

“That’s better,” she said.

Beneath it, faint against the old timber, was a tiny pencil drawing.

Jamie leaned closer.

It was a boy.

No bigger than his thumb, sketched directly onto the wood years ago and hidden beneath layer after layer of paint.

He rubbed the dust away with the side of his hand.

“What’s that?”

The girl slid off the windowsill and crouched beside him.

“Huh.”

“You’ve never seen it?” Jamie asked.

“No.”

Jamie tilted his head.

“Looks like a little boy.”

“Excellent detective work.”

“Think your gran knows it’s there?”

The girl stood again.

“Gran knows everything.”

“That’s terrifying.”

“You’ll get used to it.”

“Thanks, supervisor.”

“Any time.”

He glanced at her.

“So who are you?”

She looked offended.

“You don’t know?”

“No.”

“Wow.”

“What?”

“I thought my reputation had reached the lower streets.”

Jamie waited.

She held out her hand from the windowsill.

“Isabella Ashcroft.”

Jamie wiped his dusty palm on his jeans before shaking it.

“Jamie Morgan.”

“I know.”

“Everyone keeps saying that.”

“Gran gave a very detailed description,” Isabella said.

Jamie groaned.

She grinned.

“Izzy.”

“What?”

“Call me Izzy. Nobody calls me Isabella unless they’re angry or writing me out of a will.”

Jamie laughed.

For now it was just a joke made by a girl sitting in a dusty window, eating an apple while he worked off the cost of a broken one.

“Is she actually your gran?” Jamie asked.

“Unfortunately.”

From somewhere in the hall, Mrs Ashcroft called, “I heard that.”

Izzy raised her voice.

“Love you!”

“I doubt it,” Evelyn called back.

Jamie laughed again.

Izzy hopped down from the sill.

“She’s not a witch, by the way.”

“I never said she was.”

“You called this place the Witch House on Tuesday.”

Jamie froze.

Izzy walked towards the door.

“How do you know that?” he asked.

She turned backwards as she walked.

“You boys are louder than you think.”

Then she disappeared into the hall.

Jamie stared after her.

A second later Mrs Ashcroft appeared in the doorway.

“The Witch House?” she repeated.

Jamie closed his eyes.

“Oh, for God’s sake.”

Then he remembered the drawing.

“Mrs Ashcroft?”

“What?”

He pointed down at the skirting.

“Who’s that?”

She followed his finger.

For perhaps two seconds, Evelyn Ashcroft said nothing.

Then something softened behind her eyes.

“Goodness,” she said. “I haven’t seen him in years.”

Jamie looked at the tiny pencil boy again.

“So who is he?”

Evelyn straightened.

“Nobody you know.”

She handed him another scraper.

“Use both hands, artillery division.”

And for reasons Jamie could not yet explain, he found himself smiling.
