# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Four — Catherine's Window

Jamie thought about the window all week.

He tried not to.

That was the problem with trying not to think about something. It became the only thing his brain wanted to do.

He thought about it in maths while Mr Walters explained quadratic equations.

He thought about it at lunch while Callum and Leon argued over whether a chicken tikka baguette counted as Indian food.

He thought about it in bed on Thursday night, staring at the ceiling while rain clicked against his bedroom window.

A hundred-and-fifty-year-old stained-glass window would have been bad enough.

A dead woman's stained-glass window felt different.

Personal.

Callum noticed first.

“You've gone weird.”

Jamie looked up from his chips.

“What?”

“You keep staring at walls.”

“I’m eating.”

“You’ve had that chip in your hand for about five minutes.”

Jamie ate it.

Leon leaned across the table.

“The witch curse.”

Jamie rolled his eyes.

“Shut up.”

“She’s got him.”

“Who?” Callum asked.

“The old woman.”

Callum grinned.

“Imagine Jamie getting adopted by the witch.”

Jamie shoved his tray away.

“She’s not a witch.”

The words came out faster than he intended.

Both boys looked at him.

Callum's eyebrows rose.

“Ooooh.”

“What?”

“You defended her.”

“I did not.”

“You literally just defended her.”

“I said she’s not a witch. Because she’s obviously not a witch.”

Leon nodded solemnly.

“Classic first stage of witch possession.”

Jamie gave him two fingers.

Callum laughed.

“So what’s the granddaughter like?”

Jamie froze.

“Who?”

Callum stared at him.

“You are shit at this.”

“What are you talking about?”

“My brother said there’s some girl living there.”

“Your brother also said Mrs Ashcroft buried her husband under the greenhouse.”

“Did she?”

“No.”

“You checked?”

“No.”

“So you don't know.”

Jamie stood up with his tray.

“I hate both of you.”

Callum called after him.

“Ask her if she’s single!”

Jamie kept walking.

His ears had gone red.

---

Saturday arrived dry and bright.

For once Ashcroft House didn't look sinister.

Sunlight touched the upper windows and turned the wet slate roof silver. The valley below had emerged from a week of rain looking scrubbed clean.

Jamie walked through the gate at nine fifty-four.

He told himself the extra six minutes had nothing to do with Izzy.

Mrs Ashcroft was waiting in the hall.

“You're early.”

Jamie stopped.

“Is that bad?”

“Suspicious.”

“My dad dropped me.”

“I see.”

“Where’s Izzy?”

Evelyn's eyes sharpened.

Jamie immediately wished he had asked literally anything else.

“Why?”

“No reason.”

“You arrived six minutes early and have asked for my granddaughter before removing your coat.”

“I didn't ask for her.”

“You asked where she was.”

“That’s different.”

“Is it?”

Jamie took off his coat.

“Can I just start work?”

“I should think so.”

He heard the smallest trace of amusement in her voice as she walked away.

“Kitchen first.”

Jamie followed.

A man and woman were sitting at the long kitchen table.

The man was in his sixties with a grey beard and round glasses. The woman beside him had short white hair and a pencil tucked behind one ear. Spread across the table were sheets of tracing paper, photographs and several small numbered bags containing pieces of coloured glass.

Jamie stopped.

The window.

Mrs Ashcroft gestured towards him.

“This is Jamie Morgan.”

The woman looked up.

“The projectile specialist.”

Jamie closed his eyes.

“Does everyone know?”

Apparently they did.

The man smiled.

“I’m Peter Bowen. This is my wife, Ruth. We restore historic glass.”

Jamie looked at the fragments.

One bag held pieces of deep blue glass. Another contained amber slivers no bigger than fingernails.

“You can fix it?”

Ruth nodded.

“We can restore it.”

“What’s the difference?”

“A repair makes something functional again. Restoration tries to respect what it was.”

Jamie looked at Evelyn.

She said nothing.

Ruth spread a photograph across the table.

It showed the window before the damage.

The design was clearer here than Jamie had ever seen it from the lane.

Branches twisted upward from the bottom, carrying small blue flowers and amber leaves. Near the centre, hidden among the branches, was a tiny bird picked out in red glass.

Jamie hadn't noticed it before.

“Catherine drew this?” he asked.

Evelyn sat opposite him.

“When she was nineteen.”

“Was she an artist?”

“No.”

Izzy's voice came from the doorway.

Jamie turned.

She was wearing paint-splattered dungarees over a black T-shirt, her hair tied up again.

“She thought she was.”

Evelyn looked at her.

“Your mother was an excellent draughtswoman.”

“She drew horses with legs like tables.”

“She was nine.”

“They were terrible horses.”

Ruth smiled to herself.

Izzy came to the table and leaned over Jamie's shoulder.

“You found the bird.”

Jamie looked at her.

“You knew about it?”

“Mum put it in there for Gran.”

He glanced at Evelyn.

“Why a bird?”

Evelyn took her time answering.

“A private joke.”

Izzy rolled her eyes.

“Which means classified.”

“It means private.”

“Same thing in this house.”

Peter picked up one of the numbered bags.

“We've recovered most of the important pieces. Some will have to be remade.”

Jamie frowned.

“Can you make old glass?”

“Not old glass. But we can source hand-made glass that behaves similarly. The trick is not making the new work look too perfect.”

“Why?”

“Because perfect would be dishonest.”

That sentence stayed with Jamie.

He looked again at the photograph.

“How much will it cost?”

Evelyn answered immediately.

“That is not your concern.”

“It kind of is.”

“No.”

“I broke it.”

“Yes. We have established that repeatedly.”

“So how much?”

“Jamie.”

He crossed his arms.

Evelyn's expression hardened.

Izzy watched the two of them like she was at Wimbledon.

Peter cleared his throat.

“We don't need to discuss figures today.”

Jamie looked at him.

“Is it thousands?”

Peter made the mistake of hesitating.

Jamie's stomach sank.

“Oh.”

Evelyn's voice softened slightly.

“Your father is not paying for the glass.”

“He said he would.”

“And I told him he wouldn't.”

“Why?”

“Because I can afford it.”

Jamie glanced around the kitchen.

A bucket sat beneath a leak near the back door.

One cupboard was held closed with string.

The range cooker looked older than Gareth.

Evelyn followed his gaze.

“Again, if you have an observation—”

“I didn’t say anything.”

“You didn't need to.”

Izzy covered her mouth to hide a laugh.

Jamie leaned on the table.

“I still want to help.”

“With what?” Ruth asked.

“The window.”

Peter shook his head kindly.

“The actual leading and glasswork takes training.”

“I know. I don't mean that.”

He looked at Evelyn.

“I’ll do extra work.”

“You are already doing extra work.”

“Then more.”

Evelyn studied him.

“Why?”

Jamie looked at the photograph of Catherine's window.

He could have said because Dad was making him.

Because he felt guilty.

Because Izzy was watching.

All of those things were true.

None of them was the real answer.

“Because it shouldn't stay broken because of me.”

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then Ruth slid the photograph towards him.

“We'll need the wooden surround stripped and checked before the glass goes back.”

Jamie looked at her.

“Can I do that?”

“Under supervision.”

Evelyn opened her mouth.

Ruth raised a hand.

“He asked.”

Evelyn looked at Jamie.

“Very well.”

Jamie smiled before he could stop himself.

Izzy nudged his shoulder.

“Careful. At this rate she'll have you restoring the roof.”

“Not a chance.”

From the other side of the table Evelyn said, “The west guttering requires attention.”

Jamie pointed at Izzy.

“This is your fault.”

Izzy laughed.

---

Later, Jamie stood on the first-floor landing beneath Catherine's broken window.

Peter had shown him how to remove the cracked layers of paint from the frame without gouging the old timber.

Slowly.

That was the word everyone kept using.

Everything about repairing Ashcroft House seemed to require doing things slowly.

Jamie wasn't naturally talented at slowly.

He worked anyway.

After a while, Evelyn appeared beside him.

She watched for several minutes without speaking.

Jamie glanced down.

“Am I doing it wrong?”

“No.”

He waited.

“That’s it?”

“What more would you like?”

“I don't know. Fireworks.”

“You have already demonstrated that you should not be trusted with projectiles.”

Jamie smiled.

He returned to the frame.

“What was the private joke?”

Evelyn said nothing.

“The bird.”

“I know which private joke you mean.”

Jamie kept scraping.

“I won't tell anyone.”

“I spent thirty-five years hearing that sentence from people who absolutely intended to tell someone.”

His hand stopped.

He looked down at her.

“What?”

Evelyn's face gave nothing away.

“Nothing.”

“No, hang on.”

She turned towards the staircase.

“What do you mean thirty-five years?”

“Keep the scraper flat, Jamie.”

“Mrs Ashcroft.”

She descended one step.

Jamie grinned.

“What did you do?”

Evelyn looked back at him.

For the first time since he'd met her, Jamie saw something almost mischievous in her eyes.

“Nothing you would believe.”

Then she went downstairs.

Jamie stared after her.

From the corridor behind him, Izzy said, “Told you.”

Jamie turned.

“Told me what?”

Izzy smiled.

“Classified.”
