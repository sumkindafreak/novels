# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Nine — The Spy

For the next seven days, Jamie told nobody that Evelyn Ashcroft had worked for MI5.

This was not because he had suddenly developed a mature respect for privacy.

It was because he knew exactly what Callum would do with the information.

By Monday lunchtime Mrs Ashcroft would have assassinated three Soviet generals, escaped East Berlin inside a suitcase and slept with James Bond.

By Friday she would probably have killed Hitler.

So Jamie said nothing.

He did, however, spend an unreasonable amount of time online.

He read about the Security Service.

Counter-espionage.

Counter-subversion.

Soviet intelligence officers operating under diplomatic cover.

Double agents.

Surveillance.

Dead drops.

Files that remained secret for decades.

By Saturday his head was full of questions.

Evelyn took one look at him when he arrived and sighed.

“No.”

Jamie hadn't even removed his coat.

“I didn't say anything.”

“You have the expression.”

“What expression?”

“The one people wear immediately before asking me something tiresome.”

“I've been researching,” Jamie said.

“That is precisely what I feared.”

Izzy appeared behind Evelyn carrying a bowl of cereal.

“He’s been Googling you.”

“I have not been Googling her,” Jamie said.

Evelyn looked at him.

Jamie hesitated.

“I Googled MI5.”

“Marginally less disturbing.”

He followed them into the kitchen.

“Were you counter-espionage?”

Evelyn opened a cupboard.

“Eventually.”

Jamie stopped.

She had answered.

Izzy noticed too.

“Oh, he's getting upgraded.”

Evelyn ignored her.

“What does eventually mean?” Jamie asked.

“It means not immediately.”

“I know what the word means.”

“That places you ahead of several former colleagues.”

Jamie sat at the table.

“What did you start doing?”

“Administrative work. Research. Analysis.”

“Boring stuff?”

“Usually.”

“So no car chases?”

“No.”

“Explosions?”

“Not professionally.”

Jamie stared.

Izzy stopped eating.

Evelyn looked between them.

“That was a joke.”

“Oh,” Jamie said.

“Your disappointment is insulting.”

Jamie grinned.

“Did you ever actually catch someone?”

Evelyn poured hot water into a teapot.

“What do you imagine catching looks like?”

“I don't know. Arresting them?”

“We did not arrest people. The police do that.”

“So what did you do?” Jamie asked.

“Found out what was true.”

The answer was so ordinary that Jamie almost missed it.

Evelyn placed the teapot on the table.

“That is most intelligence work, Jamie. Television has done terrible damage to the profession. Ninety per cent of the job was information, patience and paperwork.”

“What was the other ten per cent?”

“More paperwork.”

Izzy laughed.

Jamie groaned.

Evelyn sat.

“In the seventies, a great deal of our concern involved Soviet intelligence. People operating in Britain under perfectly respectable titles while doing rather less respectable work.”

“Like diplomats?” Jamie asked.

“Sometimes.”

“How could you tell?”

“You rarely could simply by looking.”

“So how?”

“Patterns.”

Evelyn stirred her tea.

“People are creatures of pattern. Where they go. Who they meet. When they change a routine. What they do immediately before and after something important.”

Jamie leaned forward.

“Did you follow them?”

“Teams did.”

“But you said you followed a Russian once.”

Evelyn gave him a narrow look.

“You remember inconveniently well.”

“You said it.”

“I was young and temporarily attached to an operation. It was not my usual role.”

“What happened?”

“No.”

“Come on.”

“No.”

“Just one story.”

Evelyn looked at Izzy.

Izzy raised her spoon.

“I also vote story.”

“You do not have a vote,” Evelyn told her.

“Democracy is dying in this kitchen.”

Evelyn sighed.

“One story.”

Jamie sat up straighter.

“And if either of you interrupts me with a James Bond question, I stop.”

Jamie nodded immediately.

Izzy mimed zipping her mouth.

Evelyn looked towards the window as though the memory existed somewhere beyond it.

“It was 1973. There was a man attached to the Soviet trade delegation. I'll call him Sergei, although that was not his name.”

Jamie smiled.

“Obviously.”

Evelyn looked at him.

“Sorry.”

“Sergei had developed a habit of visiting a particular bookshop. There was nothing unusual about the shop. Nothing obviously unusual about the people who used it.”

“What sort of books?” Izzy asked.

Evelyn stared.

Izzy lowered her spoon.

“Sorry.”

“On three occasions Sergei entered the shop,” Evelyn continued. “On three occasions another man entered within twenty minutes. They never spoke. They were never seen together outside.”

Jamie frowned.

“So?”

“So nothing. That was the point.”

“But you thought they were meeting.”

“We thought information might be passing between them.”

“How?” Jamie asked.

Evelyn smiled faintly.

“That was what we wanted to know.”

Jamie waited.

“The fourth time, Sergei changed his routine. Different Underground station. Different route. He stopped twice for no obvious reason. Entered a café. Left by another door.”

“He knew he was being followed,” Jamie said.

“Perhaps.”

“Were you following him?”

“Part of the day.”

Jamie could barely believe the woman describing this was the same woman who had spent last Saturday arguing with him about whether filler needed another hour to dry.

“What happened?”

“Nothing dramatic. He returned to his office. The other man did not appear.”

Jamie looked disappointed.

“That’s it?”

“That day.”

“So what did you learn?”

“That he was frightened.”

Jamie frowned.

“How?”

“Because he changed.”

Evelyn leaned back.

“The important thing is not always what somebody does. Sometimes it is what they suddenly stop doing.”

Jamie thought about it.

“So did you catch him?”

Evelyn smiled.

“Eventually he went home earlier than planned.”

“Deported?”

“I didn't say that.”

“That means yes.”

“It means I didn't say that.”

Izzy laughed.

Jamie sat back.

“That is actually cool.”

“It mostly involved standing in rain,” Evelyn said.

“Still cool.”

Evelyn shook her head.

Then she pointed towards his coat.

“Stand up.”

“What?”

“Stand up.”

Jamie did.

“Go to the front door.”

“Why?”

“Because you are irritating me.”

Izzy snorted.

Jamie walked into the hall.

Evelyn followed.

“Look outside.”

He looked through the glass beside the front door.

A delivery van was parked across the lane. A man in a blue jacket stood beside it checking something on a clipboard.

A woman with a small dog was walking downhill.

Two boys on bikes passed the gate.

“All right,” Evelyn said. “Turn around.”

Jamie did.

“What colour was the dog's lead?”

He blinked.

“What?”

“The lead.”

“I don't know.”

“What was the delivery driver wearing on his head?”

“I didn't—”

“How many bicycles?”

“Two.”

“What colour?”

“One was red.”

“And the other?”

Jamie frowned.

“Black?”

“Blue.”

“You saw all that?”

“Yes.”

“You knew you were going to ask.”

“Of course.”

“That’s cheating.”

“No. That is preparing.”

Izzy leaned against the staircase.

“She used to do this to me when I was little.”

Jamie looked over at her.

“Why?”

“Because she’s mental.”

Evelyn ignored her.

“Again.”

Jamie looked through the door.

This time he tried to see everything.

A silver car passed.

There were three people inside.

The woman with the dog had stopped near the chapel.

Green lead.

The delivery driver's jacket had a tear near the shoulder.

His clipboard was orange.

There was mud on one of the gate pillars.

A crisp packet was caught beneath the hedge.

Jamie turned.

Evelyn asked six questions.

He got four right.

“Better,” she said.

Jamie smiled.

“Again.”

Evelyn raised an eyebrow.

“You have work to do.”

“Oh, come on.”

“Skirting board.”

Jamie groaned.

As he walked away Evelyn called after him.

“Jamie.”

He turned.

She tapped one finger against her temple.

“People spend their lives looking.”

Jamie waited.

“Very few of them notice.”

He nodded.

Years later, he would remember that sentence more clearly than almost anything else she taught him.

At fifteen, he simply went back to the morning room and found his scraper.
