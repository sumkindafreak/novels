# Ashcroft House

## Chapter One — The Witch House

Jamie Morgan had thrown plenty of stones in his life.

Most of them had gone into rivers.

Some had gone at trees, lampposts, abandoned shopping trolleys and, on one memorable occasion, the rear wheel of Callum Rees's bike while Callum was still riding it.

He had never thrown one through a hundred-and-fifty-year-old stained-glass window.

Until Tuesday.

“Go on, then.”

Callum grinned at him from the other side of the lane, hands buried in the pockets of his black school trousers. His tie had already disappeared. It was probably in his bag, along with three weeks of unfinished maths homework and a crushed can of energy drink he’d been told not to bring into school.

Jamie looked at the house.

“You do it.”

“I said it first.”

“That doesn’t even make sense.”

“It does.”

Leon Price, who had been trying to balance along the low stone wall beside them, jumped down into the wet grass.

“Just chuck it at the gate or something.”

Jamie turned the stone over in his fingers.

It was flat and grey, about the size of a digestive biscuit. He’d picked it up without thinking when they came off the main road and started up the narrow lane behind the old chapel.

Everything around here seemed to be made from the same grey stone.

Walls. Houses. Steps. Bridges. The old retaining walls that held the mountain back from the streets below.

Even the sky looked made of it today.

At the top of the lane, Ashcroft House sat behind rusting iron gates and two enormous stone pillars blackened by years of rain.

Most people only ever saw pieces of it.

A chimney above the trees.

A slate roof through bare branches in winter.

One of the upstairs windows when the wind moved the ivy aside.

The garden had swallowed nearly everything else.

Laurel bushes pushed through the railings. Brambles curled around the bars of the gate. Ivy had spread across one side of the house so thickly that from a distance it looked as though the building was slowly being dragged underground.

Jamie had passed it hundreds of times.

He had never been inside.

Nobody he knew had.

Which, naturally, meant everybody knew everything about it.

The woman who lived there was a witch.

Or a millionaire.

Or dead.

Or ninety-eight.

Or had killed her husband and buried him beneath the greenhouse.

Callum’s older brother swore blind that when he was twelve he’d climbed the wall at night and seen a woman standing at an upstairs window wearing a white nightdress.

Jamie had pointed out that this sounded less like evidence of witchcraft and more like evidence that an old woman owned a nightdress.

Callum’s brother had called him a knob.

The story had survived.

Jamie looked up at the house again.

One window stood out from all the others.

It was tall and narrow, set above the left side of the front entrance where the stonework rose into a little pointed gable. Unlike the dark windows around it, this one held pieces of red, blue and amber glass arranged in twisting branches and flowers.

Even under the miserable afternoon sky, it caught what little light there was.

“Bet you can’t hit the wall from here,” Callum said.

Jamie snorted.

“I can hit the wall.”

“Go on, then.”

“Why are you so desperate to see me throw a stone?”

“Because your life’s boring.”

“You spent Saturday watching your uncle change a clutch.”

“Yeah, and?”

Leon laughed.

Jamie glanced down the lane.

Nobody was coming.

He should have dropped the stone.

He would think about that later.

He would think about the exact moment his fingers tightened around it, about the stupid little surge of pride that came from knowing Callum expected him to bottle it, and about how unbelievably easy it would have been to just put the thing back on the wall.

Instead, Jamie stepped backwards.

“Wall,” he said.

Callum raised both hands.

“Wall.”

Jamie threw.

For half a second, it was perfect.

The stone sailed over the gate, turning once in the air.

Then Jamie realised it was too high.

“Ah.”

The stone struck the stained-glass window almost exactly in the centre.

The sound was enormous.

Not a neat little crack.

Not the sharp tinkling Jamie had expected from television.

It went through with a violent crash that seemed to roll across the entire valley.

Red and amber glass burst inward.

A larger blue piece dropped, hit the stone ledge beneath the window and shattered again.

For one long second, none of them moved.

Jamie stared at the hole.

Callum whispered, “Oh, shit.”

Then an alarm began screaming somewhere inside the house.

Leon was already running.

“MOVE!”

Jamie didn’t need telling twice.

They tore down the lane.

Callum nearly slipped on the wet pavement and grabbed Jamie’s blazer to stay upright, almost taking both of them down.

Behind them the alarm continued to howl.

Jamie’s heart hammered so hard it hurt.

He ran past the chapel, around the corner and down the steps towards the row of terraces below.

Leon disappeared between two parked cars.

Callum vaulted a garden wall.

Jamie followed neither of them.

He ran straight on, because running straight on felt like the sort of thing an innocent person might do.

This was immediately ruined by the fact that he was sprinting.

He slowed to a fast walk.

Then slower.

Then, because panic had apparently removed every useful thought from his head, he took his phone out and pretended to look at it.

His hands were shaking.

The alarm stopped.

Jamie looked back.

Nothing.

No police car.

No witch.

No furious millionaire with a shotgun.

He let out the breath he’d been holding.

His phone buzzed.

**CALLUM:** mate

Another message appeared.

**CALLUM:** MATE

Then:

**CALLUM:** that was fucking class

Jamie stared at it.

**JAMIE:** you told me to throw it

The reply came immediately.

**CALLUM:** at the WALL you weapon

Jamie shoved his phone back into his pocket.

“Brilliant.”

He started home.

---

By six o’clock, Jamie had almost convinced himself he’d got away with it.

This was helped considerably by the fact that nobody had kicked his front door in.

His dad was in the kitchen when Jamie got home from school, standing at the cooker with a wooden spoon in one hand and his reading glasses pushed up onto his head.

Gareth Morgan was not a frightening man by normal standards.

He was forty-two, broad across the shoulders, permanently tired around the eyes and usually smelled faintly of metal, sawdust or whatever he had been fixing that day.

He almost never shouted.

Jamie would have preferred shouting.

Shouting burned itself out.

Gareth had another thing he did instead.

He went quiet.

The quieter Gareth got, the more trouble Jamie was in.

“You all right?” Gareth asked.

“Yeah.”

“How was school?”

“Fine.”

“Anything happen?”

Jamie froze for approximately half a heartbeat.

“No.”

Gareth stirred the pan.

“Nothing interesting?”

“Nope.”

“Good day, then?”

“Yeah.”

“Lovely.”

Jamie watched him.

Gareth tasted the sauce, frowned at it and reached for the salt.

This was normal.

Normal kitchen.

Normal tea.

Normal dad.

Nobody knew.

Jamie relaxed.

“You want me to put plates out?”

“Go on, then.”

Jamie opened the cupboard.

His father said, “Who’s Mrs Ashcroft?”

Jamie dropped a plate.

It didn’t smash.

Somehow that made things worse.

He caught it against the worktop and stood completely still.

Behind him, Gareth turned the cooker off.

Jamie slowly looked over his shoulder.

His father removed his glasses from the top of his head and folded them carefully onto the counter.

That was bad.

Very bad.

“I don’t know,” Jamie said.

Gareth nodded once.

“Try again.”

Jamie considered several options.

None were good.

“The old woman up by the chapel?”

“So you do know.”

“I know who she is. I don’t know her.”

“Apparently she knows you.”

Jamie’s stomach dropped.

“How?”

Gareth stared at him.

Jamie realised too late that this was not the question an innocent person would ask.

He tried again.

“I mean—why?”

“Jamie.”

Silence.

Rain tapped against the kitchen window.

Somebody’s dog barked three houses down.

Gareth picked up his phone from beside the kettle and placed it on the table.

“Mrs Ashcroft rang me twenty minutes ago.”

Jamie looked at the phone as though it personally had betrayed him.

“How’s she got your number?”

“That’s what you’re worried about?”

“No.”

“Because I can tell you, if you like.”

Jamie said nothing.

“She rang the school.”

“Oh.”

“She gave them a description.”

Jamie stared at the floor.

“A very good description, as it happens.”

“It could’ve been anyone.”

“Apparently this mysterious anyone was wearing your school uniform, carrying your school bag, has brown hair, white trainers with a blue stripe, and was shouting ‘Callum, you prick, wait for me’ while running down the lane.”

Jamie closed his eyes.

Callum.

Of course.

Gareth waited.

Eventually Jamie muttered, “It was an accident.”

“Excellent. We’ve reached the truth already.”

“I wasn’t aiming at the window.”

“What were you aiming at?”

“The wall.”

Gareth looked at him for a long moment.

“The house has got a wall around it, Jamie.”

“I know.”

“So to hit the house, you threw a stone over one wall and at another wall?”

“When you say it like that—”

“How would you like me to say it?”

Jamie didn’t answer.

Gareth pulled out one of the kitchen chairs and sat down.

“Come here.”

That was worse than being sent to his room.

Jamie sat opposite him.

His father folded his arms.

“What did you break?”

“A window.”

“What sort of window?”

Jamie hesitated.

“An old one.”

Gareth’s eyes narrowed.

“How old?”

“I don’t know.”

“Ordinary glass?”

Jamie looked away.

“Jamie.”

“Stained glass.”

Gareth rubbed both hands over his face.

“Oh, for God’s sake.”

“It wasn’t massive.”

“You threw a rock through a stained-glass window in a Victorian house.”

“It wasn’t a rock.”

Gareth lowered his hands.

Jamie immediately regretted speaking.

“What was it?”

“A stone.”

His father stared at him.

Jamie swallowed.

“Sorry.”

Gareth stood up.

“Coat.”

“What?”

“Get your coat.”

“Why?”

“We’re going up there.”

“Now?”

“No, Jamie. Christmas.”

Jamie sank further into the chair.

“Dad.”

“Coat.”

“Can’t you just ring her?”

“I could.”

“I’ll apologise.”

“You will.”

“On the phone.”

“You won’t.”

Jamie looked towards the stairs as though an emergency escape route might suddenly appear.

Gareth pointed at the hallway.

“Coat.”

---

It was properly raining by the time they reached Ashcroft House.

Not heavy rain. Valley rain.

Fine, cold and relentless, hanging in the air and finding its way inside collars no matter how tightly they were pulled shut.

Jamie walked half a step behind his father.

The house looked different now.

Earlier, with Callum and Leon, it had been a target. A dare. A story they could tell later.

Now it seemed enormous.

The iron gates stood open.

Jamie had never seen them open before.

One leaned slightly lower than the other, its hinges thick with rust. Beyond them, a cracked stone path climbed through the overgrown garden towards the house.

Wet ivy shone black against the walls.

The conservatory on the right looked as though one hard winter might finish it off completely. Several glass panels were missing from the roof, replaced with sheets of cloudy plastic held down by pieces of timber.

One gutter had pulled away from the stonework and hung at an angle.

Higher up was the window.

Jamie stopped.

From the lane, the damage had looked bad.

From here it looked awful.

The hole was roughly the size of his head.

Fine cracks ran through several surrounding pieces. Part of the coloured pattern had collapsed entirely, leaving jagged fragments around an empty black shape.

Gareth followed his gaze.

“Nice work.”

Jamie shoved his hands deeper into his pockets.

“I said it was an accident.”

“You accidentally threw it?”

“No.”

“Then the broken window is the consequence. Not the accident.”

Jamie hated when his dad said things that sounded annoyingly correct.

They reached the front door.

It was dark green, almost black with age, set beneath a stone porch supported by two square pillars. An old brass bell pull hung beside it.

Gareth reached for it.

Jamie caught his arm.

“Wait.”

His dad looked down at his hand.

Jamie let go.

“What?” Gareth asked.

Jamie had no idea.

He looked at the door.

All the stories about the house came rushing back at once.

The dead husband.

The witch.

The grave under the greenhouse.

Obviously none of it was true.

Probably.

“Nothing.”

Gareth pulled the bell.

Somewhere deep inside Ashcroft House, something clanged.

Not chimed.

Clanged.

Jamie glanced at his father.

Gareth almost smiled.

“Very welcoming.”

Footsteps approached from inside.

Slow ones.

Then locks began opening.

One.

Two.

Three.

Jamie suddenly wondered whether apologising by phone had really been such an unreasonable suggestion.

The door opened.

The woman standing behind it was not what he expected.

For one thing, she was small.

Jamie had somehow imagined someone enormous. Bent-backed perhaps, with wild white hair and fingernails like talons.

Instead Mrs Ashcroft stood perfectly straight despite the polished wooden walking stick in her right hand.

Her silver hair was cut neatly at her jaw. She wore dark trousers, a cream blouse and a burgundy cardigan with every button done up. A thin gold chain disappeared beneath the collar.

She looked old.

Very old.

But there was nothing weak about her face.

Her eyes moved first to Gareth.

Then to Jamie.

They stopped there.

Jamie had the uncomfortable sensation of being inspected rather than looked at.

“Mr Morgan,” she said.

Her voice was crisp, educated and considerably stronger than he expected.

“Mrs Ashcroft.” Gareth offered his hand. “Thanks for seeing us.”

She shook it once.

Then looked back at Jamie.

“And this must be the artillery division.”

Gareth made a noise that might have been a cough.

Jamie felt his face burn.

“Jamie,” he said.

“I’m aware.”

“Right.”

Mrs Ashcroft waited.

Gareth nudged Jamie lightly between the shoulder blades.

Jamie shot him a look.

His father raised his eyebrows.

Jamie faced the old woman again.

“I’m sorry.”

Mrs Ashcroft said nothing.

“I didn’t mean to hit the window.”

Still nothing.

“I know that doesn’t make it better.”

Her expression changed by perhaps half a millimetre.

Jamie continued.

“It was stupid.”

“Yes.”

“And… I’m sorry.”

“Yes.”

Jamie looked at his dad.

Gareth seemed perfectly happy to leave him there forever.

Mrs Ashcroft turned away.

“Come inside.”

Jamie blinked.

“We are rather efficiently heating the mountain.”

The entrance hall swallowed them.

Jamie forgot about apologising.

He forgot about the rain.

For several seconds, he even forgot that he was in trouble.

Ashcroft House was enormous inside.

A wide staircase rose ahead of them and split at a landing beneath the shattered stained-glass window. Dark wooden panels covered the lower half of the walls. Above them hung paintings, photographs and framed maps beneath a ceiling so high Jamie couldn't imagine how anyone changed the light bulbs.

Everything smelled faintly of old books, furniture polish and damp stone.

There were too many doors.

A grandfather clock ticked somewhere to his left.

The place was beautiful.

It was also falling apart.

A bucket sat beneath a brown stain in the ceiling.

Wallpaper had peeled away beside one doorway.

A section of decorative plaster near the stairs had cracked clean through.

One of the wooden floorboards moved beneath Gareth's boot with a dull creak.

Jamie looked up at the broken window.

From inside, it was worse again.

Fragments of coloured glass still lay on the landing despite someone having swept most of the mess into a careful pile.

The remaining design showed branches curling around small blue flowers.

A large section of one branch was simply gone.

Mrs Ashcroft followed his eyes.

“That window has survived two world wars, seventeen prime ministers, a lightning strike, subsidence, and a particularly enthusiastic window cleaner in 1986.”

Jamie swallowed.

“Sorry.”

“And then it met you.”

Gareth coughed again.

This time Jamie knew for certain he was hiding a laugh.

Mrs Ashcroft walked towards the staircase.

“I've had someone assess the damage.”

Gareth's expression became serious.

“And?”

“It can be restored.”

“Good.”

“It will not be inexpensive.”

“No.”

“I can cover it,” Gareth said. “Whatever the cost is. We’ll sort it.”

Jamie looked at him.

He knew money wasn't endless in their house.

His father must have seen the look because he didn’t return it.

Mrs Ashcroft studied Gareth for a moment.

“I assumed that would be your answer.”

“It’s his damage.”

“Yes.”

Gareth nodded.

“So send me the bill.”

Mrs Ashcroft looked at Jamie.

“No.”

Jamie frowned.

Gareth did too.

“Sorry?”

“I said no.”

Mrs Ashcroft tapped the end of her stick once against the wooden floor.

“I don't require your money, Mr Morgan.”

Jamie glanced around the decaying entrance hall.

He managed not to say anything.

Mrs Ashcroft noticed anyway.

Her eyes narrowed.

“Do you have an observation, James?”

“Jamie.”

“I prefer James.”

“Well, I prefer Jamie.”

Gareth closed his eyes briefly.

Mrs Ashcroft stared at Jamie.

Then, unexpectedly, the corner of her mouth moved.

Not quite a smile.

But close.

“Very well. Jamie.”

She turned to Gareth.

“You said he should take responsibility.”

“He should.”

“Then allow him to.”

Jamie looked between them.

A horrible feeling began forming in his stomach.

Gareth seemed to have the same thought.

“What exactly did you have in mind?”

Mrs Ashcroft gestured with her walking stick towards the hall.

For the first time, Jamie really looked at everything that needed doing.

The peeling paint.

The cracked skirting boards.

The damp patch.

The sticking door.

The loose section of banister.

Then he remembered the gutter outside.

And the conservatory.

And the garden.

“No,” Jamie said.

Neither adult looked at him.

Mrs Ashcroft continued calmly.

“The restoration specialist will repair the glass. I would not trust your son with a butter knife at present, let alone lead came.”

Jamie opened his mouth.

Gareth pointed at him without looking.

Jamie closed it.

“But there is plenty of ordinary work here,” she continued. “Work which has been neglected for far too long.”

“You want him to work off the cost?” Gareth asked.

“I want him to understand that damage is easier to cause than repair.”

Silence.

Jamie stared at his father.

Gareth stared at Mrs Ashcroft.

Then he looked around the hall.

Jamie could practically see the idea settling into place behind his eyes.

“No,” Jamie said again.

Gareth nodded slowly.

“I like it.”

“Dad.”

“How often?”

“Dad.”

“Saturday mornings to begin with.”

Jamie stepped forward.

“You cannot be serious.”

Gareth finally looked at him.

“You broke her window.”

“I said sorry.”

“Good start.”

“I'll pay you back.”

“With what?”

Jamie hesitated.

“Birthday money.”

“You spent your birthday money.”

“I’ll have another birthday.”

Mrs Ashcroft made a tiny sound that might have been a laugh.

Jamie turned to her.

“This is child labour.”

“It is Saturday morning.”

“Same thing.”

“Then I suggest you contact your union.”

Gareth was openly smiling now.

Jamie hated both of them.

Mrs Ashcroft held out her hand.

For a moment, Jamie thought she wanted him to shake it.

Then he realised she was pointing towards the shattered window.

“Ten o'clock,” she said. “This Saturday.”

Jamie looked up at the broken coloured glass.

Rainwater had begun collecting on the outside ledge.

Beyond it he could see grey sky, bare branches and the roofs of the valley below.

He had the sudden ridiculous feeling that the house itself was looking back at him.

“Fine.”

Mrs Ashcroft raised an eyebrow.

Gareth waited.

Jamie sighed.

“Fine. Ten o'clock.”

“Excellent.”

She turned towards the door.

Apparently they had been dismissed.

Jamie followed his father back outside.

The rain had grown heavier.

They were halfway down the path when a movement above made Jamie stop.

One of the curtains on the second floor shifted.

A face appeared behind the glass.

Not Mrs Ashcroft.

A girl.

She looked about his age. Maybe older.

Dark hair fell loose around her shoulders. She had one hand against the window and was watching him with an expression he couldn't quite read.

Then she smiled.

Not a polite smile.

An amused one.

She had obviously heard everything.

Jamie stood in the rain staring up at her.

The girl lifted her hand and gave him a tiny wave.

Then the curtain dropped back into place.

“Jamie?”

He looked towards the gate.

His dad was waiting.

“What?”

“You coming?”

Jamie glanced once more at the upstairs window.

Nobody was there.

He shoved his hands into his pockets and walked after Gareth.

Saturday morning suddenly didn't seem quite as bad.
