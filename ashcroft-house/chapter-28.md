# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Twenty-Eight — Ashcroft

By the following September, almost everything had acquired a legal word.

Evelyn's death was a murder.

What happened to Jamie was attempted murder.

The house was an asset.

The money was an estate.

Izzy was a defendant.

Jamie was a witness.

People who had once eaten stew around the same table became names in statements and bundles of evidence.

Jamie hated all of it.

Izzy eventually pleaded guilty.

Jamie did not attend every hearing.

He went when he had to.

He gave evidence about the false alibi, the brass stair rod and what Izzy had told him in the study.

He described the push on the first-floor landing without looking at her.

Once, while leaving court, he saw her through a glass door.

For half a second she was simply Izzy again.

Dark hair.

Hands inside the sleeves of a jumper.

Seventeen years old and frightened.

Then Jamie remembered Evelyn.

Both truths remained.

He never learned how to make one erase the other.

Perhaps he was not supposed to.

When the criminal case finally ended, Margaret Pryce asked Jamie and Gareth to come to her office.

Jamie assumed it was another piece of paperwork.

He was wrong.

---

Pryce & Watkins occupied the first floor above an estate agent in town.

Jamie had expected mahogany walls and people wearing wigs.

Instead there was grey carpet, a water cooler and a receptionist eating yoghurt behind a computer.

“Disappointing,” Jamie whispered.

Gareth looked at him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

Margaret met them in a small meeting room.

On the table sat a thick folder and one cream envelope.

Jamie recognised Evelyn's handwriting immediately.

His name was on the front.

**JAMIE MORGAN**

Not James.

Jamie stared at it.

Margaret noticed.

“She was very specific.”

“About what?”

“The name.”

Jamie laughed quietly.

“Good.”

They sat.

Margaret opened the folder.

“There are several things I need to explain, and some of them are complicated.”

Jamie looked at Gareth.

“Great.”

Margaret smiled faintly.

“You already know Evelyn revised her will shortly before she died.”

Jamie nodded.

“She had revised it before that too. Several times over the years. Catherine had originally been the principal beneficiary. After Catherine's death, Isabella became the principal beneficiary.”

Jamie's stomach tightened at the name.

Margaret continued carefully.

“In the most recent will, Evelyn made arrangements intended to protect both Isabella and the house. A substantial financial inheritance was to be held in trust for Isabella. Ashcroft House itself was subject to restrictions intended to prevent an immediate sale.”

“That’s what they argued about,” Jamie said.

“Yes.”

Gareth folded his arms.

“So what happens now?”

Margaret looked at Jamie.

“There is a legal principle which prevents a person from benefiting from unlawfully killing the person from whom they would inherit.”

Jamie nodded slowly.

“Izzy can't have it.”

“Correct.”

“What happens to it instead?”

Margaret placed one hand on the folder.

“Evelyn anticipated the possibility that a beneficiary might die before her, refuse an inheritance or otherwise become legally unable to receive it. There is a substitute beneficiary.”

Jamie looked at Gareth.

Gareth looked equally confused.

“Who?” Jamie asked.

Margaret turned the folder around.

A photocopied page faced him.

There were paragraphs of legal language Jamie barely understood.

Margaret pointed to one line.

He read it.

Then read it again.

**James — known as Jamie — Gareth Morgan.**

Jamie stared.

“No.”

Margaret said nothing.

“That’s me.”

“Yes.”

“No, I mean—”

Jamie laughed nervously.

“There has to be another one.”

“Another Jamie Gareth Morgan?”

“Could be.”

“Living three streets below Ashcroft House?”

Jamie looked at his father.

Gareth's mouth had fallen open slightly.

Jamie had never seen that before.

“Dad.”

“I've got nothing.”

Jamie looked back at Margaret.

“What does it mean?”

“It means, after the specific expenses and obligations of the estate are dealt with, you are the beneficiary of Evelyn's residuary estate.”

Jamie frowned.

“In English.”

Margaret allowed herself a smile.

“Ashcroft House passes for your benefit.”

Jamie stopped breathing.

“The house?”

“Yes.”

“And the land?”

“Yes.”

“The greenhouse?”

“Yes.”

“The conservatory?”

Margaret nodded.

“The contents, subject to a few specific gifts, also form part of the estate.”

Jamie stared at the table.

“What about the money?”

Margaret paused.

“A substantial portion of the remaining investments also passes under the same clause.”

Jamie looked up.

“How substantial?”

Gareth said, “Jamie.”

“What? I feel like this is a fair time to ask.”

Margaret gave him a figure.

Jamie sat back.

“Oh.”

Gareth said nothing.

Jamie looked at him.

“You all right?”

“No.”

“Same.”

Margaret closed the folder.

“There are important qualifications. You are still a minor. You do not leave here today with unrestricted control of the assets. The estate will be held and administered under the trust provisions Evelyn established until the relevant age. There are trustees, professional oversight and responsibilities connected with the property.”

“So I don't get a giant cheque?”

“No.”

“Rude.”

Gareth covered his face with one hand.

Margaret laughed.

“You do, however, have a beneficial interest in the house, and the trustees can fund necessary maintenance and restoration. Your father will be involved in decisions affecting you.”

Jamie stared at the cream envelope.

“Why?”

Margaret's expression softened.

“I think Evelyn would prefer to answer that herself.”

She slid the envelope towards him.

Jamie touched it but did not open it.

“Did she know?”

“Know what?”

“What Izzy would do.”

Margaret shook her head immediately.

“No.”

Jamie felt relief he could not explain.

“She was worried about Isabella's relationship with money. She was worried about the house. She was worried about what grief had done to both of them. But no, Jamie. She did not believe her granddaughter would kill her.”

Jamie nodded.

“Why put me in, then?”

“Because wills require contingencies.”

“That’s the lawyer answer.”

“Yes.”

“What’s the real one?”

Margaret looked at the envelope.

“Read the letter.”

---

Jamie opened it outside.

He and Gareth sat on a bench overlooking the river because Jamie could not read Evelyn's last words beneath office lighting.

The paper was cream, the handwriting neat and slightly slanted.

He began.

**Dear Jamie,**

**If Margaret has given you this letter, circumstances have clearly become inconvenient. I dislike writing for an audience I cannot question, so you will have to tolerate a monologue.**

Jamie laughed through the pressure already building in his throat.

Gareth looked towards the river and gave him privacy.

Jamie continued.

**You came to Ashcroft House because you broke a window. This was not, in my experience, the traditional beginning of a friendship.**

**You then complained about almost every task I gave you, misplaced tools with extraordinary consistency, made dreadful tea despite repeated instruction, and developed an inflated opinion of your carpentry after successfully repairing one door.**

Jamie wiped his eyes.

“Cheeky old cow.”

Gareth smiled without turning.

**You also stayed when you were no longer obliged to stay.**

Jamie stopped.

He read the sentence twice.

**That mattered.**

**You once asked why I had allowed the house to deteriorate when I could afford to repair it. I gave you several evasive answers before eventually telling you the truth. After Catherine died, I stopped caring whether Ashcroft House survived.**

**What I did not tell you was that houses are rather like people in one unfortunate respect: it is very difficult to save one when nobody particularly minds whether it remains standing.**

Jamie's vision blurred.

He wiped the page quickly, terrified a tear might damage the ink.

**You minded.**

**Then Isabella minded because you did. Eventually, to my considerable irritation, I began to mind again as well.**

Jamie closed his eyes.

He could hear Evelyn saying it.

Not sentimental.

Almost annoyed by the emotion of her own letter.

He read on.

**Do not interpret any inheritance from me as payment. You paid your debt long ago. Nor is it a reward for being good. You are fifteen. The evidence is insufficient.**

Jamie laughed again.

**If Ashcroft House becomes your responsibility, you may keep it, change it or one day sell it. I would prefer that you do not turn it into luxury flats, but death presumably limits my voting rights.**

**Do not preserve the house as a museum to me. Houses should be lived in. Make noise. Repair what matters. Leave some things crooked. Perfection, as Ruth Bowen correctly told you, would be dishonest.**

Jamie looked towards Gareth.

His father had started crying too.

Neither commented.

There was one final paragraph.

**And because I know you will otherwise continue asking long after I am capable of refusing: the bird in Catherine's window is a robin. When Catherine was eight, she accused me of spending more time observing strangers than looking at my own family. She pointed to a robin in the garden and told me that if I insisted on watching everything, I ought to watch something nice occasionally. Eleven years later she hid the robin in the window. It was her way of ensuring that I did.**

**She was irritatingly effective.**

Jamie laughed and cried at the same time.

The letter ended:

**Look carefully, Jamie. But remember to look at the things worth seeing.**

**Evelyn**

Below her name she had added one final line.

**P.S. Your work on the window frame was acceptable. Do not let this go to your head.**

Jamie folded the letter carefully.

For a long time, he and Gareth sat beside the river without speaking.

Then Gareth said, “So.”

Jamie looked at him.

“You own a mansion.”

“Apparently.”

“You know the gutters are fucked?”

Jamie laughed.

“Yeah.”

“Roof too.”

“Yep.”

“Conservatory still needs work.”

“I know.”

Gareth nodded.

“Better get started, then.”

---

The following spring, scaffolding covered the west side of Ashcroft House.

Not all of it.

Jamie refused to let contractors fix everything at once.

Some jobs required professionals.

Roofing.

Electrical work.

Structural repairs.

The things Gareth had taught him never to pretend he understood.

But other jobs were theirs.

On a bright Saturday morning, Jamie stood halfway up a ladder beside the front porch fitting new gutter brackets.

Gareth stood below holding the ladder.

The garden had been cut back enough to see the house from the lane again.

Fresh glass shone in the conservatory roof.

One upstairs room had been replastered.

The iron gates had been cleaned but not replaced.

Jamie insisted they stay crooked.

Character.

Inside, the library smelled of timber and dust.

Evelyn's letters remained locked in the cabinet until someone decided what should happen to them.

Her old walking stick stood beside the study desk, returned after the case finished.

Catherine's bedroom remained untouched for now.

Some repairs could wait.

Jamie tightened the bracket.

Gareth looked up.

“You've put that on backwards.”

Jamie stopped.

“No, I haven't.”

“You have.”

Jamie leaned sideways and looked.

The bracket was backwards.

He stared down at his father.

Gareth smiled.

“Wrong.”

Jamie started laughing.

“Fuck off.”

“Language.”

“You're not Evelyn.”

“No.”

Jamie looked through the open front door.

Sunlight passed through Catherine's stained-glass window and scattered colour across the entrance hall.

Blue flowers.

Amber leaves.

And hidden among them, a tiny red robin.

Something worth seeing.

Jamie turned the bracket around.

Then carried on fixing the house.
