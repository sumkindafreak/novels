# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Ten — Rain on Glass

By November, the conservatory had a floor.

This was not technically new.

The floor had existed since approximately 1884.

But it had been buried beneath enough dirt, broken pottery, dead plants and general neglect that uncovering it felt like archaeology.

Jamie stood in the middle of the black-and-white tiles with both hands on his hips.

“Look at that.”

Izzy was sitting on the old metal table, eating biscuits from a packet she had found in the kitchen.

“I am looking.”

“It’s good.”

“It’s a floor.”

Jamie turned.

“You sound like your gran.”

“Take that back.”

“No.”

Izzy threw a biscuit at him.

Jamie caught it.

“Thanks.”

“You weren't supposed to catch it.”

“Spy training.”

“You did two observation games with an eighty-one-year-old woman.”

“Elite training.”

He ate the biscuit.

The conservatory still had a long way to go.

The roof leaked.

The timber around the doors needed work.

Half the glass was opaque with grime.

But the worst of the rubbish had gone. The old raised beds were cleared. A narrow cast-iron shelf had been cleaned and painted. Jamie had repaired one of the wooden benches under Gareth's supervision.

Izzy had planted three cheap supermarket herbs in terracotta pots.

Two had died.

The mint seemed impossible to kill.

Evelyn had pretended not to notice any of them.

Then Jamie arrived one Saturday and discovered three bags of fresh compost stacked beside the doors.

Nobody mentioned it.

That was how things worked at Ashcroft House.

Rain began tapping against the roof.

Izzy looked up.

“Here we go.”

Jamie glanced towards the sky.

The morning had started bright, but a dark bank of cloud had rolled across the valley without warning.

Within seconds, the tapping became a roar.

Rain hammered the glass.

Water poured through one missing roof panel and splashed into the bucket Jamie had placed beneath it.

He moved another bucket under a leak.

Izzy laughed.

“Very professional.”

“Shut up and get that one.”

She grabbed a pot from the bench and slid it beneath a drip.

Another leak appeared.

Then another.

Jamie looked around.

“This thing has more holes than roof.”

“Character.”

“That is not character.”

“Gran says it is.”

“Your gran calls damp patina.”

“Same thing.”

Thunder rolled over the hills.

The lights inside the house flickered.

Izzy looked towards the doors.

“Power will go.”

“Does it always?”

“When it rains like this.”

“That seems safe.”

“Ashcroft House laughs at safety.”

As if on cue, the lights went out.

Jamie stared at her.

Izzy spread her hands.

“See?”

The conservatory did not become dark.

Grey daylight still filled it through the roof and windows, but the house beyond turned shadowy.

Rain blurred the valley into nothing.

For a while neither of them spoke.

Jamie listened to water running down the glass.

It felt strangely private in there.

The entire world reduced to one room, two people and rain.

Izzy slid down from the table and sat on the tiled floor with her back against it.

Jamie sat opposite her.

She passed him the biscuits.

“Your dad coming at one?”

“Supposed to.”

“Road'll be awful.”

“He'll still come.”

Izzy nodded.

Jamie looked at her.

“What?” she said.

“Nothing.”

“You’re doing Gran's silence thing again.”

“I’m not.”

“Yes, you are.”

Jamie smiled.

“Where’s your dad?”

The smile left her face.

He immediately regretted asking.

“Sorry.”

“No, it’s fine.”

Izzy pulled one knee up and wrapped her arms around it.

“Gone.”

“Gone where?”

“Wherever men like him go.”

Jamie waited.

Izzy looked towards the rain.

“He and Mum split when I was six. He sent cards for a few years. Then Mum got ill and suddenly everything was very difficult for him.”

Jamie frowned.

“He didn't come back?”

“He came to the funeral.”

“Oh.”

“Stayed two nights. Told Gran he'd ‘keep in touch’.”

She made quotation marks with her fingers.

“Still waiting.”

Jamie looked at the floor.

“That’s shit.”

“Yep.”

“My mum left too.”

Izzy looked at him.

“You never said.”

“You never asked.”

“Fair.”

Jamie picked at the edge of the biscuit packet.

“She lives in Bristol now. I see her sometimes.”

“How sometimes?”

“Christmas. Birthday. Maybe a weekend if she remembers before Thursday.”

Izzy watched him.

“You angry?”

Jamie shrugged.

That was his standard answer whenever anybody asked something too close to the centre.

Izzy did not let him have it.

“That’s not an answer.”

He smiled despite himself.

“You've been spending too much time with Evelyn.”

“So have you.”

Jamie looked towards the black doorway into the house.

“Dad stayed.”

Izzy nodded.

“Yeah.”

“So I don't know. Could be worse.”

“That still isn't an answer.”

Jamie thought about it.

“Sometimes.”

“Angry?”

“Yeah.”

Izzy nodded as though he had given the correct password.

“Me too.”

Thunder rolled again.

The rain intensified.

A leak near the wall became a steady stream.

Neither moved to catch it.

Izzy looked at him.

“You know Gran likes you.”

Jamie laughed.

“No, she doesn't.”

“She does.”

“She called me structurally incompetent this morning.”

“That is basically affection.”

Jamie smiled.

“She likes you too.”

Izzy looked away.

“Obviously. I'm her granddaughter.”

Jamie heard something in the answer.

He wasn't sure what.

Evelyn's voice came back to him.

The important thing isn't always what somebody does.

Sometimes it's what they suddenly stop doing.

Jamie looked at Izzy more carefully.

“You don't think she does.”

Izzy's head snapped back.

“What?”

“You don't think she likes you.”

“That’s stupid.”

“You went weird when I said it.”

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“Congratulations, MI5.”

Jamie laughed.

Izzy looked down at her hands.

“She loves Mum.”

Jamie waited.

“Loved,” Izzy corrected.

The rain filled the silence.

“Sometimes I think when she looks at me, she sees Mum. And then sometimes I think she remembers I'm not her.”

Jamie didn't know what to say.

So he said the first honest thing that came to him.

“She looks at you like you're you.”

Izzy looked up.

“You don't know that.”

“No.”

“Then why say it?”

“Because I've seen her look at Catherine's photo.”

Izzy stayed very still.

“And?”

“It’s different.”

She studied him.

Jamie suddenly became aware of how close they were sitting.

Not touching.

But close.

Closer than before.

Izzy smiled faintly.

“You notice too much now.”

“Elite training.”

“Idiot.”

“Probably.”

Another burst of thunder made the glass vibrate above them.

Izzy flinched.

Jamie laughed.

“You're scared of thunder?”

“No.”

“You jumped.”

“I reacted.”

“You absolutely jumped.”

“Shut up.”

Jamie was still smiling when Izzy leaned forward and kissed him.

It lasted perhaps one second.

Maybe two.

Long enough for every useful thought to leave Jamie's head.

Then Izzy pulled back.

She looked almost as surprised as he felt.

Jamie stared at her.

“Oh.”

Izzy started laughing.

“What?”

“That’s what you've got?”

“I wasn't ready.”

“You had weeks.”

“For thunder?”

“For me, you idiot.”

Jamie could feel his face burning.

Izzy smiled.

“Do you want me to apologise?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Jamie swallowed.

Then, because his heart was trying to break through his ribs and because there did not seem to be a sensible alternative, he leaned forward and kissed her back.

This time neither of them laughed immediately afterward.

When they finally pulled apart, Izzy rested her forehead against his for a moment.

The lights came back on.

Both of them jumped.

From the kitchen, Evelyn shouted, “If either of you has been touching the fuse box, stop.”

Izzy covered her mouth.

Jamie whispered, “She knows everything.”

Izzy started laughing again.

Outside, the rain kept falling.
