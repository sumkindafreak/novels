# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Seven — Ashcroft Saturdays

By the middle of October, Saturday mornings had become something Jamie no longer complained about.

At least not sincerely.

He still made a performance of it for his father.

Every Saturday at nine fifteen he would come downstairs in old jeans and a hoodie, drag himself into the kitchen and announce that his human rights were being violated.

Gareth would hand him toast.

Jamie would ask whether the European Court of Human Rights accepted cases involving skirting boards.

Gareth would tell him to put his boots on.

Then, invariably, Jamie would be ready first.

The work changed from week to week.

Ashcroft House seemed capable of producing faults faster than he could repair them.

He stripped flaking paint from a window frame.

He learned how to fill cracks without leaving half the filler on the floor.

He replaced a rotten section of skirting in the pantry.

He cleaned moss from stone steps with a stiff brush until his hands ached.

Under Gareth’s supervision, he rehung another door.

Under Evelyn's supervision, he rehung it again.

“Wrong,” she said.

“It shuts.”

“It drags.”

“It barely drags.”

“Then it is barely wrong.”

Jamie took it off again.

The strange thing was that he was getting better.

Not dramatically.

He still measured things incorrectly. He still stripped screw heads by being impatient. He still had a gift for putting tools down in places where they immediately became invisible.

But the mistakes started changing.

At first he made mistakes because he didn't know what he was doing.

Later, he made mistakes because he thought he knew what he was doing.

Gareth told him this was progress.

Jamie suspected it was another adult trick.

---

The house changed too.

Only slightly.

Nobody passing on the lane would have noticed.

The gutters still sagged.

The west wing still leaked.

The conservatory still looked like a greenhouse after a small war.

But inside, things began working again.

Doors closed.

Windows opened.

The morning room stopped smelling of old paint.

One section of the conservatory floor emerged from beneath twenty years of dirt, revealing black-and-white tiles arranged in a faded geometric pattern.

Jamie found himself showing things to Gareth when he came to collect him.

“Look at this.”

“Nice.”

“And that frame was rotten there, so I cut it back and—”

“Good.”

“And that bit of plaster was loose.”

“Jamie.”

“What?”

“I can see it.”

“Oh.”

Gareth smiled.

“Looks good.”

Jamie tried not to look pleased.

---

Izzy became part of Saturdays too.

Sometimes she helped.

Mostly she claimed to help.

She was surprisingly good at painting and unbelievably bad at cleaning brushes.

She had no patience for instructions.

Jamie had no patience for her lack of patience.

This resulted in arguments which Evelyn referred to as “background noise.”

One afternoon they painted the old pantry shelves.

Izzy flicked paint at Jamie.

Jamie retaliated.

Ten minutes later a pale green handprint appeared on the back of Izzy's black T-shirt.

Jamie denied responsibility despite standing beside her with a paint-covered hand.

Izzy chased him through the kitchen.

Evelyn did not look up from her newspaper.

“If either of you touches the hall wallpaper,” she said, “I will bury you beneath the greenhouse and finally make the local rumours accurate.”

Jamie stopped running.

Izzy stared at her grandmother.

Evelyn turned a page.

Jamie whispered, “She knows about that?”

Izzy whispered back, “She knows everything.”

---

Sometimes they did no work at all.

Rain would hammer the windows so hard that outside jobs became impossible, and Evelyn would decide there was no point pretending otherwise.

On those mornings they ended up in the kitchen.

Jamie discovered Evelyn liked crossword puzzles, hated daytime television and had opinions about almost every prime minister of the past sixty years.

Most of those opinions were not complimentary.

She also knew things.

Not school things exactly.

Useful things.

She could identify trees by their bark.

She could explain why old houses had servants' bells in particular places.

She knew how to fold a map so the section you needed stayed visible.

She could tell when somebody was lying with an accuracy that Jamie found deeply inconvenient.

One wet Saturday, he told her he had finished his English homework.

Evelyn looked at him over the top of her glasses.

“No, you haven't.”

Jamie stared.

“How do you know?”

“You looked towards your bag before answering.”

“That means nothing.”

“It means your homework is in your bag.”

“That still doesn't mean I haven't done it.”

“Show me.”

Jamie did not move.

Evelyn returned to the crossword.

“Thought so.”

Izzy nearly fell off her chair laughing.

Jamie glared at both of them.

“Fine.”

He pulled his exercise book out.

The assignment was an essay on whether Shakespeare's Macbeth was responsible for his own downfall.

Evelyn read the title.

“Good question.”

“It’s not.”

“It is.”

“He kills people because witches tell him things.”

Evelyn raised an eyebrow.

“An interpretation unlikely to earn you the highest grade.”

“See? This is why school is stupid.”

She closed the crossword.

“What do you think?”

Jamie sighed.

“I just told you.”

“No. You told me what happened. What do you think?”

He frowned.

Evelyn waited.

Jamie hated waiting adults. They forced words out of him.

“I think he wanted to do it anyway.”

“Why?”

“Because if somebody told me I was going to be king, I wouldn't immediately start murdering everyone.”

Izzy looked up from her phone.

“Speak for yourself.”

Jamie continued.

“The witches just give him permission, sort of. He hears what he wants.”

Evelyn nodded.

“Write that.”

“That?”

“Yes.”

“In an essay?”

“Preferably with punctuation.”

Jamie looked at the blank page.

Then began writing.

Twenty minutes later he had almost a page.

Evelyn never dictated a sentence.

She only asked questions.

Why?

What makes you think that?

Where does he choose?

Where does he pretend he had no choice?

By the end, Jamie had written more than he usually managed in an entire lesson.

He looked at it suspiciously.

“That’s annoying.”

“What is?”

“You made it easy.”

“No. You knew the answer.”

“I didn't.”

“You did. You merely hadn't arranged it yet.”

Jamie reread what he had written.

He liked that.

He did not tell her.

---

Later that afternoon, while Gareth was delayed at work, Jamie stayed longer than usual.

He and Izzy sat on the wide stone steps outside the conservatory, sharing a packet of crisps.

The valley below was turning gold in the low autumn sun.

Smoke or mist hung between the houses.

Izzy rested her elbows on her knees.

“You like her.”

Jamie ate another crisp.

“Who?”

“Gran.”

“She’s all right.”

“That means yes.”

“She’s terrifying.”

“That also means yes.”

Jamie looked towards the kitchen window.

Evelyn was visible inside, moving slowly between the table and kettle.

“She’s different to what people say.”

“People say she’s a witch.”

“You say that too.”

“I’m allowed.”

Jamie smiled.

“Do you like living here?”

Izzy looked at the house.

There was a long pause.

“Sometimes.”

“What about the rest?”

She shrugged.

“It’s complicated.”

Jamie waited.

Izzy glanced at him.

“Stop doing that.”

“What?”

“That thing Gran does.”

“What thing?”

“Waiting until people talk.”

Jamie laughed.

“I didn't do anything.”

“Exactly.”

She nudged him with her shoulder.

“Annoying.”

He nudged her back.

Their shoulders stayed touching a little longer than necessary.

Neither moved.

Jamie became suddenly, painfully aware of it.

Of her arm against his.

Of the smell of paint and shampoo.

Of the fact that he had no idea where to look.

Izzy seemed completely relaxed.

Jamie concentrated very hard on the valley.

After a while she said, “You know you don't have to keep coming forever.”

Jamie looked at her.

“What?”

“Gran hasn't told you?”

“Told me what?”

Izzy smiled faintly.

“I think you've nearly paid for the window.”

For reasons Jamie didn't immediately understand, the thought bothered him.

He looked back at the house.

At the window frames he had stripped.

At the conservatory they had started clearing.

At the kitchen where Evelyn was now tapping the window to tell them Gareth had arrived.

Jamie stood.

“Good,” he said.

Izzy watched him.

“Yeah?”

“Obviously.”

He picked up his bag.

“Freedom.”

But on the way home, Jamie found himself wondering exactly how many Saturdays freedom was supposed to take.
