# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Twelve — The Library

The library was Jamie's favourite room in Ashcroft House.

He would not have admitted this at school.

At school, liking a library too much seemed like the sort of thing that followed you around until sixth form.

But Ashcroft's library was different.

It occupied nearly the entire back corner of the ground floor and had windows on two sides. Shelves rose from floor to ceiling. A rolling ladder ran along one wall on a brass rail. There were leather chairs with cracked arms, a green-shaded reading lamp and a fireplace deep enough to sit inside if you were sufficiently stupid.

Jamie had considered it.

Evelyn had threatened him.

The books were not arranged in any system Jamie understood.

History beside gardening.

Poetry beside engineering manuals.

Russian novels beside biographies of prime ministers.

One shelf contained nothing except atlases from different decades.

Another had old government reports that looked deliberately boring.

“Did you read all these?” Jamie asked.

Evelyn was sitting at the large desk beneath the window, going through a stack of letters.

“No.”

“Most?”

“No.”

“Half?”

She looked up.

“Do you imagine owning books is a contractual promise to finish them?”

“Kind of.”

“Then never buy books.”

Jamie ran a finger along the spines.

He had volunteered to repair the lower section of the west window where old water damage had softened the timber.

Gareth had already checked that the structure around it was sound.

Jamie's job was to remove the damaged section, treat what could be saved and fit a replacement piece.

Evelyn had told him to take his time.

Jamie had discovered that this phrase now annoyed him less than it used to.

He knelt beside the window and measured again.

Then once more.

“Three times?” Evelyn asked without looking up.

“You trained me.”

“I corrupted you.”

Jamie smiled.

He marked the timber.

An enormous old ledger sat on the corner of Evelyn's desk, half buried beneath correspondence.

Jamie moved it aside to make room for the offcut he was using as a guide.

The thing was heavier than it looked.

Its cover was black leather, cracked pale along the edges, with a tarnished brass clasp and faded gold lettering pressed into the front.

**HARROW & SONS**

Jamie turned it slightly towards the light.

“This yours?”

Evelyn glanced across the desk.

“Good Lord, no.”

“What is it?”

“Something that should probably have been thrown away a very long time ago.”

Jamie shrugged and put it back exactly where he had found it.

A folder slipped from the desk behind him and hit the floor.

Several pages spread across the rug.

“I’ve got it.”

Jamie stood before Evelyn could bend.

He gathered the papers.

Most meant nothing to him.

Bank statements.

A letter from a solicitor.

Something headed ASHCROFT HOLDINGS LTD.

He placed them back into the folder.

Then stopped.

One sheet contained a number with too many zeros.

Jamie looked again.

He knew he shouldn't.

Looking twice transformed accidental seeing into nosiness.

He looked anyway.

“Is that yours?”

Evelyn held out her hand.

“Folder.”

Jamie gave it to her.

“How much money have you got?”

“None of your business.”

“That number had six zeros.”

“So do many telephone numbers.”

“It had a pound sign.”

Evelyn closed the folder.

Jamie looked around the room.

The wallpaper was peeling near the ceiling.

One curtain was faded almost white where the sun reached it.

The fireplace tiles were cracked.

He pointed towards the bucket in the far corner.

“That has been catching water for two months.”

“Yes.”

“You're rich.”

Evelyn's expression tightened.

“I am comfortable.”

“You're millionaire comfortable.”

“That is an exceptionally vulgar phrase.”

“It’s also true.”

Izzy entered carrying two mugs.

“What’s true?”

Jamie pointed at Evelyn.

“She's loaded.”

Izzy looked at her grandmother.

“Oh. He found out.”

Jamie stared.

“You knew?”

“Obviously.”

“Why does nobody mention this?”

Izzy handed him a mug.

“Because Gran thinks discussing money is vulgar.”

“It is vulgar.”

Jamie looked around again.

“You have holes in the roof.”

“They are not the same subject.”

“They absolutely are.”

Evelyn removed her glasses.

“Sit down.”

Jamie hesitated.

“Am I in trouble?”

“Not yet.”

He sat in one of the leather chairs.

Izzy took the other.

Evelyn remained behind the desk.

“My father owned land, industrial property and several businesses,” she said. “Most of which he sold before I was born. He invested well. I inherited part of that estate. I also inherited this house.”

Jamie listened.

“I worked for most of my adult life. I invested my own income. Some property increased considerably in value. None of this is particularly mysterious.”

“How much?”

Evelyn stared at him.

“Sorry.”

Izzy grinned.

Jamie looked at her.

“So you're rich too.”

Evelyn answered before Izzy could.

“No.”

Izzy's smile disappeared.

Jamie looked between them.

Evelyn continued.

“Isabella is seventeen.”

“I know.”

“She owns very little beyond her clothes, her laptop and several appalling pairs of shoes.”

Izzy crossed one boot over the other.

“These cost more than your entire outfit.”

Jamie looked at them.

“Why?”

Izzy laughed.

Evelyn did not.

Jamie noticed.

He looked back at the old woman.

“So when you die—”

Izzy kicked his ankle.

“What?”

“You can't just say that.”

Evelyn folded her hands.

“I am eighty-one, not immortal.”

Jamie felt embarrassed anyway.

“I mean the house. Does Izzy get it?”

Evelyn's eyes moved briefly to her granddaughter.

The pause was tiny.

Jamie noticed it because Evelyn had taught him to notice pauses.

“My estate is arranged privately.”

Izzy looked away.

Jamie recognised the wall going up and stopped asking.

He picked up his tea.

It was, predictably, violent.

“I still don't understand,” he said.

“What?”

“You could repair all of this tomorrow.”

Evelyn looked at the shelves.

“Yes.”

“You could hire twenty people.”

“Yes.”

“Then why didn't you?”

Izzy remained very still.

Jamie knew they had come back to the same place as the conservatory.

Catherine.

Loss.

The years when Evelyn had allowed the house to decay around her.

But this time Evelyn answered differently.

“Because I stopped caring whether it survived.”

The sentence landed quietly.

Jamie looked at her.

Evelyn's eyes stayed on the window.

“After Catherine died, rooms became obligations. Repairs became obligations. The house became an enormous list of things requiring attention from somebody who no longer had much attention to give.”

Izzy stared into her tea.

Jamie thought about his own bedroom after his mother left.

For months he had refused to take down a calendar she had bought him, even after the year changed.

Not because he liked it.

Because changing anything felt like admitting something was over.

He understood more than he wanted to.

“So why now?” he asked.

Evelyn looked at him.

Jamie gestured around.

“You're buying timber. You got compost. Peter and Ruth are doing the window. You got someone to quote the conservatory roof.”

Izzy turned sharply.

“You got a quote?”

Evelyn ignored her.

Jamie smiled.

“Why now?”

For once Evelyn did not have a quick answer.

She looked towards the new piece of timber leaning beside Jamie's tools.

Then at the section of window he had carefully stripped that morning.

Then back at him.

“Perhaps neglect becomes tiresome eventually.”

Jamie grinned.

“Bullshit.”

Izzy inhaled sharply.

Evelyn's eyebrow rose.

“Language.”

“Sorry.”

He smiled anyway.

“You're fixing it because we started.”

Evelyn regarded him for a long moment.

“An inflated sense of importance is unattractive, Jamie.”

“That’s not a no.”

Izzy started laughing.

Evelyn put her glasses back on.

“Finish the window.”

Jamie stood.

“Yes, boss.”

He returned to the damaged frame.

Behind him, Izzy said, “Gran?”

“What?”

“Did you really get a quote for the conservatory?”

A pause.

“Two.”

Jamie smiled at the timber.

The house had started caring too.
