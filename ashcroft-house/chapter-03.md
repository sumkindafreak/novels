# Ashcroft House

## Chapter Three — The Girl Upstairs

By eleven thirty, Jamie had discovered three things.

The first was that removing old paint from skirting boards was considerably harder than adults made it look.

The second was that Mrs Ashcroft had an almost supernatural ability to appear whenever he stopped working.

The third was that Izzy was not helping.

“Missed a bit,” she said.

Jamie sat back on his heels.

“I have removed approximately nine hundred years of paint from this wall.”

“Still missed a bit.”

She pointed.

He looked.

There was, irritatingly, a bit.

“You could help.”

“I could.”

“Are you going to?”

“No.”

Jamie scraped at it.

Izzy had been in and out of the room all morning, sometimes sitting on the windowsill, sometimes disappearing for twenty minutes and then returning with food she never offered to share.

She seemed completely at home in the enormous, decaying building in a way Jamie couldn't imagine anybody being.

Every strange noise meant something to her.

A knock in the pipes.

“Bathroom tap.”

A deep thump from somewhere above.

“Bedroom door. Wind catches it.”

A scratching sound behind the wall.

“Mice.”

Jamie had stopped scraping.

“Mice?”

“Probably.”

“Probably?”

“Could be Gran.”

Jamie looked at her.

Izzy kept a straight face for three full seconds before laughing.

At twelve, Mrs Ashcroft appeared in the doorway.

“Lunch.”

Jamie dropped the scraper immediately.

Mrs Ashcroft looked at the section he'd completed.

“Hm.”

Jamie waited.

“Hm good or hm bad?”

“Hm unfinished.”

“It’s lunch.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

“So you may stop being unfinished for half an hour.”

Jamie stood and stretched his back.

“Thank God.”

Mrs Ashcroft glanced towards the ceiling.

“I suspect He has more pressing concerns.”

Izzy was already leaving.

“Come on, vandal.”

Jamie followed her.

“Do you actually know my name?”

“James?”

“That’s her thing.”

“Artillery division?”

“Worse.”

“Window boy?”

“I hate you.”

“Jamie, then.”

They crossed the hall, but instead of going towards the kitchen Izzy opened a narrow door Jamie had assumed was a cupboard.

Behind it was a staircase.

Not the grand staircase in the entrance hall.

This one was steep, dark and barely wide enough for one person.

Jamie stopped.

“Where does that go?”

“Up.”

“Helpful.”

“It’s a staircase.”

She started climbing.

Jamie followed.

The walls were plain here. No paintings. No wallpaper. Just old cream paint and a wooden handrail worn smooth by generations of hands.

“Servants used this,” Izzy said over her shoulder. “Kitchen to the bedrooms without walking through the main hall.”

“You had servants?”

“I didn't. The house did.”

“At night?”

“No, Jamie. In 1890.”

He shoved her lightly between the shoulder blades.

She laughed and hurried upward.

The staircase opened onto the second floor at the end of a long corridor.

Jamie had only seen the house from downstairs.

Up here it felt larger.

Doors lined both sides. Some were closed. Others stood open into bedrooms full of covered furniture.

Sunlight came through tall windows overlooking the valley.

From here Jamie could see the terraces stacked across the opposite hillside, rows of grey roofs interrupted by trees, chimneys and the white line of a road climbing towards the mountain.

“You can see my house from here,” he said.

Izzy came beside him.

“Which one?”

Jamie pointed.

“Down there. Red door. Three streets below the chapel.”

She leaned closer to the glass.

“I can’t see a red door.”

“Because you’re blind.”

“I’m not blind.”

“You live in a house with eighty windows. Spoilt.”

Izzy looked around the corridor.

“Most of them leak.”

Jamie laughed.

She didn't.

For a moment something in her expression changed.

Not sadness exactly.

Tiredness.

Then it was gone.

“Come on.”

She led him along the corridor.

“Where are we going?”

“Tour.”

“Does your gran know?”

“No.”

“Good start.”

They passed a bedroom with faded blue wallpaper and a four-poster bed hidden beneath a white sheet.

Another room contained nothing except cardboard boxes stacked almost to the ceiling.

At the far end, Izzy opened a door onto a narrower corridor.

“This is the west side.”

The air changed immediately.

Colder.

Damp.

A strip of wallpaper hung loose from the ceiling.

Plastic buckets sat beneath dark stains.

Jamie looked up.

“Roof?”

“Roof.”

“You know you can pay people to fix those.”

“Tell Gran.”

“I did, sort of.”

“What did she say?”

“Nothing.”

Izzy gave a small smile.

“That sounds like Gran.”

They continued until they reached a door with a brass handle.

Izzy opened it.

A huge room lay beyond.

It had once been beautiful.

Jamie could tell that immediately.

Tall windows filled one wall. Ornate plasterwork ran around the ceiling. A marble fireplace stood at the far end beneath a cracked mirror.

But rain had got in.

The wallpaper had blistered. Floorboards near one window had turned almost black. A section of ceiling in the corner had collapsed, leaving lath exposed like broken ribs.

Jamie stepped inside.

“What was this?”

“Mum’s room.”

He looked at Izzy.

She had stopped in the doorway.

Jamie suddenly remembered that Mrs Ashcroft's daughter was dead.

“Your mum?”

Izzy nodded.

“Catherine.”

“I’m sorry.”

She shrugged too quickly.

“It was ages ago.”

Jamie knew enough to recognise a sentence that meant the opposite of what it said.

“How old were you?”

“Twelve.”

“Oh.”

Izzy looked around the room.

“Cancer. Fast one. Everyone always asks eventually.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Yes, you were.”

“I might not have.”

“You definitely were.”

Jamie didn't argue.

A photograph stood on the mantelpiece beneath the cracked mirror.

It was dusty, but the glass was intact.

A woman in her thirties smiled at the camera, dark hair falling over one shoulder. Beside her stood a much younger Izzy with missing front teeth and both arms wrapped around the woman's waist.

Jamie recognised the face immediately.

“You look like her.”

Izzy stared at the picture.

“Everyone says that.”

“Sorry.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

That made her laugh quietly.

Jamie stepped closer to the photograph.

There was another person in the background.

Mrs Ashcroft, younger but unmistakable, standing beside a table with her arms folded.

She wasn't looking at the camera.

She was looking at Catherine.

And smiling.

Jamie had never seen that expression on her face.

Not yet.

“Gran kept the room like this?” he asked.

“Mostly.”

“But the roof—”

“She stopped coming up here after Mum died.”

Jamie looked at the water stain spreading across the ceiling.

For the first time, Ashcroft House seemed less like a neglected building and more like something deliberately abandoned.

A place somebody had stopped trying to save.

Footsteps sounded in the corridor.

Izzy's eyes widened.

“Oh, shit.”

Mrs Ashcroft appeared in the doorway.

She looked first at Izzy.

Then at Jamie.

Then at the photograph on the mantelpiece.

Nobody spoke.

Jamie expected fury.

Instead Evelyn's face went completely still.

“I thought lunch had been declared,” she said.

Izzy straightened.

“I was showing him around.”

“So I see.”

“Gran—”

“Kitchen.”

Izzy rolled her eyes but obeyed.

Jamie started after her.

“Jamie.”

He stopped.

Mrs Ashcroft waited until Izzy had gone.

Then she entered the room.

Her walking stick tapped softly against the floorboards.

Jamie looked at the photograph.

“I didn't know.”

“No.”

“Izzy showed me.”

“I gathered.”

“Sorry.”

Mrs Ashcroft stood beside him.

For several seconds they looked at Catherine together.

“She designed the window,” Evelyn said.

Jamie turned.

“The stained-glass one?”

“Yes.”

The room suddenly seemed colder.

Jamie looked towards the corridor, as though he might somehow see through two walls and down into the entrance hall.

“The one I broke.”

“The one you broke.”

He stared at the photograph again.

Catherine was laughing at something outside the frame.

Jamie felt sick.

On Tuesday the window had been old glass.

Expensive glass.

Something he had damaged.

Now it belonged to somebody.

“I really am sorry,” he said.

Evelyn looked at him.

This time she did not answer with sarcasm.

“I know,” she said.

And somehow that felt worse.
