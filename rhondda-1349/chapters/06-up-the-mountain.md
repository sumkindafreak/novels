# Chapter Six — Up the Mountain

**3–7 June 1349**

Gwenllian complained before they had climbed a quarter of the way.

Ieuan had never been so grateful for anything in his life.

"My foot hurts."

"Which one?"

"This one."

She lifted the wrong foot, nearly fell and grabbed his sleeve.

"Both, then."

"You said one."

"I changed my mind."

"Feet don't work like that."

"Mine do."

Ieuan tightened the strap across his shoulder and kept walking.

The bundle had become heavier since leaving Hendre Gelli even though nothing had been added to it. The climb did that. So did knowing there was no adult ahead waiting to take some of the load.

The familiar track rose across the slope instead of attacking it straight. Ieuan knew every turn. He knew where the ground held water beneath grass, where a boot could slide on stone, where cattle had broken the edge of the path during the previous summer.

Gwenllian knew it too.

Not as well.

She still looked behind them too often.

Ieuan did not.

Once was enough.

He allowed himself one glance when the track reached a shoulder of ground above the holding.

Hendre Gelli looked very small.

A dark roof.

The yard.

The patch of ground where Tad had stood telling him to stop fighting the brindled cow.

The door was invisible from there.

So was Mam.

Ieuan faced uphill again.

"Come on."

"I need to stop."

"We stopped five minutes ago."

"That wasn't stopping. You told me to drink."

"That is stopping."

"No. That's drinking."

He turned.

Gwenllian stood with both hands on her knees, breathing hard.

Angharad's voice came back to him.

*She'll say she isn't tired until she falls. Make her stop before that.*

This time she was saying she was tired.

Ieuan immediately felt guilty for not listening.

"Fine. Sit."

Gwenllian dropped onto a stone.

"How long?"

"Until I say."

"How long is that?"

"Longer if you keep talking."

She went silent.

For perhaps ten seconds.

"Will Mam know where we are?"

Ieuan stared across the valley.

"Aye."

"How?"

"Because she told us to come here."

"But if she wakes up—"

"She knows."

Gwenllian picked at the wooden animal in her lap.

"You said someone would help her."

"They will."

"Who?"

Ieuan swallowed.

"Meurig. Someone."

"Meurig didn't come yesterday."

"He was busy."

"With dead people?"

"Aye."

Gwenllian looked down.

Ieuan closed his eyes.

"Come on," he said more gently. "We're nearly there."

They were not.

But the lie was smaller than the others.

---

The upland shelter was worse than Ieuan remembered.

That was Gwenllian's first opinion.

"The roof's broken."

"It leaked last year."

"There's mud inside."

"It's a cattle shelter."

"It smells."

"Cattle."

"I hate it."

Ieuan put the bundle down.

"Good. Then you can walk back."

Gwenllian stared at him.

He regretted it immediately.

"I'm joking."

"No you aren't."

"Mostly."

She folded her arms.

Ieuan began working.

That was easier.

The shelter sat tucked against the slope where generations of hands had used the ground itself as protection. The roof was rough and patched, with one side always giving trouble when rain came from the north. A stone-lined place for fire needed clearing of old ash and damp debris. The little enclosure outside had partly fallen where animals pushed it in winter.

Ieuan knew all of that.

He knew where the nearest reliable water ran over stone above the muddy pool below.

He knew which wood stayed driest beneath the overhang.

He knew the corner where smoke collected if the wind blew down-slope.

For the first time in nearly two weeks, knowledge was enough.

He cleared the hearth.

Made fire.

Collected water.

Checked the roof.

Moved their bedding to the drier side.

Found an old length of cord still usable.

By the time he had done those things, Gwenllian had stopped complaining.

She sat near the fire with the wooden animal and watched him.

"What?" Ieuan asked.

"Nothing."

"You're looking."

"You look like Tad."

Ieuan's hands stopped.

"No I don't."

"When you're telling things what to do."

"Things don't listen to me."

"Neither did they listen to him."

Ieuan almost laughed.

Instead he bent back to the fire.

"Drink."

"I just did."

"Again."

"I don't want—"

"Mam said you don't drink enough when it tastes smoky."

Gwenllian's face changed.

Ieuan wished he had said nothing.

She took the cup anyway.

---

That evening was the first time he believed they might actually manage.

Not forever.

He was not stupid enough to imagine forever.

A few days.

Long enough for the sickness below to pass.

Long enough for somebody to go to Hendre Gelli.

Long enough for Meurig to find them.

Long enough for Mam—

He stopped the thought there.

Gwenllian ate beside the fire and complained that the food was worse than Mam's.

"Mam burned things too," Ieuan said.

"Not this badly."

"You ate the burnt spoon."

"I didn't eat the spoon."

"You nearly did."

"That was your fault."

"How?"

"You shouted."

"I wasn't even there."

"You shout a lot."

Ieuan looked at her.

"You're becoming Madog."

Gwenllian was quiet.

He cursed himself again.

Every ordinary joke now contained a dead person if you stepped on it badly.

He changed the subject.

"Tomorrow we'll look for the cattle higher up."

"All of them?"

"Whatever's there."

"Can I help?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Whether you listen."

"I listen."

Ieuan snorted.

"I do."

"You hear. That's not the same."

"Tad said that about you."

"No he didn't."

"He did."

"When?"

"When you weren't listening."

This time Ieuan laughed.

Gwenllian did too.

The sound filled the poor shelter far more completely than the fire did.

For one night, there were two children on a mountain and neither one was dying.

Ieuan slept.

---

He woke wet.

The old awareness came first.

Warmth gone cold.

Cloth against skin.

The sour smell beginning.

His eyes opened into grey morning light.

Gwenllian slept beside the other wall.

Ieuan moved quickly.

He rolled the blanket inward.

Gathered straw.

Started toward the entrance.

"I know."

He froze.

Gwenllian's eyes were open.

Ieuan's face burned.

"Shut up."

She sat up.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"No."

He stared at her.

No grin.

No goat's-arse face.

No threat to tell Elen.

Gwenllian stood and picked up the dry edge of one cloth.

"This bit's all right."

Ieuan grabbed it from her.

"I can do it."

She stepped back.

He carried the wet straw out.

When he returned, she was putting wood near the hearth.

Ieuan sat on his heels.

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"I did. I said I know."

"You know what I mean."

Gwenllian shrugged.

"It's not funny today."

That hurt more than teasing would have.

Ieuan looked away.

"It was never funny."

"Sometimes it was."

He gave her a look.

She almost smiled.

Almost.

Then she said, "I'm hungry."

Ieuan stood.

"Good."

"Why is that good?"

"Because hungry people are annoying."

"You're always hungry."

"Exactly."

The morning became almost ordinary.

That was their good day.

---

They found two cattle farther along the upland grazing.

One belonged to Hendre Gelli.

The other Ieuan thought was Madog's, though he could not be certain from distance until he approached.

The animals were nervous but healthy enough.

Ieuan did not attempt to bring them immediately to the shelter.

He watched.

Gwenllian stood beside him.

"Why aren't you moving them?"

"Watching."

"You've been watching for ages."

"That's because you keep talking."

"Tad would have moved them by now."

"No he wouldn't."

The words came naturally.

Then both fell silent.

Ieuan studied the cattle.

One favoured the higher track.

The other wanted to stay close.

"We'll leave them for now," he decided.

"Why?"

"There's enough grazing. We don't need another thing to manage today."

Gwenllian nodded as if he had said something wise.

It made him uneasy.

They gathered what fuel they could carry and returned.

Gwenllian told a ridiculous story about Elen trying to catch a chicken and falling into a trough the previous summer.

Ieuan corrected half of it because he had been there and Gwenllian's version increasingly involved a pig that had never existed.

They argued all the way back.

In the afternoon Gwenllian slept.

Ieuan was glad.

He thought the climb had finally caught up with her.

At dusk she woke and said she was cold.

The fire was hot.

Ieuan stopped breathing.

"What?"

"Cold."

"You're near the door. Move."

She moved closer to the hearth.

Five minutes later she was still shivering.

Ieuan put the back of his hand against her forehead.

Warm.

Too warm.

"You walked too much," he said.

"You made me."

"Aye. That's all."

"My legs hurt."

"Mine too."

"My head hurts."

"Smoke."

Gwenllian looked at him.

Ieuan heard his own voice.

Too fast.

Too many reasons.

A bad lie.

He lifted her arm.

"What are you doing?"

"Nothing."

"You're looking for—"

"Shut up."

Nothing beneath one arm.

His hope surged so violently he almost laughed.

Then he checked the other.

Gwenllian cried out.

Ieuan's fingers had found it.

Small.

Not small enough.

A hard, painful swelling beneath the arm.

He withdrew his hand.

Gwenllian's face twisted.

"You hurt me."

Ieuan stared.

"Sorry."

"Why did you do that?"

He could not answer.

"Ieuan?"

He stood.

Water.

Cloth.

Fire.

Prayer.

The next thing.

Then the next.

Then the next.

"Lie down," he said.

Gwenllian's eyes followed him.

"Is it the same?"

"No."

"You're lying."

"Lie down."

"Is it the same as Tad?"

"No."

"Mam?"

Ieuan turned on her.

"I said no!"

She flinched.

He immediately crouched beside her.

"Sorry. Sorry. It's just a swelling. It could be from walking or carrying or anything."

Gwenllian looked at him.

They both knew.

Ieuan took her hand.

"I'm going to fix it."

That was the worst lie he had ever told.

---

On Saturday, Ieuan became everyone.

He became Mam with a wet cloth.

Tad keeping the fire alive.

Meurig whispering the prayers he could remember.

The old woman with bitter herbs, though he had none of what she used and substituted things Angharad had once given for fever.

He became himself only when none of those worked.

Gwenllian drifted in and out of sleep.

The swelling grew.

Her fever rose.

She complained of pain, then stopped complaining so much, which frightened him more.

At midday Ieuan thought of Penrhys.

Ffynnon Fair.

Mary's well.

People went there for healing.

The thought struck him so hard he stood immediately.

He could carry her.

He had carried sacks heavier than Gwenllian.

Not over that ground.

Not quickly.

Not while fever made every movement hurt.

But perhaps—

He looked at her.

The route in his mind became real.

Down from the shelter.

Across exposed ground.

Tracks where other people might be sick.

Climbing again.

Hours.

Maybe nobody at the shrine.

Maybe everybody there.

Maybe the sickness there too.

Gwenllian opened her eyes.

"Why are you standing?"

Ieuan sat again.

"No reason."

"You're doing the lying face."

"I don't have a lying face."

"You do. Your mouth goes funny."

"My mouth is normal."

"It's ugly."

Ieuan laughed despite himself.

"You look worse."

"I know."

The answer took the breath from him.

Gwenllian stared at the roof.

"Is Mam coming?"

Ieuan's throat closed.

"I don't know."

It was the first honest answer he had given her about Angharad.

Gwenllian nodded.

"I'm thirsty."

Ieuan helped her drink.

Some ran down her chin.

He wiped it with his sleeve.

"Don't tell Mam," she whispered.

"What?"

"I spilled it."

Ieuan looked at her.

Then down at the cup.

"I won't."

"She'll say I waste everything."

"Aye."

"She always says that."

"Aye."

Gwenllian closed her eyes.

Ieuan held the cup for a long time after she stopped drinking.

---

Sunday morning arrived quietly.

There was no bell on the mountain.

No sound from church carried that far through the damp air.

Ieuan knew it was Sunday because he had counted days since leaving.

He tried to pray properly.

He could not remember the order of words.

"God," he said instead.

Then stopped.

What did you say after that?

Please?

He had said please.

For Tad.

For Mam.

For himself.

Perhaps God was tired of hearing him.

"Please don't take her."

Gwenllian's breathing rasped softly beside him.

Ieuan put both hands together.

"You took everyone else."

The words came before fear of blasphemy.

"You don't need her."

He waited.

Nothing.

"I do."

Gwenllian moved.

Ieuan leaned over her.

"Gwen?"

Her eyes opened halfway.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Then stop saying my name."

He laughed once.

"All right."

"You're loud."

"Sorry."

"I'm tired."

"Sleep."

She did.

By afternoon, Ieuan realised she had stopped waking when he touched her shoulder.

He shook harder.

"Gwenllian."

Nothing.

"Gwen."

He pressed his fingers to her face.

Warm.

Still warm.

"Wake up."

He lifted her.

Her head fell against him.

"Wake up."

He put water to her lips.

It ran down her cheek.

"Don't. Come on."

He shook her.

Harder.

"Stop it."

As if she were choosing.

"Stop it!"

No answer.

Ieuan became very still.

His whole body locked around her.

He sat with Gwenllian in his arms and stared at the opposite wall.

He did not know how long.

At some point the fire went low.

He did not feed it.

At some point rain began.

He heard it tapping on the leaking side of the roof.

He did not move the bedding.

At some point he noticed the cup still beside her.

He moved it nearer in case she woke thirsty.

Then understood what he had done.

He threw the cup across the shelter.

It struck the wall and cracked.

Ieuan stared at it.

"Good," he whispered.

His voice sounded wrong.

"Good."

He lowered Gwenllian onto the bedding.

Pulled the blanket over her.

Then pulled it back because she hated being covered over her face.

That thought broke something.

Ieuan pressed both fists against his eyes.

He did not scream.

The sound that came out was smaller.

Worse.

When darkness began to fill the shelter, practical thought returned because practical thought always returned.

A body could not remain there forever.

Tad had been carried.

Tad had been prayed over.

Tad had been buried with people around him.

Mam had at least been left in their house where someone might find her.

Gwenllian had only him.

Ieuan sat beside his little sister until he could no longer see her face properly.

There was nobody to tell him where to bury her.
