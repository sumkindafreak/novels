# Chapter Three — The First Bell

**15–22 May 1349**

By the middle of May, the valley had changed colour.

Not much. Not enough that Ieuan could have pointed to a morning and said *there, that was when spring properly arrived*. The brown had simply withdrawn from the lower slopes day by day. Grass thickened around the wetter ground. Hazel leaves opened. The cattle no longer had to nose so hard for anything worth eating, and the light stayed longer in the evenings.

The stories stayed too.

That was the part Ieuan noticed least.

People had been saying the sickness was coming for more than a month. Coming from the east. Coming from the south. Coming from Cardiff. Coming from Bristol. Coming through ports Ieuan had never seen and could not have found if someone had put a map in front of him.

Everything was always coming.

Rain came.

Rent came due.

Calves came badly and had to be pulled.

Winter came even when summer felt endless.

If a thing had not yet reached Hendre Gelli, it was difficult to fear it every hour of the day.

Especially when the red cow had started refusing the lower pasture.

"She's favouring the left," Ieuan said.

Dafydd squinted at the animal.

"Is she?"

"Aye."

"Since when?"

"Yesterday."

"You didn't say yesterday."

"I wasn't certain."

Dafydd looked at him.

Ieuan felt the familiar irritation rising.

"You always tell me not to say a thing until I've watched it."

"I tell you to watch before you move it. I don't tell you to become a monk and keep vows of silence."

"So I should have said?"

"You should have said you thought she was favouring the left."

Ieuan folded his arms.

"That's the same thing."

"No. One is watching. The other is pretending you've become God and know everything."

Ieuan looked away.

Dafydd crouched beside the cow and ran a hand down the leg.

"Hoof," he said.

"I knew that."

"Of course you did."

From the yard, Gwenllian shouted, "Mam says stop arguing with Tad and bring the pail."

Ieuan shouted back, "Tell Mam I heard her."

"She says if you heard her, why haven't you moved?"

He closed his eyes.

Dafydd smiled.

"Your mother has a gift."

"For what?"

"Knowing exactly where you are without looking."

Ieuan fetched the pail.

It had been nearly two weeks since Easter.

He had wet himself twice.

The first time had made him furious.

The second had made him feel stupid for believing three dry nights meant anything.

He had stopped praying about it for several days out of spite, then felt guilty and started again without admitting to himself that he had.

Life had returned to normal so thoroughly that the traveller's face already seemed like something from a story.

Until Ieuan noticed there was no smoke from Madog's holding.

He was carrying the pail back when it caught his eye.

Madog's roof sat lower across the slope, partly hidden by a rise. There should have been smoke at that hour. Nest kept a fire as constantly as Angharad did, and Madog complained about it because he claimed she would burn all Glamorgan if left unsupervised.

There was nothing.

Ieuan stopped.

"What?" Dafydd asked.

Ieuan pointed.

His father's expression changed.

Not much.

Just enough.

"Stay here."

"Why?"

"Because I told you."

"I can come."

"No."

Dafydd wiped his hands and started down the track.

Ieuan watched him go.

Gwenllian came beside him.

"Where's Tad going?"

"Madog's."

"Why?"

"Don't know."

"There's no smoke."

Ieuan looked at her.

"I saw."

"Maybe they're gone."

"Gone where?"

She shrugged.

"Somewhere."

Children, Ieuan thought, could say a word like *somewhere* as if it were a real place.

---

Dafydd came back before midday.

He washed his hands outside.

That was the first thing Ieuan noticed.

Not because washing was strange. Because of the way he did it.

He poured water slowly and scrubbed longer than necessary, then poured more.

Angharad stood in the doorway.

"Madog?"

Dafydd shook his head once.

"Bad?"

"Aye."

"The sickness?"

He hesitated.

"Maybe."

Ieuan felt something move through his belly.

Gwenllian was behind Angharad, quiet for once.

"What does he have?" Ieuan asked.

Dafydd glanced at him.

"Fever. Pain."

"Where?"

"Does it matter?"

"The man at church said you'd know."

Angharad said, "Enough."

"But—"

"Enough."

Dafydd dried his hands on his tunic.

"Nest needs water carried. Their cow wants moving. I'll go back after we've eaten."

Ieuan stared.

"You said I couldn't go."

"You aren't."

"Why?"

"Because there is no reason for four people to stand in a sick man's house when two can do the jobs outside."

"I'm not frightened."

Dafydd looked at him for so long that Ieuan wished he had not said it.

"Good," his father said. "Then you won't mind staying here."

That ended it.

For an hour.

Then Angharad told Ieuan to carry a sack of meal to Nest because she had run low.

"Tad said—"

"Your tad said you weren't to stand in Madog's house. He did not say you had become unable to carry food."

Ieuan lifted the sack.

"Should I leave it outside?"

Angharad stopped what she was doing.

That was the first time Ieuan saw fear plainly in her face.

It vanished quickly.

"Aye," she said. "Call for Nest. Don't go in unless she asks you."

"Why?"

"Ieuan."

"Everyone keeps telling me not to ask things."

"Because sometimes the answer is that nobody knows."

He did not like that answer.

He carried the sack anyway.

Madog's holding felt wrong before he reached it.

The yard was untidy in ways Madog never allowed. A bucket lay on its side. One of the hens scratched near the doorway where Nest usually chased them away. The cow lowed from behind a rough enclosure, impatient and full-uddered.

Ieuan stopped well short of the door.

"Nest?"

Nothing.

He called louder.

"Nest!"

A moment later she appeared.

Ieuan almost did not recognise her.

Her hair was tied badly and there was a dark patch of sweat at the neck of her dress. She looked furious.

"What?"

"Mam sent meal."

Nest stared at the sack as if it had personally insulted her.

Then her face changed.

"Put it there."

He lowered it beside the wall.

From inside the house came a sound.

Not a shout.

Not a word.

A low groan dragged out through clenched teeth.

Ieuan looked at the doorway.

Nest stepped sideways, blocking more of it.

"Don't."

He froze.

"Is it the swelling?"

Her eyes narrowed.

"Who said?"

"The traveller. He said—"

"Travellers say everything."

Another groan came from inside.

Nest's anger broke.

For just a second.

"Under his arm," she said.

Ieuan's own armpits seemed suddenly too warm.

He pressed his elbows against his sides.

Nest saw.

"Don't start that."

"What?"

"Feeling yourself every five breaths. I've done nothing else since yesterday and I'm still standing."

Ieuan dropped his arms.

"Can I help?"

"You can milk the cow before she kicks the fence apart."

That he knew how to do.

The relief shamed him.

He went to the cow.

---

Meurig came later.

Ieuan saw him from the enclosure.

The priest walked quickly, carrying what he needed for a sick man and nothing else. Dafydd arrived not long after. They spoke outside before Meurig entered.

Ieuan could not hear everything.

He did not need to.

The tone was enough.

The same man who had joked about mud in the church stood now with his head bent close to Dafydd's, both of them looking at the door as if there were something behind it they did not want released.

Elen sat on a low stone near the wall.

She had not cried.

That made her look younger.

Ieuan carried the milk to her mother.

Elen said, "He keeps asking for water."

"Then give him water."

"He drinks it and asks again."

Ieuan had no answer.

"Is Gruffudd here?" she asked.

"No."

"He said he might come."

"He shouldn't."

Elen looked at him sharply.

"Why?"

"Because—"

He stopped.

Because he was frightened.

Because he did not want Gruffudd in the house.

Because he did not want himself in the house.

Because perhaps sickness travelled like smoke and every person who went through that doorway carried some of it out again.

He knew none of those things.

"No reason," he said.

Elen looked away.

"You're a bad liar."

"I'm not lying."

"See?"

Even then, even with Madog groaning inside, she almost smiled.

Ieuan almost smiled too.

Then Madog cried out.

Everything stopped.

---

The next three days were filled with bells and absences.

Rogation prayers came during a week when fewer people seemed willing to stand near one another.

Ieuan had always understood prayer for land and crops in the simple way he understood weather: people asked God for what they needed because there was nowhere else to ask. This year the petitions seemed too small.

Good ground.

Protection from storms.

A fruitful year.

What about a house where a man could not bear the touch of cloth beneath his arm?

What about whatever had travelled from town to town until it reached Madog?

Ieuan found himself checking his body.

At first only once.

Then twice.

Hand beneath one arm while nobody watched.

Fingers at his neck.

A quick press at the groin when he went outside to relieve himself.

Nothing.

Every time, relief lasted less long.

At home, Angharad caught him doing it beside the door.

"Stop."

Ieuan pulled his hand away.

"I had an itch."

"Under both arms?"

"Aye."

She looked at him.

"You are your father's son."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means you lie badly and then become offended when people notice."

Dafydd, repairing a wooden handle near the hearth, said, "Leave me out of this."

Angharad looked at him.

"You once told me a ewe had opened the gate herself."

"She might have."

"With what?"

"Determination."

Gwenllian laughed.

For a few moments Madog was not in the house across the slope.

Then the bell rang.

Once.

A pause.

Again.

Angharad stopped smiling.

Dafydd put the handle down.

Nobody needed to say whose death it was.

---

Madog ap Rhys died on Ascension Day.

Ieuan did not see him die.

He saw what came after.

The body was wrapped.

People came, but not enough.

Nest's face looked made from something hard and pale. Elen cried only once that Ieuan saw, and that was when someone tried to take Madog's old cap from a peg because it was still in the way.

"Leave it," she said.

The person left it.

Meurig's prayers were the same prayers.

That was almost worse.

Ieuan had heard death prayers before. Old people died. Infants died. Men fell badly. Women did not survive childbirth. There was nothing new in a grave.

But Madog had been laughing at Dafydd about rain only weeks ago.

Madog had stood by the upper gate with two teeth missing and said Nest blamed everything on judgement.

Madog had always been there.

Now people spoke around the space where he was not.

The burial was not disorderly.

It was hurried.

Those were different things.

Ieuan understood that without having words for it.

People did what had to be done and did less of everything around it. Fewer stopped afterward. Fewer touched Nest. Someone left food by the wall instead of bringing it inside.

Meurig looked exhausted.

When the grave was filled, Ieuan realised the priest was staring at his own hands.

Not praying.

Just staring.

Dafydd put a hand briefly on Madog's eldest surviving kin's shoulder.

Then he wiped that hand on his tunic.

Ieuan saw.

Dafydd saw him see.

Neither spoke.

On the walk home, Gwenllian asked, "Does Madog know he's dead?"

Ieuan snapped, "How would I know?"

"I asked Mam."

Angharad took a breath.

"His soul knows more now than we do."

"Where is it?"

"With God, if God has taken him."

"But his body is there."

"His body isn't all of him."

Gwenllian thought about that.

"If I died, would I still know Ieuan?"

"Don't say that," Ieuan said.

She looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because you don't."

"Don't what?"

"Die."

Dafydd said, "Everyone dies."

Ieuan turned on him.

"That's helpful."

His father looked surprised.

Ieuan immediately wished he could swallow the words.

Angharad said nothing.

Dafydd walked another few paces.

"Your mam's right," he said. "Whatever God keeps of us is more than the bit we bury."

Gwenllian seemed satisfied.

Ieuan was not.

---

The following morning, Madog's holding had smoke again.

Very little.

But enough that Ieuan saw it and felt relieved.

Nest and Elen were still there.

The world had not ended.

He told himself that twice.

Then, while he and Dafydd were repairing a hurdle below the yard, his father stopped working.

Only for a moment.

Dafydd shifted his shoulder.

His face tightened.

Ieuan noticed because he had spent all week noticing bodies.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Dafydd reached across himself and rubbed beneath his left arm.

Ieuan stared at the movement.

"What?" Dafydd said again.

"Why are you doing that?"

"Doing what?"

"Your arm."

Dafydd dropped his hand.

"Pulled something."

Ieuan's mouth had gone dry.

"Let me see."

His father actually laughed.

"No."

"Tad."

"Ieuan."

"Madog had—"

"I know what Madog had."

"Then let me see."

"You will do no such thing."

Dafydd picked up the hurdle again.

Ieuan did not move.

"It's only sore," Dafydd said.

"From what?"

"Work. Remember work? The thing you are currently not doing?"

Ieuan kept staring.

His father lifted the hurdle.

For perhaps half a heartbeat, the weight made his face change again.

Pain.

Real pain.

Then it was gone.

"Take the other end," Dafydd said.

Ieuan obeyed.

He watched his father's left arm for the rest of the day.
