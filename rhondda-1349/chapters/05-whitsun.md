# Chapter Five — Whitsun

**29 May–2 June 1349**

Angharad said she was tired until the morning she could no longer stand long enough to make the lie convincing.

On Friday she rose before Ieuan, as always.

He heard her at the hearth. Heard the scrape of the pot. Heard her tell Gwenllian to move her feet before she stepped on them.

For perhaps ten minutes, he believed Thursday evening had meant nothing.

Then he came through the hanging cloth and saw his mother gripping the edge of the table with both hands.

Her face was pale except for two bright patches high on her cheeks.

"Mam."

"Water."

"Sit down."

"Water first."

"I'll get it. Sit."

Angharad turned on him with enough irritation that hope flared.

"I know how to sit, Ieuan. Fetch the water."

He obeyed.

When he returned, she was sitting.

Gwenllian stood beside her.

"She says she's cold."

"I heard."

"But the fire's hot."

"I know."

"So why—"

"I know."

Gwenllian flinched.

Ieuan immediately hated himself.

He put the vessel down.

"Sorry."

His sister did not answer.

Angharad drank.

Ieuan watched her throat move.

He knew the pattern now.

That was the worst of it.

When Madog became ill, adults had understood before him.

With Dafydd, Angharad had understood.

Now there was nobody to explain anything.

He touched his mother's forehead.

Hot.

"It's not the same," he said.

Angharad looked at him.

"Ieuan—"

"Tad's was under his arm. You could be tired. You didn't sleep. You haven't slept for days."

"Aye."

"So that's what it is."

"Maybe."

"Not maybe."

He heard himself speaking faster.

"You've been by the fire. You're hot because of that. And you were outside after the burial and it was cold and—"

Angharad put a hand over his.

He stopped.

"Do the morning jobs," she said.

"Mam."

"The morning jobs."

"I can stay."

"And watch me?"

He stared at her.

"Will watching me water the cattle?"

Dafydd would have said almost exactly the same thing.

That made Ieuan angry.

"Everyone keeps talking like work matters more than people."

Angharad's eyes sharpened.

"Work is how people stay alive."

He looked away.

"Go."

Ieuan went.

---

By midday, everything was wrong at once.

The fire had gone low because he forgot fuel.

Gwenllian complained she was hungry because he had not given her enough.

One of the cattle had worked loose because he tied a fastening badly in his hurry.

Angharad needed water.

The pot caught at the bottom.

He tried to scrape it clean and broke the wooden spoon.

"Not that one," Angharad called weakly from the bedding.

Ieuan stared at the pieces.

"What?"

"The spoon. That was the good one."

Something in him snapped.

"Then you should've told me before I broke it!"

Silence.

Gwenllian froze near the wall.

Ieuan breathed hard.

Angharad said, "I didn't know you were going to break it."

He looked down at the spoon.

Then laughed once.

A horrible sound.

"No."

Angharad almost smiled.

Ieuan sat on the floor.

"I can't do all this."

His mother looked at him.

"No."

He waited for the rest.

It did not come.

"What do you mean, no?"

"I mean you can't."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

"The next thing."

"Which thing?"

"What needs doing most."

"Everything needs doing most."

"No." She closed her eyes for a moment. "The animals have water?"

"Aye."

"Then they can wait. Gwenllian's eaten?"

Ieuan glanced at his sister.

"Some."

"Feed her. Then the fire. Then me."

"Why are you last?"

Angharad opened one eye.

"Because I'm lying down. I can wait longer."

"That's stupid."

"Probably. Do it anyway."

Ieuan fed Gwenllian.

Then the fire.

Then brought Angharad water.

For nearly an hour the house did not collapse.

He began to understand that this was what adults did.

Not everything.

The next thing.

Then the next.

Then the next.

Until there was another morning.

---

On Saturday the swelling appeared.

Ieuan saw Angharad's hand at her neck.

He knew before she moved it away.

"Show me."

"No."

"Mam."

"You've seen enough swellings."

"Show me."

She looked at him.

Perhaps it was the way he said it.

Perhaps she no longer had strength to argue.

She pulled the cloth slightly aside.

The lump sat near the side of her neck, painful and hard beneath the skin.

Ieuan looked away first.

Gwenllian was outside.

Thank God.

"No," he said.

Angharad covered it.

"Ieuan."

"No."

"Listen."

"No."

"Listen to me."

"Tad died four days ago."

The words came out louder than he meant.

"That's enough."

Angharad's face broke.

Not completely.

Just enough that he saw his mother instead of the woman who always knew what to do.

"I know," she whispered.

Ieuan turned away.

He wanted to hit something.

There was nothing worth hitting.

The wall would hurt his hand.

The pot had done nothing.

God was too far away.

He went outside and split wood until his palms blistered.

---

Whitsunday came with a bell Ieuan could no longer understand.

It rang in the distance while he carried water.

Once, that bell told him where the community was.

Mass.

Prayer.

A death.

A feast.

A warning.

Now it only meant somebody somewhere was still strong enough to pull a rope.

Gwenllian heard it too.

"Are we going?"

Ieuan looked at her.

"Where?"

"Church."

He almost laughed.

"Mam can't."

"We could go."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I'm staying here."

"I could go with Elen."

The name hurt.

They had not seen Elen since Madog's burial.

Nest's holding had gone quiet again. Whether they had fled, fallen sick or simply refused visitors, Ieuan did not know.

"No."

Gwenllian kicked at the dirt.

"You say no to everything now."

"Because you ask stupid things."

"Mam would let me."

"Mam can't even stand."

The moment the words were out, he wished he could cut his tongue off.

Gwenllian's face emptied.

She went inside.

Ieuan stayed by the water vessel.

He stared at his hands.

Dafydd's knife hung at his belt.

He had started thinking of it as his.

That felt like another betrayal.

---

Angharad's fever worsened through Sunday.

Meurig did not come.

Ieuan sent word through a man passing lower on the track.

The man returned hours later with no priest.

"He's at two houses below," he said. "Maybe three. They don't know where he'll be by dark."

"Tell him my mother needs him."

"They all do."

Ieuan stepped closer.

"Tell him."

The man lifted both hands.

"I will."

Ieuan watched him leave.

Gwenllian stood in the doorway.

"Will Mam die?"

"No."

"You said Tad wouldn't."

Ieuan felt the world tilt.

"I didn't say that."

"You did."

"I said he might get better."

"You said it wasn't the sickness."

"At first."

"So is Mam going to die?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

Ieuan's mouth kept moving because stopping would mean answering.

"Because hers is different and she's stronger than Tad was when it started and she's been drinking more water and it's in her neck, not under her arm, and the woman said different swellings can mean different things and—"

Gwenllian stared at him.

"You're lying."

He stopped.

His sister's eyes filled.

"You're bad at it."

Ieuan could think of nothing cruel enough to say back.

So he held her.

She resisted for half a second.

Then clung to him.

Inside, Angharad coughed.

The bell rang again.

---

Monday was the last day Angharad told him what to do properly.

The fever eased for a few hours.

Not gone.

Only far enough away that her eyes became clear and her words stopped drifting.

Ieuan almost mistook it for recovery.

Then she said, "There are two sacks behind the board near the back wall."

His hope died immediately.

"I know."

"No, you don't. One is meal. One is peas. Don't take both if you have to climb. They're too heavy."

"We're not climbing."

"Listen."

"No."

"Ieuan."

"You're getting better."

Angharad closed her eyes.

"Don't make me waste the good part of the day arguing with you."

He sat beside her.

Gwenllian slept curled against the wall.

"Where would we go?"

"The cattle shelter. Higher toward Gelli."

"I know where it is."

"Then you know the roof leaks on the north side. Sleep away from it."

"Mam."

"Water's below the stones. Not the first pool if it's been raining. It fouls. Go farther up."

"Stop."

"Take one pot. Not the large one. Knife. Fire things. One blanket each if you can manage it."

Ieuan's eyes burned.

"You're coming."

Angharad looked at him.

He had never seen her look so tired.

"If I can."

"You are."

"If I can."

"No."

"And Gwenllian—"

"I know."

"No, listen. She's eight. She'll say she isn't tired until she falls. Make her stop before that. She'll drink too little if the water tastes smoky. Make her drink. She'll want to carry things to prove she can. Don't let her carry anything you need back."

Ieuan wiped his face angrily.

"You make her sound like a goat."

Angharad smiled.

"Goats are quieter."

A small laugh escaped him.

It became a sob before he could stop it.

He put both hands over his face.

Angharad touched the back of his wrist.

"Ieuan."

He shook his head.

"Look at me."

He did.

"You don't have to know everything."

"Tad did."

"Your father knew about six things and pretended the rest."

Ieuan laughed wetly.

"He'll hear you."

Angharad's expression changed.

Only for a second.

Then she said, "Then perhaps he'll finally learn something."

Ieuan held her hand.

He did not let go until she fell asleep.

---

Angharad died on Tuesday.

The morning had barely begun.

Ieuan knew because her breathing had changed during the night and then changed again near dawn.

He sat beside her.

Gwenllian slept.

For a while Angharad seemed to be looking toward the roof.

Then she looked at Ieuan.

He leaned closer.

"Mam?"

Her lips moved.

He could not hear.

"What?"

He put his ear near her mouth.

The word might have been his name.

It might have been water.

It might have been nothing.

He would spend years deciding it had been his name and then deciding he had invented that because he needed it.

Her hand moved once against the blanket.

Then stopped.

Ieuan sat still.

His body became very quiet.

That was what happened when he was frightened.

Dafydd had been buried five days earlier.

Ieuan knew what came next.

That knowledge made everything worse.

A body had to be wrapped.

Someone had to carry it.

Someone had to dig.

Someone had to find Meurig.

Someone had to stay with Gwenllian.

Someone had to keep the animals watered.

Someone had to decide whether the house was safe.

There was only him.

Gwenllian woke.

She looked at Angharad.

Then Ieuan.

"No."

He could not answer.

She crawled toward their mother.

Ieuan caught her.

"No!"

Gwenllian fought him with surprising strength.

"Let go! Mam!"

"Don't."

"Let me go!"

"Gwen—"

She kicked him.

He held on.

"She's dead!"

Silence.

The words struck harder than he intended.

Gwenllian collapsed against him.

Ieuan held his sister while she cried into his shoulder.

He stared at the wall over her head.

He did not cry.

There was no room.

---

By midday, Ieuan had made the decision.

He hated himself for it.

Angharad could not be buried by him alone while Gwenllian remained in the house.

Not properly.

Not quickly.

Not without leaving an eight-year-old alone beside a dead body while he searched for help that might never come.

He covered his mother carefully.

He said the prayers he remembered.

He told himself someone would come.

Meurig.

A neighbour.

Anyone.

The lie was necessary.

He packed.

Badly.

At first he tried to take everything Angharad had named and several things she had not.

Two pots.

Too much grain.

The heavy blanket.

A wooden bowl Gwenllian insisted belonged to her.

Cord.

Extra clothing.

A small stool for reasons he could not explain even to himself.

He got the bundle onto his back and nearly fell.

Gwenllian watched.

"That's too much."

"I know."

"Why did you take the stool?"

Ieuan stared at it.

"I don't know."

He began removing things.

The stool first.

One pot.

Some grain.

A tool too heavy for the value it gave.

Then his hand closed around a small scrap of cloth Angharad had used to tie her hair.

He put it back.

Then picked it up again.

It weighed nothing.

He tucked it inside his tunic.

Gwenllian had found a little carved wooden thing Dafydd once made badly from a scrap — not quite a horse, not quite a dog.

"I'm taking this."

"No."

Her face closed.

Ieuan looked at the object.

Useless.

Completely useless.

"Fine."

She gripped it.

Dafydd's knife stayed at Ieuan's belt.

He never considered leaving it.

Near evening, they sat beside Angharad one last time.

Gwenllian held their mother's hand.

Ieuan could not.

He had too many things left to do if they were to leave at first light.

That was what he told himself.

---

Before dawn on Wednesday, Hendre Gelli was darker than Ieuan remembered.

He tightened the bundle.

Checked the fire.

Then realised there was no reason to bank it for later.

He stood looking at the hearth.

Every instinct said leave it ready.

Mam would want embers.

Tad would complain if they came back to a dead fire.

They were not coming back that evening.

Ieuan scattered the safest of the burning pieces and made certain nothing could catch.

Gwenllian stood by the door.

She looked tiny beneath the blanket rolled across her shoulders.

"Ready?" he asked.

"No."

He nodded.

"Me neither."

They stepped outside.

The valley was grey with early light.

Ieuan closed the door.

Gwenllian looked back at it.

"When is Mam coming?"

His chest tightened so hard he could barely breathe.

He had known she would ask eventually.

He had not prepared an answer.

"Later."

Gwenllian stared at him.

"When?"

Ieuan adjusted the bundle though it did not need adjusting.

"When someone helps her."

"Then she'll come up?"

"Aye."

"Today?"

"Maybe."

Gwenllian's eyes stayed on his face.

Ieuan added too much.

"Meurig will find someone and they'll take care of her first and then if she's strong enough—"

He stopped.

Gwenllian looked away.

"You're lying again."

Ieuan could not deny it.

They started up the mountain.
