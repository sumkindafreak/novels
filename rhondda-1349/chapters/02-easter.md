# Chapter Two — Easter

**5–12 April 1349**

On Palm Sunday, Ieuan woke dry.

He lay still for a moment anyway.

Habit made him check before hope did.

One hand beneath the blanket. Straw cool and rough under his fingers. Wool dry against his legs.

Nothing.

Ieuan smiled into the dark.

It was a small smile, because even alone there were things a boy of fourteen ought not look too pleased about.

He dressed before anyone else properly woke and carried his bedding outside as though he had some reason to shake it. The dawn was pale over the ridge. Rain had stopped in the night and water still ran from every hollow in the ground, silver where the first light found it.

He checked the blanket again.

Dry.

Perhaps it had stopped.

He did not say that aloud.

Saying a thing aloud was a good way to make God notice you had become proud of it.

By the time they left for Ystradyfodwg, Gwenllian had already complained about her shoes, the mud, the distance and the fact that Angharad would not let her carry a bundle she insisted was no heavier than a hen.

“It is heavier than a hen,” Ieuan said.

“You don't know.”

“I know what a hen weighs.”

“You don't know what this weighs.”

“I can see it.”

“You can't see weight.”

Ieuan looked at Dafydd.

“Can I leave her here?”

“No,” Dafydd said.

“Can I push her in the river?”

“After Easter.”

Gwenllian kicked mud at Ieuan's ankle.

Angharad caught her by the shoulder before the second kick.

“Do that again and you'll walk beside me all the way.”

Gwenllian immediately dropped behind to Ieuan's side.

“I hate walking beside Mam.”

“I heard that,” Angharad said.

“I know.”

The path toward the church was busier than usual.

Not crowded. Glynrhondda did not crowd easily. People came from scattered holdings and slopes, appearing one or two at a time along tracks Ieuan knew and others he rarely used. A family from farther up the valley walked ahead with two small children and an old woman who stopped every few dozen paces to complain about the ground. Madog ap Rhys and Nest were already near the church when Ieuan's family arrived. Elen stood with them.

And Gruffudd ap Ieuan.

Ieuan saw him before Gruffudd saw Ieuan and immediately wished he had not.

Gruffudd was fifteen and had lately discovered that this made him wiser, stronger and more important than everybody who was fourteen. He had grown quickly over winter and now seemed to think his new shoulders were a personal achievement.

Ieuan looked at Gwenllian.

“If you say anything—”

“I won't.”

“Anything at all.”

“I know.”

“About anything.”

She frowned.

“I have to speak.”

“You know what I mean.”

Gwenllian's expression changed.

For once there was no joke in it.

“I won't tell.”

Ieuan studied her face, looking for the grin.

There wasn't one.

“Good.”

Then she added, “Unless you push me in the river.”

He should have known.

Gruffudd noticed them.

“Thought you weren't coming.”

Ieuan shrugged.

“Why wouldn't we?”

Gruffudd shrugged back, because apparently neither of them knew how ordinary speech worked anymore.

“Heard your red cow got loose.”

“She didn't.”

“Madog said she did.”

“He said she pushed a gate.”

“So she got loose.”

“No.”

“If she's outside the gate—”

“She wasn't outside it.”

Gruffudd smiled slowly.

Ieuan hated that smile.

Elen, standing beside her mother, said, “Are you two going to spend Holy Week arguing about a cow?”

Gruffudd turned toward her.

“I wasn't arguing.”

“You were losing, then.”

Ieuan laughed before he could stop himself.

Gruffudd's smile vanished.

That improved Palm Sunday considerably.

Inside the church, the familiar world closed around them: stone, timber, smoke, damp wool, bodies pressed closer together than they would have been at home, the priest's voice moving through words Ieuan had heard all his life without always understanding every Latin syllable.

Meurig ap Cynan looked tired.

Not ill. Just tired in the ordinary way of a man who had too many people asking him to remember too many things.

Before the service he caught Dafydd near the door and said, “Your wife's sister sent word from Llantrisant.”

Angharad leaned across.

“My sister?”

“Not bad news,” Meurig said quickly. “Only that the boy's hand is healing.”

Angharad nodded.

“Tell her I'll send something when someone goes down.”

“If I remember.”

“You'd better.”

Meurig gave her a flat look.

“You are welcome to take the parish yourself.”

“I'd have it better ordered by Tuesday.”

He almost smiled.

That was how Ieuan knew him. Not as the man who stood between God and the parish, though of course he did. Meurig was also the man who forgot names when three people spoke at once, who complained that mud entered the church no matter how often he asked people to wipe their feet, and who had once chased a hen out through the door in the middle of Mass while everybody pretended not to laugh.

Afterward, people remained longer than usual.

There was always talk after church. Animals. Weather. Who needed help. Who owed work. Who had seen somebody on a road. Who had not paid for something. Who was marrying whom and whether that was wise.

This time the sickness entered the talk again.

Ieuan heard it first from Nest.

“A woman down toward Llantrisant says they've buried six from one household.”

Madog shook his head.

“A woman says.”

“Her cousin saw them.”

“Her cousin says.”

Nest folded her arms.

“You didn't believe your own mother when she told you your boots were wet.”

“They were on my feet. I had evidence.”

Elen looked at Ieuan.

“They've been talking about it for three days.”

“Madog likes not believing things,” Ieuan said.

“I believe plenty.” Madog had heard him. “I believe you're standing there listening when you should be helping your father.”

Ieuan turned.

Dafydd was carrying something toward the path.

He hurried after him.

Behind, Gruffudd called, “Careful your cow doesn't escape while you're gone.”

Ieuan did not turn around.

He wanted to.

That counted as restraint.

Holy Week did not make the cattle holy.

On Monday morning the brindled cow put her foot into a patch of soft ground and had to be coaxed out. Tuesday brought rain sideways across the slope and Dafydd discovered that a section of roof needed attention where water had worked its way beneath the thatch. On Wednesday Angharad sent Ieuan for water twice because he had somehow forgotten the first instruction while completing the second one.

“You're useless this week,” she told him.

“I've done everything.”

“You have done several things. That is not the same.”

On Thursday he woke dry again.

He checked three times.

He said nothing.

By then he had begun thinking about it too much.

At night, lying in the little end, he tried not to sleep on his back because he had once decided that sleeping on his back might be the cause. Then he remembered he had wet himself on his side plenty of times and turned over again. He stopped drinking before bed until Angharad noticed and made him drink because, as she said, being thirsty was not a cure for anything except having too much water.

He prayed.

Not loudly.

Not even properly, perhaps.

He had prayed before, of course. For forgiveness. For people. For weather. For animals. For the souls of the dead. For things the priest told everybody to pray for.

But this prayer was his.

Please let it have stopped.

That was all.

Good Friday came cold and grey.

The church felt different that day. Quieter. Emptier even with people inside it. The solemnity sat heavily over the familiar place, and Ieuan tried to keep his mind where it belonged.

It kept wandering.

He thought about the little end.

Then about Gruffudd.

Then about whether the dry nights meant anything.

Then, ashamed of thinking about himself on such a day, he tried to concentrate harder.

Afterward a man arrived along the lower path while people were still dispersing.

He was not from the immediate valley. Ieuan knew that before anyone said it. The man's clothes were wet nearly to the knee and his boots carried a different colour of road mud. A pack sat crooked over one shoulder. He stopped near the churchyard and asked for water.

Meurig gave him some.

The man drank too quickly, coughed, drank again and wiped his mouth with his sleeve.

“Coming from Llantrisant?” somebody asked.

“Past it.”

“Cardiff?”

The man shook his head.

“Not there.”

He looked around at the people watching him and seemed irritated by the number of faces.

“What?”

Nest said, “You've heard of the sickness?”

The man's expression changed.

Not fear exactly.

Tiredness with fear underneath it.

“Heard?” he said.

Nobody answered.

“I've seen it.”

Madog moved slightly closer.

“Where?”

The man named a place Ieuan knew only as somewhere beyond the roads he travelled.

“How many?” Dafydd asked.

The traveller stared at him.

“Enough.”

“That's not a number.”

“No.”

His voice hardened.

“Because there isn't always someone left to count.”

The small conversations around them stopped.

Ieuan felt a prickling beneath his arms.

Without meaning to, he pressed one hand against his side.

The traveller noticed.

“You'll know if you've got it,” he said.

Angharad pulled Ieuan's hand away.

“Don't.”

He lowered it.

The traveller continued.

“Two in one house. Four in another. A priest dead. Men refusing to carry bodies unless they're paid first. People leaving servants where they fall.”

Meurig's face tightened at that.

“Men say all sorts in fear.”

“I watched a woman put her husband outside herself because no one would enter the house.”

That silenced even Nest.

Somebody near the back said, “God's judgement.”

Someone else said, “Bad air from the towns.”

A third voice muttered something about foreigners and ships.

The traveller closed his eyes for a moment.

“Call it what you like.”

Madog said, “If it's as bad as you say, why are you travelling?”

The man opened his eyes.

“Because staying did not make the dead less dead.”

Nobody had an answer to that.

Angharad eventually put one hand on Ieuan's shoulder.

“Come.”

He resisted without moving.

“I want to hear.”

“You have heard enough.”

“But—”

“Your sister needs watching.”

Gwenllian, three paces away and doing nothing dangerous whatsoever, looked offended.

“I don't.”

“You do now,” Angharad said.

Ieuan was dragged back into ordinary life by force of maternal instruction.

He spent the walk home trying to imagine a house where nobody answered the door.

It would not settle properly in his head.

A house meant people.

A fire.

Someone shouting from another room.

A pot needing moving.

An animal making noise outside.

Gwenllian talking when nobody had asked her to.

A house with nobody in it was simply a roof over things.

By evening, though, the thought had faded.

Dafydd found water working beneath a bank and Ieuan spent an hour helping divert it. Gwenllian dropped a wooden spoon into the fire and cried because Angharad made her retrieve another one herself. Someone had to check the red cow. Someone had to carry fuel.

The world kept giving them things that needed doing.

On Saturday night, Ieuan barely slept.

Not because of the sickness.

Because he wanted Sunday morning to arrive.

He knew this was foolish.

Two dry nights did not make anything certain.

Three might.

He woke before dawn.

For several heartbeats he was too frightened to move.

Then he checked.

Dry.

A laugh came out of him before he could stop it.

He covered his mouth.

The little end remained dark.

He sat up slowly.

Three.

Three dry nights in the same week.

It had stopped.

It had to have stopped.

His prayer had been heard.

The thought came so quickly and felt so obvious that he immediately followed it with another prayer, this one mostly apology for assuming he understood what God had done.

But he grinned while doing it.

Easter morning spread clear and cold over Glynrhondda.

Angharad had kept back what she could for the day. Nothing about the meal would have impressed a lord, but to Ieuan it felt rich after the restraint of Lent and the solemnity of the previous days: better bread than the ordinary coarse pieces, eggs, a little cheese, ale, and enough that Gwenllian began asking what would be left before she had finished what was already in front of her.

“Nothing if you keep talking over it,” Dafydd said.

“I can talk and eat.”

“We've noticed.”

Gwenllian pointed at Ieuan.

“He eats faster.”

“He's bigger.” Angharad sat down at last. “That's what happens when people grow.”

“I'll be bigger than him.”

“No,” Ieuan said.

“You don't know.”

“I do know that.”

“What if I grow very tall?”

“Then you'll be an ugly tall woman.”

Gwenllian gasped.

Angharad tried not to laugh and failed.

It was not a polite laugh. It burst out of her, louder than Ieuan expected, and once she had started she could not stop because Gwenllian's offended face made it worse.

Even Dafydd smiled.

Ieuan stared at his mother.

“What?” she said.

“Nothing.”

“Then stop looking at me like I've grown another head.”

Gwenllian was still furious.

“I will be beautiful.”

“Of course,” Dafydd said.

Ieuan frowned.

“You didn't even look.”

“I know better than to answer that question wrongly.”

Angharad kicked him lightly beneath the table.

Later, outside, Dafydd and Ieuan checked the cattle together before they went toward church.

The brindled cow had stopped trying the gate.

Ieuan checked the bank anyway.

Dafydd watched him do it.

“You've been checking that every day?”

“Yes.”

“Without me telling you?”

Ieuan tried to make his voice casual.

“I know she pushes it.”

Dafydd grunted.

That was all.

They walked several paces.

Then Dafydd said, “Good.”

One word.

Ieuan felt it all the way into his chest.

He immediately pretended he had not.

At church the Easter gathering was warmer than Palm Sunday had been, despite the cold outside.

People smiled more. Children were less successfully controlled. Even Meurig looked temporarily restored by the day, though he still had the expression of a man who expected someone to bring him a problem before noon.

Ieuan saw Gruffudd.

Gruffudd looked at him.

Neither mentioned cows.

That felt almost holy.

Elen asked Gwenllian something and both girls laughed.

Ieuan's stomach tightened.

He watched Gwenllian's face.

She glanced at him once.

Then turned back to Elen.

Whatever she said, Elen did not look at Ieuan.

The secret remained his.

He felt ashamed of how relieved that made him on Easter Sunday.

The traveller appeared later in the day.

Not the same man as Good Friday.

This one came from the direction of the Penrhys route with a small pack and a stick polished pale where his hand had rubbed it. He was older, perhaps Madog's age, though thinner, and one boot had been tied around the ankle with cord where the leather had split.

He did not arrive dramatically.

He asked whether the way toward Llantrisant remained passable after the rain.

Madog told him it did.

Then someone asked where he had come from.

That was enough.

The man had seen sickness too.

Not rumours.

Not a cousin of somebody who had heard from somebody else.

He had passed a place where graves were being opened faster than the ground could settle over the previous ones. He had slept in a barn because an inn would not admit strangers. He had seen a servant turn away from a house because everybody inside was ill.

People listened differently this time.

Dafydd did not mock the source.

Nest did not say judgement.

Meurig asked careful questions.

“How long between the first sick and the last?”

The traveller rubbed his mouth.

“I don't know.”

“Days?”

“Days. A week. Less in some houses.”

“Did anyone recover?”

“One man said his brother did.”

“Saw him?” Madog asked.

“No.”

Madog nodded once, accepting the weakness of the answer without dismissing everything around it.

A man Ieuan barely knew said, “Those places are far from here.”

The traveller looked at him.

“They were far from the last place too.”

Nobody spoke.

Ieuan could hear children farther away. Somebody laughing. A dog barking at nothing. Wind moving over the slope.

The stranger shifted his pack higher.

“I'd go before dark,” he said.

Dafydd asked, “You think it will reach Glamorgan?”

The traveller looked at him for long enough that Ieuan wished his father had not asked.

Then the man said, very quietly,

“It is coming this way.”
