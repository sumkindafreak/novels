# Chapter Eleven — A Man's Place

**1352–1354**

Mail was easier to understand than men.

A broken ring was broken.

A leather strap was cracked or it wasn't.

Rust appeared where water had been allowed to sit. A buckle failed because somebody had ignored it. A horse limped because something hurt.

Men could look you in the face and tell you that you did not belong somewhere while asking you to do the work that kept them there.

Ieuan learned this slowly.

The first time he helped arm a fighting man, he almost dropped the mail shirt.

"Both hands," the man snapped.

"I have both hands on it."

"Then use stronger ones."

Ieuan gritted his teeth and lifted.

The shirt was heavier than it had looked folded over a bench. It smelled faintly of oil, sweat and old metal. Beneath it the man wore padded protection that made him seem larger before any steel went on.

Ieuan helped settle the mail.

Checked a strap because he had been told.

Passed a helmet.

Then a sword.

He held the sword one second too long.

The man noticed.

"Planning to keep it?"

Ieuan released it.

"No."

"Good."

"I only—"

"When you can keep the rain out of my mail, perhaps I'll care what you can do with a blade."

Ieuan's face heated.

He returned to the straps.

Later, alone, he replayed the insult until it became a challenge.

He kept the mail dry.

---

Iorwerth ap Morgan first spoke to him in Welsh.

That was why Ieuan liked him for almost three seconds.

They were in a yard outside a larger household stopping place, and Ieuan was struggling with a pack saddle somebody else had fitted badly. A tall man in his late twenties or early thirties watched from near the wall.

"You're pulling against yourself," he said in Welsh.

Ieuan turned, surprised by the language.

The man's clothes marked him immediately as someone above Ieuan's rank even before the sword at his side did. Not a great lord. Not a man servants scattered before. But no one would mistake him for stable labour.

"I'm not."

"You are."

"The strap's twisted."

"Because you're pulling against yourself."

Ieuan looked down.

It was.

He hated the man immediately.

"I knew that."

"Then your hands are disobedient."

Ieuan untwisted it.

The man stepped closer.

"Who taught you horses?"

"Rhys ap Llywelyn."

"And who taught you to answer questions as though every one is an accusation?"

Ieuan stared.

The man's mouth moved slightly.

"Iorwerth ap Morgan."

Ieuan had heard the name.

An esquire from a better Welsh family, moving in the same broad military and household network as men serving Warwick and Matthew de Soor.

Ieuan said, "Ieuan ap Dafydd."

"I know."

That unsettled him.

"Why?"

"Because you've been described."

"By who?"

"Men who are tired of you arguing with them."

Ieuan bristled.

Iorwerth looked at the saddle.

"Still wrong."

Ieuan looked down.

It was not.

He turned back sharply.

Iorwerth was already walking away.

Ieuan stared after him.

A groom beside the door laughed.

"He likes you."

"Then I'd hate to see dislike."

"You will."

---

Ieuan expected shared language to mean shared ground.

It did not.

Iorwerth spoke Welsh as naturally as Ieuan did, but his Welsh came from a house where men owned land, carried arms and expected to be listened to.

He knew French words Ieuan still had to catch from context.

He sat where Ieuan did not sit.

He spoke to knights without the small change in posture Ieuan had learned to make automatically.

When he gave a servant an order, nobody paused to consider whether the order belonged to him.

Ieuan resented him for this.

Iorwerth appeared not to notice.

That made it worse.

The first time he corrected Ieuan publicly, it was over a helmet strap.

"Too loose," Iorwerth said.

Ieuan had checked it.

"It isn't."

Iorwerth pulled once.

The strap slipped.

Several men saw.

Ieuan wanted the ground to open.

Iorwerth simply handed it back.

"Again."

Ieuan fixed it.

For the next month he checked every strap twice.

He also imagined twelve different arguments in which he proved Iorwerth wrong.

None happened.

---

Weapons came later.

Not because anyone decided Ieuan had the soul of a warrior.

Because armed men travelled with horses, baggage, money, documents and goods, and sometimes the servants standing near those things were expected not to run away when trouble appeared.

His first training was with a staff.

Ieuan was terrible.

He had strength enough.

Balance too.

What he did not have was any idea where to put himself.

He stepped too far.

Crowded the man beside him.

Turned when he should have held.

Tried to use speed where position mattered.

Iorwerth knocked him onto his back in wet grass.

Ieuan lay there breathing hard.

"Again," Iorwerth said.

"You could tell me what I did wrong."

"You left the line."

"I was trying to get around you."

"Exactly."

Ieuan sat up.

"If I get around you, I can hit you."

"And the man beside you gets hit because you've opened the space."

"So I'm supposed to stand there?"

"Sometimes."

Ieuan frowned.

"That's stupid."

Iorwerth nodded.

"War will disappoint you often."

He offered a hand.

Ieuan ignored it and stood himself.

Iorwerth sighed.

"Pride is a heavy pack."

Ieuan glared.

"Rhys says that."

"Then perhaps one day you'll listen to one of us."

---

He improved.

Not quickly enough for his liking.

Spears made more sense because distance made more sense. Holding position made sense once he understood what happened to the men beside him if he did not. A shield was less about looking heroic than keeping a piece of wood where it stopped something sharp.

The sword came later.

The first time Ieuan held one for practice, he expected it to feel important.

It felt awkward.

Heavy near the hilt in a different way from the work knives he knew. Alive in the hand, but only because mistakes travelled quickly down the blade.

Iorwerth watched him make several.

"You're trying to win every exchange."

"Isn't that the point?"

"The point is to be alive after it."

Ieuan lowered the sword.

The words were simple.

He understood them better than Iorwerth knew.

---

In 1354, Ieuan carried a spear on a real journey for the first time.

The party was small.

Two armed men.

Iorwerth.

Ieuan and another servant.

Three horses carrying goods and documents that somebody considered valuable enough to guard.

The route was not meant to be dangerous.

That meant nothing.

They met four men near a wooded stretch where the track narrowed.

Not soldiers.

Not obviously thieves.

Men who stood where travellers had to slow down and asked questions that were not theirs to ask.

Iorwerth's hand settled near his sword.

Ieuan's heart accelerated.

This was it.

The moment every practice exchange had promised.

One of the strangers stepped closer to the lead horse.

Ieuan moved.

Iorwerth's arm struck his chest hard enough to stop him.

"Stay."

"He's reaching—"

"Stay."

The stranger looked from Ieuan to Iorwerth.

Something in Iorwerth's face made him reconsider.

Words followed.

Hard ones.

The men wanted payment for passage they had no right to demand. Iorwerth refused. One reached toward a rein.

Ieuan tightened his grip on the spear.

"Stay," Iorwerth said again.

Ieuan stayed.

That was harder than moving.

Eventually the men stepped aside.

No fight.

No blood.

No great story afterward.

Ieuan was furious.

"We could have taken them."

Iorwerth looked at him.

"Perhaps."

"Then why—"

"Because the documents arrived. The horses arrived. We arrived."

"They insulted us."

"You think steel exists to settle every insult?"

Ieuan said nothing.

Iorwerth nodded toward the packs.

"Your job was not to prove you are brave. Your job was to bring those through."

Ieuan hated the lesson.

He needed it.

---

Better equipment came in pieces.

A padded garment first.

Not new.

Altered.

It pinched beneath one arm until a seam was let out.

A simple helmet whose previous owner had apparently possessed a head shaped differently from Ieuan's.

A spear that was not ceremonial and not his forever but was expected to remain in good condition.

Mail elements later, old but serviceable.

Ieuan became proud of them despite himself.

The first time another servant saw the better protection and said, "They dressing stable boys as gentlemen now?" Ieuan nearly started a fight.

The man was called Robert and came from a family with enough property that his resentment had logic behind it.

"Somebody has to keep gentlemen alive," Ieuan said.

Robert smiled.

"You think a helmet does that to you?"

"No."

"Good."

The conversation ended.

They worked beside each other the next day without incident.

Ieuan began understanding that class could cut without anybody being evil.

Men had been taught the world had an order.

He was becoming inconvenient to that order.

Part of him enjoyed it.

---

The wetting became rarer.

Not gone.

Never gone enough that he trusted it.

In late 1354, while travelling with Matthew's household network, Ieuan woke after several dry weeks and knew.

His whole body went cold.

There were men sleeping three feet away.

Not stable boys who had known him from fifteen.

Men who carried weapons.

Servants trying to climb the same social ladder.

Men like Robert.

Ieuan moved before panic could freeze him.

Rolled the bedding.

Separated the damp cloth.

Took it outside under the excuse of checking a horse.

One servant stirred.

"Where you going?"

"Mare was coughing."

Too much detail.

Ieuan knew immediately.

The servant grunted and slept again.

Outside, Ieuan leaned against the wall with the wet cloth hidden beneath his arm.

He was nineteen.

He could ride hard for a day.

Maintain armour.

Use a spear without embarrassing himself.

Understand commands in more than one language.

Travel armed.

Keep expensive horses alive.

And one wet blanket could still turn him back into the boy in the little end.

He stood there until the shame passed enough to work.

It always passed enough to work.

---

By the end of 1354, a word began appearing more often in conversation.

Gascony.

Ieuan knew where it was in the way people without maps knew distant places: across the sea, under the English king's claim, full of war and wine and men whose names travelled farther than they did.

Then other words gathered around it.

Ships.

Supplies.

Warwick.

The prince.

Horses.

Wages.

Service.

Ieuan listened too closely.

Iorwerth noticed.

"You want to go."

Ieuan pretended surprise.

"Where?"

"Rome."

Ieuan gave him a look.

Iorwerth smiled faintly.

"Gascony."

Ieuan looked toward the stable.

"Maybe."

"Bad liar."

"Everybody says that."

"Because everybody has eyes."

Ieuan rubbed a thumb along a repaired strap.

"Would they take me?"

Iorwerth's expression changed slightly.

Not pity.

Assessment.

"If Matthew's people need horses managed, you're useful. If baggage needs guarding, you're useful. If you keep imagining overseas war is a tournament song, you're not."

"I don't."

"Good."

Later, word came.

Ieuan would be part of the preparations.

He said yes too quickly.

The man delivering the instruction barely noticed.

That night Ieuan lay awake and understood what yes meant.

France.

Sea.

A campaign.

Gwenllian's grave on a mountain farther away than it had ever been.

For six years, he had been leaving home in small pieces.

This would be the first time the distance could not be walked back.
