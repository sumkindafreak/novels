# Chapter Fifteen — Winter Arms

**December 1355–summer 1356**

The promotion began with someone else mucking out Brân's stall.

Ieuan stood in the stable entrance and stared at the younger servant.

The boy looked up.

"What?"

"What are you doing?"

"What does it look like?"

"Why?"

The boy frowned.

"Because I was told."

"By who?"

"Master John."

Ieuan stepped inside.

"He rubs on the left if the blanket sits too far forward. And the grey beside him needs—"

"I know."

"You don't know."

"You've told me four times."

Ieuan stopped.

That was true.

From behind, Iorwerth said, "If you interfere with that poor boy's dung any longer, I will make it officially yours again."

Ieuan turned.

"Why isn't it mine now?"

"Because you're wanted."

"Where?"

"Armed watch."

"Again?"

"You sound disappointed."

"I'm not."

"Then stop looking at the stall like you've been widowed."

Ieuan followed him.

The order became a pattern.

Ieuan was still responsible for his horse in the way every mounted fighting man remained responsible for the animal keeping him useful.

But he was no longer simply the man who kept other men's horses ready.

Someone else carried part of that work because Ieuan was expected elsewhere.

At first he found this inefficient.

Then frightening.

---

The formal change came without ceremony.

A senior Warwick-affinity officer sat at a table with another man and several records while Ieuan stood in front of them feeling as if he had been summoned for theft.

Iorwerth stood near the wall.

The officer looked Ieuan over.

"Ieuan ap Dafydd."

"Aye."

"Matthew de Soor's people speak well of your years in service."

Ieuan did not know what answer this required.

"Aye."

The officer looked at Iorwerth.

"Does he always answer like that?"

"When nervous, yes. When confident, he argues."

Ieuan glared.

The officer almost smiled.

"The November action. Remounts. You held with the escort?"

"I was ordered."

"And before that?"

"Horses. Household work. Armed escort when needed."

"Can you fight?"

Ieuan thought of the brown sleeve.

"Enough."

The officer nodded.

"Good answer."

Ieuan disliked that too.

The arrangement was explained in practical pieces.

Ieuan would serve at man-at-arms level within the wider company.

Higher expectation.

Higher pay.

A suitable horse approved for the role.

Equipment to be brought up to standard.

Armed duty as routine rather than exception.

A lower servant assigned where necessary.

Ieuan listened.

He had wanted this for years.

The wanting and the receiving felt nothing alike.

"Questions?" the officer asked.

Ieuan had twenty.

"No."

Iorwerth sighed audibly.

The officer looked at him.

"He has questions."

"He always has questions."

Ieuan scowled.

"What?"

The officer leaned back.

"Ask them. Better here than while somebody is trying to kill you."

Ieuan did.

Pay.

Horse.

Whose equipment.

Which watch.

Who he answered to.

What happened if Matthew's people shifted elsewhere.

By the end, the officer looked tired.

Iorwerth looked pleased.

Ieuan realised this had been a test too.

Everything was a test once men decided you might rise.

---

His armour did not fit.

Not all of it.

That was almost reassuring.

The padded layers could be altered.

Mail sat properly enough after adjustment.

One plate reinforcement pinched at the shoulder and rubbed skin raw during the first long duty.

Ieuan endured it for two days because complaining felt weak.

Iorwerth noticed him rolling the shoulder.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"Take it off."

"It's fine."

"Take it off."

Ieuan removed the piece.

The skin beneath was broken.

Iorwerth looked at him.

"You planned to let armour wound you before the French get another chance?"

"It'll wear in."

"Your flesh?"

Ieuan said nothing.

The piece was altered.

That annoyed him more than the pain because it proved how easily the problem could have been solved if pride had not got there first.

The rest of his equipment came together the same way his status had.

Nothing descended from heaven complete.

A serviceable bascinet.

Mail.

Padded protection.

Better gauntlets.

Sword.

Spear.

Pieces issued, purchased, altered, inherited through household stores or bought with money earned in a campaign that had burned other people's homes.

Ieuan looked at the whole collection one night and thought:

*Two cows.*

Then realised some of it was worth more than that.

The old mental measure remained.

Cattle.

Grain.

Winter.

He had entered a world where men wore fortunes on their bodies.

---

The younger servant assigned to Ieuan was called Tomas.

He was sixteen and convinced he knew everything about horses because his uncle bred them.

Ieuan disliked him instantly for reasons that had nothing to do with Tomas.

"Strap's done," Tomas said.

Ieuan checked it.

"I said it's done."

Ieuan pulled once.

Loose.

Tomas's face reddened.

"I was going to tighten it."

"Then it wasn't done."

"You don't have to check everything I do."

Ieuan opened his mouth.

Dafydd's voice came out.

"Apparently I do."

Ieuan stopped.

Tomas stared.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You looked strange."

"Fix the strap."

Tomas muttered something.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Ieuan almost laughed.

Later, when Tomas handled a nervous horse properly without help, Ieuan watched.

The boy looked at him, waiting.

Ieuan said, "Good."

Tomas tried to hide how pleased he was.

Ieuan felt something in his chest twist.

He knew exactly what one word could do.

He began using it more carefully after that.

---

The winter accident came after enough dry nights to make him careless.

That was always how it happened.

Weeks without trouble.

A growing confidence that perhaps, at twenty, the body had finally finished humiliating him.

Then warmth.

Cold.

Awareness.

Ieuan's eyes opened in darkness.

For several heartbeats he was fourteen.

The little end.

Mam beyond the cloth.

Gwenllian's voice.

Tad's sigh.

Then winter quarters returned around him.

Men sleeping close.

Better bedding than he used to own.

Armed men whose respect he had spent years earning.

Ieuan's heart hammered.

He moved silently.

Fold the damp inward.

Get the cloth away.

Find dry straw.

Outside before anyone—

"Where are you going?"

Iorwerth.

Ieuan froze.

"Horse."

Too fast.

Iorwerth sat up.

"What horse?"

"Mine."

"What happened?"

"Nothing."

Ieuan knew from the silence that the lie had failed.

Iorwerth stood.

Ieuan held the rolled bedding against himself like stolen goods.

Iorwerth looked at it.

Then at Ieuan.

Understanding arrived.

Ieuan wanted to die.

Not literally.

For the first time since 1349, shame briefly seemed worse.

"Don't," Ieuan said.

Iorwerth frowned.

"Don't what?"

"Say anything."

"I haven't."

"You know."

"Aye."

Ieuan waited for disgust.

A joke.

Questions.

Some new way the world would punish him for being the wrong sort of man inside the armour.

Iorwerth looked toward the cold doorway.

"Change it before it freezes."

Ieuan stared.

"What?"

"The bedding. Unless you want to sleep on a board tomorrow."

"That's all?"

Iorwerth looked genuinely confused.

"Do you want advice?"

"No."

"Good. I have none."

He lay back down.

"We're on watch before dawn. Be ready."

Ieuan stood with the wet bedding in his arms.

Nothing happened.

His life did not collapse.

His rank did not disappear.

Iorwerth did not become unable to look at him.

Ieuan went outside.

He changed the bedding.

For the first time in his life, an outsider knew.

The world kept turning.

He almost did not know what to do with that freedom.

---

By spring 1356, Ieuan had served enough armed duties that the title no longer felt borrowed every morning.

Patrols.

Garrison watches.

Escorts.

Small frontier operations.

Once, a minor position yielded after negotiation without a fight.

Ieuan watched prisoners give weapons over and found the whole thing strangely civil.

A captured man was not automatically killed.

Rank mattered.

Means mattered.

A man alive could be worth money.

That idea had always existed around him, but now Ieuan understood the practical structure.

Who accepted surrender.

Who had claim.

Who owed shares upward.

How custody worked.

One man said, "Dead Frenchmen are useful to God. Live Frenchmen are useful to us."

Iorwerth called that blasphemous and economically accurate.

Ieuan remembered it.

---

He also began possessing money in a way he never had before.

Not wealth.

Wages.

Shares.

Equipment whose maintenance mattered because replacing it would hurt him directly.

A stake.

Ieuan once held a sum in his hand and automatically translated it into grain.

Then cattle.

Then how long Hendre Gelli could have survived on it.

He stopped.

"What?" Tomas asked.

"Nothing."

"You always say nothing when it's something."

Ieuan looked at him.

"You're becoming irritating."

"I learned from you."

Ieuan almost smiled.

The thought of knighthood still did not occur seriously to him.

Knights were men like Matthew de Soor.

Iorwerth perhaps one day.

Edward Despenser already.

Men with standing, land, horses, money, names people knew.

Ieuan was pleased enough that men now expected him to stand beside them in danger.

That had once seemed impossible.

---

In early July, new orders came.

The Prince of Wales was moving out again.

On **6 July 1356**, the campaign began to gather at La Réole.

Ieuan readied his horse.

Tomas brought the tack.

"Checked?" Ieuan asked.

"Twice."

Ieuan checked anyway.

Tomas sighed.

"You don't trust me."

"I don't trust straps."

"Same thing."

Ieuan tightened one buckle a fraction.

"Good."

Tomas tried not to smile.

Ieuan looked toward Iorwerth.

The older esquire was already armed.

"Ready?" he asked.

Ieuan settled the sword at his side.

Once he would have said no because readiness felt too close to pride.

Now he knew better.

"Ready enough."

Iorwerth nodded.

They rode north.
