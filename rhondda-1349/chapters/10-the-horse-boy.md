# Chapter Ten — The Horse Boy

**Winter 1349–1352**

The first winter Ieuan spent away from Hendre Gelli taught him that horses could be more troublesome than people and more expensive than either.

He had thought cattle were difficult.

Cattle were honest.

A cow wanted grass, water, space and whatever calf it had decided belonged to it. If frightened, it usually showed you what frightened it. If angry, it generally made the fact obvious before trying to put a hoof through you.

Horses lied.

At least that was how Ieuan described it to Rhys after a grey gelding stood perfectly still for ten minutes and then tried to bite him when he reached for the girth.

"It didn't lie," Rhys said.

"It did."

"How?"

"It looked calm."

"You looked stupid. Neither thing means what you think."

Ieuan glared.

Rhys handed him the brush again.

"Watch the ear."

"I was."

"No. You were watching the mouth because you expected teeth. The ear told you first."

Ieuan looked at the gelding.

One ear had indeed turned back.

"Now what?" Rhys asked.

"Don't reach?"

"Not like that. Let him know where you are."

Ieuan put one hand on the horse's shoulder and moved more slowly.

The gelding stayed still.

"Better," Rhys said.

That word still had power over him.

He hated that.

---

Winter work began before light and ended when the animals were settled, which meant it ended whenever the animals permitted.

Ieuan carried hay until his back ached.

He mucked out stalls until he stopped noticing the smell and then noticed only when somebody else smelled worse.

He learned to keep tack dry, to feel heat in a leg before swelling became obvious, to notice when a hoof had begun to crack and when a horse's refusal was pain rather than temper.

He learned that a riding horse could be ruined by a badly fitted strap and a pack animal by a man who thought one extra sack could not matter.

He learned men lied about loads more often than horses did.

"It's only another half-sack," one servant said.

Ieuan put both hands on the pack and pushed.

The pony shifted.

"Take it off."

"Rhys said—"

"Rhys isn't under it."

The servant laughed.

"You've become important."

Ieuan's face heated.

"Take it off."

The man did, mostly because Rhys arrived at that moment and said, "If the boy is telling you not to break my pony, perhaps listen."

Ieuan remembered that for weeks.

Not the praise.

The word.

*My pony.*

Rhys trusted him with something that belonged to someone else.

That was a different kind of trust from Hendre Gelli.

At home, everything had been shared because there were only four of them.

Here, every object had an owner.

Every tool, saddle, animal, blanket, bucket, bit of leather, bit of iron and mouthful of grain belonged to somebody with more right to it than Ieuan did.

He learned to ask before touching.

He learned who expected him not to ask.

He learned that rank was a language long before he learned enough English or French to understand half the words around it.

---

The nights were harder.

He slept with other servants whenever work took them too far from Glynrhondda.

Sometimes in lofts.

Sometimes near stables.

Sometimes on floors in rooms where five men breathed, snored and scratched themselves through the dark.

Ieuan began drinking less after dusk.

It did not always help.

He learned to wake at the slightest movement.

To check bedding before anyone else stirred.

To keep a spare handful of straw near when he could.

To volunteer for early horse checks because being the first awake made things easier.

Once, after three bad nights of little sleep, Rhys caught him leaning against a stall wall with his eyes closed.

"If you fall under that mare, she'll flatten you."

Ieuan opened his eyes.

"I'm awake."

"Your face isn't."

"I didn't sleep much."

Rhys looked at him.

Ieuan almost said why.

The words rose as far as his throat.

Then shame closed around them.

"Owain snores."

From the next stall, Owain shouted, "I heard that."

Rhys said, "So did everybody within ten miles."

The moment passed.

Ieuan was relieved.

Then angry with himself for being relieved.

He could move cattle alone across a hillside.

He could sleep beside men who had survived plague, burial and hunger.

But he could not say four simple words.

*I wet the bed.*

He hated the power of them.

---

By spring 1350, Rhys gave him a bay pony with a white mark on one hind leg and said, "This one's yours."

Ieuan looked up sharply.

Rhys rolled his eyes.

"To work. Not to inherit."

"I know."

"Good. Because he's worth more than your opinion of yourself."

The pony was called Brân by one servant and "that bastard" by everyone else.

Ieuan preferred Brân.

The first week, Brân stepped on him twice.

The second, Ieuan put the saddle on badly enough that Rhys made him remove and refit it three times while two older servants watched.

"Again," Rhys said.

Ieuan pulled the strap free.

"It was fine."

"Again."

"It won't slip."

Rhys touched one finger beneath the girth.

"Again."

Ieuan wanted to throw the saddle at him.

He did not.

He learned.

By summer he could prepare Brân without thinking through each movement.

By autumn he could tell from twenty paces whether the pony was standing wrong.

By winter Rhys left him responsible for three animals while he travelled elsewhere for two days.

Ieuan pretended this was unremarkable.

He checked them six times before noon.

---

The first route Ieuan got badly wrong was not in the Rhondda.

That was the reason.

He had become too used to knowing.

In Glynrhondda, water had patterns.

Tracks had histories.

A turn in the ground told him what lay beyond because he had walked it since childhood.

Farther south and east, the land stopped answering him.

In 1351 he travelled with a small pack group carrying goods and messages between holdings connected to the broader Glamorgan estate world.

One instruction came partly in English.

Ieuan understood most of it.

He thought he understood the rest.

That was worse than not understanding.

He took them toward a crossing that looked usable from above.

The first pony sank nearly to the knee.

Ieuan swore and pulled the animal back.

The man behind him shouted.

"Other way!"

"I know!"

"Then why are we here?"

Ieuan's face burned.

The proper crossing lay half a mile farther down.

The error cost them time, soaked one load and earned him a lecture from Rhys that lasted exactly three sentences.

"If you don't understand, ask. If you think you understand, check. Pride is heavier than any pack."

Ieuan hated that too.

He remembered it forever.

After that, he began collecting routes like tools.

Landmarks.

Fords.

Bridges.

Places where carts cut ground badly after rain.

Which holding would give water.

Which would charge for it.

Which officials expected English.

Which servants switched easily between Welsh and English.

Which French words meant something expensive was about to become his responsibility.

His languages grew through work.

Not elegantly.

He learned the words for strap before poetry.

For oats before honour.

For wait, hold, lead, stop, lame, broken, payment, lord and fool.

The last existed everywhere.

---

He went back to Glynrhondda in the autumn of 1351.

Not because he had decided to go home.

Work took him near enough that pretending he had no reason became ridiculous.

Hendre Gelli stood.

That was the first surprise.

Ieuan had imagined decay happening faster.

The roof had changed. Somebody had repaired one side with different material. The yard was used, though not as his family had used it. A man Ieuan barely knew now kept animals near part of the holding under an arrangement Ieuan did not understand and did not ask about.

The house was no longer empty.

That hurt differently.

He stayed outside.

The man came to the door.

"Ieuan?"

He nodded.

"I heard you were with stock men near Llantrisant."

"Sometimes."

"Place hasn't been left to rot."

Ieuan looked at the roof.

"I can see."

The man shifted awkwardly.

"Your mam—"

"I know."

He did not.

Not where.

Not exactly.

That had never changed.

Ieuan left before the conversation could become kindness.

He climbed instead.

Gwenllian's grave took longer to find than he expected.

Panic rose before he saw the stones.

Then there it was.

Smaller.

That offended him.

The grave had been enormous in his memory.

The whole mountain had seemed built around it.

Now it was a low place under stones and rough grass.

Ieuan stood above it.

He was taller.

That was all.

The grave had not shrunk.

He had grown.

"That's stupid," he said aloud.

He repaired two stones that had shifted.

Then sat.

He told Gwenllian about Brân.

Not because horses mattered.

Because she would have complained that he never told her anything.

"He bites," Ieuan said. "You'd like him."

The wind moved over the ridge.

"Rhys is still an arse."

That felt important to report.

He stayed until light began to go.

Returning home had not taken him backward.

There was nowhere backward to go.

---

Sir Matthew de Soor entered Ieuan's life through a horse.

Ieuan did not meet the knight first.

He met the animal.

A dark bay with a white star and the temperament of a lord whose tax had gone unpaid.

"Careful," an older groom said. "He'll strike if you crowd him."

Ieuan looked at the horse.

"Why?"

"Because he's evil."

"Horses aren't evil."

"This one is."

Ieuan watched.

The horse disliked pressure from the left rear. One ear stayed fixed when anyone approached there. An old injury perhaps. Pain. Memory.

Ieuan moved differently.

The horse stayed calm.

The groom frowned.

"Do that again."

Ieuan did.

Later he was told the animal belonged to men travelling in Sir Matthew's wider household and administrative service.

That meant little until someone specifically asked for him the next time the horse needed moving.

"The Welsh boy," a servant said.

Ieuan looked around.

"Which Welsh boy?"

"You."

"There are twenty of us."

"The one who doesn't get kicked."

Ieuan almost said that narrowed it only slightly.

He went.

Better horses meant better tack.

Better tack meant every mistake had a price.

Leather was cared for differently.

Metal had to be kept clean and dry.

Servants around armed men moved with a sharper sense of who could order whom.

Ieuan began seeing mail, helmets and weapons not as exciting objects but as things that rusted, split, tore, needed oil, needed straps and weighed too much when the man who owned them was not carrying them.

One afternoon a senior servant handed Ieuan a helmet and said, "Clean that. Properly."

Ieuan caught it awkwardly.

The metal was heavier than expected.

"Whose?"

The man looked at him.

"Does that change how clean it gets?"

"No."

"Then why ask?"

Ieuan looked at the helmet.

War entered his hands that day as dirt and maintenance.

Nothing more.

---

By 1352, people had stopped calling him *the plague boy* where he worked.

Most had forgotten the story.

Or heard so many stories like it that his no longer seemed unusual.

He became the horse boy instead.

That should have annoyed him.

It did not.

A horse boy had a job.

He travelled with better mounts.

Carried messages when someone trusted his memory.

Supervised younger servants badly at first, then less badly.

Prepared animals for men carrying arms.

Learned when to speak and, more importantly, when men above him expected silence.

He was still a servant.

Still ate where servants ate.

Still slept where servants slept.

Still checked his bedding before anyone woke.

Still returned to Glynrhondda when he could.

Still carried Dafydd's little folding knife even though he now handled things worth ten times more.

One evening, a man in Matthew's service tossed a mail coif toward him without warning.

Ieuan caught it against his chest.

The weight nearly pulled his arms down.

"Oil it," the man said. "Check the edge. There's a broken ring somewhere near the left side."

Ieuan looked at the mail.

Then at the man.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Then stop standing there."

Ieuan took the armour to the light.

He spread it carefully across his knees.

For the first time in his life, something made for war had been placed in his hands because somebody trusted him not to ruin it.
