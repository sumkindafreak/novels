# Chapter Eight — Empty Houses

**14–30 June 1349**

Hendre Gelli looked wrong before Ieuan reached the door.

Nothing obvious had changed.

The roof was still the roof.

The yard still sloped the same way toward the wetter ground.

The hurdle Dafydd had complained about still leaned slightly because Ieuan had never fixed it properly after all.

A hen scratched near the wall as if none of those facts mattered.

What was wrong was that nobody came out.

Ieuan had known nobody would.

He had known all the way down from the mountain.

Knowing did not stop him listening.

Mam calling from inside.

Gwenllian asking where he had been.

Tad telling him to stop standing in the doorway.

Nothing.

Ieuan put his hand on the latch.

He almost knocked.

The movement stopped halfway.

He opened the door.

The smell struck first.

Not death.

He had feared that.

Instead: cold ash, damp cloth, old food, mouse droppings, the stale closed-house smell of a place that had not been properly lived in for days.

Ieuan froze.

Angharad was gone.

The bedding where he had covered her was empty.

For one terrible second he thought she had woken.

He knew better.

Still the thought came.

"Mam?"

His own voice sounded ridiculous.

No answer.

The cloth he had used remained folded badly near the wall.

Someone had come.

Someone had taken her.

Ieuan stepped inside.

"Who?"

The house did not answer that either.

He looked for signs because signs were easier than grief.

Mud near the entrance that was not his.

A moved stool.

The door had been shut again.

Someone had cared enough to shut it.

That meant someone had found her.

Taken her where?

Churchyard?

A grave somewhere else?

Was Meurig there?

Had anyone said her name?

Ieuan put both hands against the table and breathed.

He had come for food.

Food.

Only food.

He looked toward the storage place.

Then at the little end.

His sleeping bay waited behind the hanging cloth exactly as he left it.

For some reason that was worse than the empty main bedding.

He could not go in.

Ieuan turned away.

---

The stores were not what memory promised.

That annoyed him.

In the shelter he had imagined Hendre Gelli full of food because Hendre Gelli had always contained food.

Mam kept it.

That did not mean it was endless.

The meal sack was lower than he remembered. The peas Angharad told him about were still there but one corner had become damp. A smaller bag had been gnawed through and Ieuan swore when grain spilled across the floor.

"Bastards."

He looked for the rat and saw nothing.

A clay vessel still held salt.

Not much.

He found a hard piece of cheese wrapped where Angharad hid better food from Gwenllian and stared at it.

Then laughed bitterly.

"Knew it."

Gwenllian always said Mam hid things.

Ieuan sat with the cheese in his hand.

He could almost hear his sister complaining that he had found it after she was dead.

He cut a piece with Dafydd's knife.

Ate it.

Immediately felt guilty.

Mam would have said not that much.

The thought made him take another piece out of spite.

Then he wrapped the rest carefully.

Ieuan gathered what he could carry.

A smaller bag of meal.

Peas.

Salt.

A better cup.

One more blanket.

Cord.

Needle and thread because Mam would have asked why he had taken everything except the thing that repaired clothes.

He reached for a heavier iron tool.

Stopped.

Put it back.

He had learned something from the stool.

Before leaving, he stood in the centre of the house.

"I'm coming back," he said.

He did not know who the promise was for.

Then he heard a cow lowing nearby.

Ieuan went outside.

---

The cow was theirs.

He knew her immediately.

Not the red one.

The smaller dun cow that had always been easier to milk and harder to keep from pushing her nose into places it did not belong.

She stood beyond the yard with a rope trailing from her neck.

Ieuan stared.

"How are you still here?"

The cow chewed.

He moved toward her.

She stepped away.

Ieuan stopped.

Old habit told him to go faster before she moved farther.

Dafydd's voice came instead.

*Watch the beast before you move it.*

Ieuan exhaled.

The cow was not fleeing.

She was watching him and the doorway behind him, uncertain.

He turned slightly instead of walking straight at her.

Waited.

The cow stretched her neck.

Ieuan held out one hand.

"Come on."

Nothing.

"You've been stupid for years. Don't start being clever now."

The cow stepped closer.

He let her smell him.

Then took the rope.

The relief was absurd.

A cow.

Not a person.

Not rescue.

A cow.

Still something alive now depended on what he did next.

Ieuan checked her udder and swore softly.

Too full.

He brought her into the yard and milked awkwardly because the first attempt hurt his blistered hands.

Warm milk struck the vessel.

A normal sound.

For the first time in days, he ate because there was a reason to eat.

The cow needed him strong tomorrow.

That was enough.

---

He slept outside the main room that night.

Not at the mountain shelter.

Not properly inside Hendre Gelli either.

He made a place near the service end where air moved and the doorway remained close.

The house was his.

He could not bear it.

In the morning he returned uphill with food and checked Gwenllian's grave.

Then came down again.

That became his pattern for several days.

Mountain.

House.

Cow.

Stores.

Fire.

Grave.

Sleep.

The routine almost became safe.

Then he went to Madog's holding.

---

Ieuan stood outside longer than he had at his own door.

Madog's place had never belonged to him.

That mattered.

The yard was emptier than before. No cow. No Nest. No Elen.

The cap still hung on the peg visible through the doorway.

Ieuan remembered Elen telling someone not to move it.

He did not touch it.

He called once.

"Nest?"

Nothing.

"Elen?"

Nothing.

Maybe they had left.

Maybe they were dead elsewhere.

Maybe they would return tomorrow and find Ieuan stealing from them.

He nearly left.

Then saw the coil of rope.

Good rope.

Better than what remained at Hendre Gelli.

The cow needed securing.

Winter would need more.

Ieuan stepped inside.

"I'm borrowing it," he said.

Madog's house stayed quiet.

He took the rope.

Then found a small sack of grain.

He did not take that.

He found a usable pot.

Left it.

A metal hook.

He picked it up.

Put it down.

"Rope," he told himself. "Only rope."

At the door he stopped.

Nest used to complain if anyone entered with muddy shoes.

Ieuan looked at his boots.

"Sorry."

He almost smiled.

Then hated that he had smiled.

He left with the rope.

Three days later he returned for the grain.

Survival was eating rules one small bite at a time.

---

The churchyard had changed.

Ieuan approached from the side and saw new earth before he saw people.

Too much new earth.

Not one great pit.

Graves.

Some close together.

Some poorly finished.

Several marked only enough that somebody would know where a person lay.

The church door stood open.

Ieuan hesitated.

"Meurig?"

No answer.

He went inside.

The space smelled of wax, damp and emptiness.

A man Ieuan vaguely recognised from farther down the valley was moving something near the wall.

The man turned quickly.

"Who's that?"

"Ieuan. Dafydd's son."

Recognition took time.

Then pity arrived and Ieuan immediately disliked him.

"Ah."

"Where's Meurig?"

The man looked away.

"Don't know."

"What do you mean?"

"He was here. Then he went to a house. Somebody said he was ill. Somebody else said he went farther down."

"Is he dead?"

"I said I don't know."

Ieuan clenched his jaw.

"Who took my mam?"

The man looked at him again.

"Your mam?"

"Angharad. From Hendre Gelli. Somebody took her from the house."

The man rubbed his forehead.

"There've been many."

"She isn't many."

The words came out hard.

The man stiffened.

Then his face softened.

"No."

Ieuan waited.

"I think two men from below found someone at Gelli. A woman. They brought her here."

"Where?"

The man looked toward the churchyard.

"I don't know which grave."

Ieuan stared at him.

"You buried her and don't know where?"

"I didn't bury her."

"Who did?"

"Boy, I don't know."

Ieuan wanted to hit him.

Instead he became still.

The man seemed more frightened by that.

Ieuan went outside.

He stood among the new graves.

One of them was Mam.

Maybe.

Perhaps.

He could not even be certain.

He knelt beside one mound because it looked the right age.

Then realised three others did too.

He said a prayer over all of them.

That would have to be enough.

Nothing was ever enough anymore.

---

Ieuan met Goronwy over a sack of peas.

That was how the living re-entered his world.

Not with help.

With an argument.

The sack sat in a small storage shed beside an abandoned holding Ieuan did not know well. He found it first.

At least he believed he had.

He was dragging it toward the doorway when a voice said, "Leave it."

Ieuan froze.

A man stood outside.

Not old.

Older than Ieuan. Perhaps twenty-five. Perhaps thirty. Thin from bad weeks, beard untidy, one sleeve torn near the elbow.

A knife hung at his belt.

Ieuan's hand went to Dafydd's.

The man's eyes followed.

"Don't."

"It's mine."

"Is it?"

"I found it."

"So did I yesterday."

"Then why didn't you take it?"

"Because I had two hands and three things."

Ieuan tightened his grip on the sack.

"That's not my problem."

The man almost smiled.

"No. It isn't."

For a moment neither moved.

Ieuan had imagined meeting another survivor many times.

In none of those imaginings did the person want his peas.

"Name?" the man asked.

"Why?"

"So I know what to call you while you're stealing my food."

"Your food?"

"Goronwy ap Tudur."

Ieuan said nothing.

Goronwy raised his brows.

"Or I can call you piss-face if you prefer."

The word hit Ieuan like a blow even though Goronwy could not possibly know.

His knife came halfway free.

Goronwy's expression changed immediately.

"Easy."

Ieuan stopped.

His heart hammered.

Goronwy held both hands slightly apart.

"I meant nothing by it."

Ieuan slowly pushed the knife back.

"Don't call me that."

"Fine."

"Ieuan."

"Ieuan what?"

"Ap Dafydd."

Goronwy looked at him more carefully.

"Dafydd from Gelli?"

Ieuan's body went rigid.

"You knew him?"

"Knew who he was."

"He's dead."

Goronwy nodded.

"Most are."

The flatness of it made Ieuan hate him.

Then he saw something in the older man's face and realised flatness was not the same as not caring.

Goronwy looked at the sack.

"Half."

"No."

"Then I take it."

"Try."

Goronwy sighed.

"You really want to bleed over peas?"

Ieuan looked down.

No.

He did not.

"Half," Goronwy repeated.

Ieuan cut the sack's contents between two smaller containers as fairly as he could.

Goronwy watched every handful.

"You count like an old widow."

"And you're still getting half."

"Aye."

They left in opposite directions.

Ieuan looked back twice.

So did Goronwy.

The dead were easier.

The dead did not make claims.

---

By the end of June, the cow had become Ieuan's clock.

Morning milk.

Movement.

Water.

Evening.

He hated being tied to her.

He feared losing her more.

One afternoon he stood on the slope watching the grass and realised something.

It was growing.

That should have been good.

Instead his stomach sank.

Hay.

Winter.

The words came together like a trap.

The cow could live now because grass was everywhere.

In winter, grass would not be everywhere.

She would need fodder.

Cut.

Dried.

Stored.

Protected from rain.

Ieuan looked toward Hendre Gelli.

Roof repairs.

Stores.

Grain.

Fuel.

The cow.

Maybe other stock worth saving.

The upland shelter.

Gwenllian's grave.

All of it.

One boy.

He had survived from morning to morning because morning was close enough to understand.

Winter was months away.

For the first time since the plague entered the valley, Ieuan thought that far ahead.

The thought terrified him.

Not because winter might kill him.

Because some part of him had assumed he would still be there when it arrived.

Ieuan stood in the long grass with Dafydd's knife at his hip and the cow chewing behind him.

He looked at the work.

One boy could not do it all.
