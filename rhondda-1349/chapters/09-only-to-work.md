# Chapter Nine — Only to Work

**July–autumn 1349**

Rhys ap Llywelyn arrived on a wet morning with three men, two ponies and the expression of someone who had already been disappointed by the day.

Ieuan saw them from the slope above Hendre Gelli.

His first thought was *Goronwy*.

His second was *officials*.

Neither improved his mood.

He left the cow where she was and walked down slowly, one hand near the knife at his belt.

The strangers had stopped outside the holding.

One man was checking the empty enclosure. Another had gone to look at the roof. The third held both pony reins and swore softly when one animal tried to eat a piece of wet thatch.

The older man in the centre looked up as Ieuan approached.

He was perhaps thirty-five, broad through the chest without being large, beard cut short enough not to catch in tack, clothes practical rather than poor. His boots were muddy in the way of someone who expected them to be muddy.

"You live here?" he asked.

Ieuan stopped several paces away.

"Who wants to know?"

The man's eyes moved briefly to the knife.

"A boy with manners, clearly."

"Who are you?"

"Rhys ap Llywelyn."

Ieuan waited.

Rhys seemed to expect more.

"And?"

"And I'm standing in the rain asking whether you live here."

Ieuan looked at the other men.

"What do you want?"

Rhys sighed.

"Animals. Names. Holdings that still have people in them. Holdings that don't. Anything that can be moved before somebody else steals it, dies beside it or lets it wander into the next valley."

"Who sent you?"

"Men who own more paper than sense."

One of the others laughed.

Rhys gave him a look.

"Some of it belongs to Llantrisant administration. Some to people who still have claims. Some we're sorting because nobody knows whose it is anymore. Happy?"

Ieuan was not.

"The cow's ours."

Rhys looked toward the slope.

"That one?"

"Aye."

"You milk her?"

"Aye."

"Alone?"

Ieuan stiffened.

"I know how."

"Didn't say you didn't."

"She's ours."

"I heard you the first time."

Rhys looked past him toward the house.

"Dafydd ap Hywel?"

Ieuan's jaw tightened.

"Dead."

"Angharad?"

"Dead."

Rhys's eyes returned to Ieuan.

"Sister?"

Ieuan did not answer.

That was answer enough.

Rhys nodded once.

No pitying noise.

No hand on the shoulder.

He turned toward the man near the enclosure.

"Write—"

Then stopped.

"No, don't write anything yet."

The other man muttered, "I haven't got anything to write on."

"Then your incompetence has saved us time."

Ieuan almost smiled.

Rhys looked at him again.

"How many cattle did your father keep?"

Ieuan told him.

"How many have you seen since?"

Ieuan told him that too.

"Madog ap Rhys's animals?"

"One I know. Maybe more higher up."

"You know the upper tracks?"

"Better than you."

Rhys's mouth moved slightly.

"Probably."

He asked Ieuan where water held after rain. Which path remained usable for stock. Which holding below had a broken enclosure. Where the ground pinched near a bank. Whether anyone still lived at Nest's house.

Ieuan answered everything he could.

When he did not know, he said so.

Rhys noticed that.

"You worked cattle with your father?"

"Aye."

"Horses?"

"Not much."

"Good."

Ieuan frowned.

"Why good?"

"Boys who've touched a pony twice usually tell me they're horsemen. Takes longer to unteach that than teach the animal."

Rhys looked up at the weather.

"We're moving stock tomorrow. You can help."

Ieuan stared.

"No."

"Food for the day."

"No."

"You know the tracks."

"They're my tracks."

"The mountain doesn't know your name."

"I have work here."

Rhys glanced at the roof, then the overgrown patches, then the cow.

"Aye. More than you can do."

Heat rose in Ieuan's face.

"I've done it this long."

"It's July."

"I know what month it is."

"Do you know how much hay you'll need?"

Ieuan said nothing.

Rhys nodded.

"Tomorrow. We start after first light. If you aren't there, we'll manage badly without you."

He turned away.

"I said no."

Rhys looked back.

"I heard."

Then he left.

Ieuan hated him immediately.

---

The next day Ieuan proved Rhys wrong.

For half a day.

He cut grass until his shoulders burned.

He turned what he had cut.

Moved the cow.

Repaired one section of the roof.

Carried water.

Checked the upland shelter.

Returned to find the weather changing.

He worked faster.

That was the mistake.

A tied bundle slipped.

He tried to carry too much at once.

The load opened halfway across the yard and scattered damp grass into mud.

Ieuan swore, dropped to his knees and gathered it.

Rain began.

Not heavy.

Enough.

By the time he had the hay under cover, much of it was wet again.

The cow had worked loose while he was carrying.

He found her near the lower track.

Then remembered the grain he had left uncovered because the roof patch was not finished.

He ran back.

Water had come through exactly where he knew it would.

Ieuan stood in the house with rain dripping from his hair and looked at the damp grain.

He could still salvage most of it.

That was not the point.

His hands shook.

"I can do it," he said aloud.

Nobody argued.

The silence made him angrier.

"I can."

He worked until dark.

At some point he cut his palm on a tool and wrapped it badly.

He slept without eating enough.

In the morning the wound had opened again.

Ieuan looked at the work still waiting.

One boy could do all of it.

If days were twice as long.

If he never slept.

If nothing ever went wrong.

If winter never came.

---

Rhys returned three days later.

Ieuan saw him and considered hiding.

He was ashamed enough that hiding from a man he disliked seemed reasonable.

Instead he kept mending a strap.

Rhys approached alone this time.

"Roof leaks."

"I know."

"Hay's wet."

"I know."

"Hand's bleeding."

Ieuan looked down.

The cut had soaked through the wrap again.

"I know."

Rhys sat on a low stone.

"You're very knowledgeable."

Ieuan glared.

"If you're here to laugh—"

"I'm here because we're moving cattle tomorrow."

"I said no."

"And I didn't listen."

Ieuan returned to the strap.

Rhys watched.

"Three days," he said.

"What?"

"Work three days. Food while you're with us. Help move stock. You show us the upper route. One of my men helps you bring that hay under cover properly afterward."

Ieuan stopped.

"Why?"

"Because I need the route. You need hands."

"You'll take the cow."

"No."

"How do I know?"

"You don't."

Ieuan stared at him.

Rhys shrugged.

"That's how people work."

"People steal."

"Aye."

"People lie."

"Aye."

"Then why should I trust you?"

"You shouldn't. Not yet."

That answer unsettled Ieuan more than a promise would have.

Rhys nodded toward the strap.

"You're putting the buckle backward."

Ieuan looked.

He was.

He swore and turned it.

Rhys did not smile.

"Three days."

Ieuan kept working.

"Temporary."

"That's what three days means."

"I come back here."

"Aye."

"Every night."

Rhys considered.

"First two, maybe. Third we're too far toward Llantrisant. You'll sleep with the men."

Ieuan's stomach tightened.

"No."

Rhys studied him.

"Why?"

"I don't sleep with strangers."

"Then sleep beside the horses. They complain less."

Ieuan looked at him sharply.

Rhys had already moved on.

"First light tomorrow. Bring the knife. Leave anything you can't afford to lose."

He stood.

"I'm only coming to work," Ieuan said.

Rhys nodded.

"Of course."

Ieuan hated the way he said it.

---

Working beside men again was exhausting in ways solitude had not been.

They spoke.

Constantly.

They complained about food, weather, animals, officials, each other and anyone richer than themselves. One man sang badly until Rhys threatened to make him walk behind the cattle where nobody would hear him over the lowing.

Ieuan had forgotten how often people touched things without asking.

Someone borrowed his cord.

Someone else took the place by the fire he had been looking at.

A man called Owain ate from the wrong bowl and did not understand why Ieuan became angry.

"It's a bowl."

"It's mine."

"Looks like everybody else's."

"It isn't."

Owain handed it back.

"You're a strange little bastard."

Ieuan nearly hit him.

Rhys said, "He's fourteen. You're twenty-eight. If he wins, I'm dismissing you."

Owain laughed.

Ieuan did not.

Later Owain passed him a better piece of bread without comment.

Ieuan took it.

That was how friendships seemed to work among men: insult, threat, bread.

Rhys gave orders without explaining each one.

Ieuan resented this until he realised explanations came after work if he asked a sensible question.

"Why not take them down the lower way?" Ieuan asked while they held a group of cattle near a junction.

"Soft ground."

"It's dry today."

"Cart went through yesterday. Cut it up."

Ieuan nodded.

Another time he argued.

"That track's too narrow for six."

One man said, "Boy knows everything."

Rhys looked at Ieuan.

"Why?"

"Wall on one side. Drop on the other. Third cow will turn when she can't see the front."

"How do you know?"

"Because that's what ours did last winter."

Rhys considered.

Then changed the route.

Nobody praised Ieuan.

Nobody mocked him either.

It felt almost exactly like working with Tad.

That frightened him more than he expected.

---

The first time Ieuan handled one of Rhys's ponies, he did it wrong.

He approached as he would a calm cow.

The pony moved sideways before he expected and nearly crushed his foot against a wall.

Rhys caught the reins.

"Not cattle."

Ieuan jerked away.

"I know."

"Apparently not."

"It moved."

"Horses do that."

Ieuan glared.

Rhys handed him the reins again.

"Watch her ears. Watch the eye. Don't stand where her fear puts you under a hoof."

Ieuan's irritation faded.

The words were different.

The lesson wasn't.

He looked at the pony properly.

"She's watching the cloth."

A loose piece of canvas flapped behind them.

Rhys nodded.

"Move her or move the cloth?"

Ieuan thought.

"Cloth."

"Good."

One word.

It struck him in exactly the same place Dafydd's had on Easter morning.

Ieuan looked away quickly.

---

Three days became six.

Then ten.

Not all together.

Ieuan returned to Hendre Gelli when work allowed.

He milked the cow.

Checked the roof.

Went up to Gwenllian's grave.

Sat there and told her almost nothing.

He did not tell her he liked the ponies.

That felt disloyal.

He did not tell her he sometimes laughed with Owain.

That felt worse.

He did tell her that Rhys was an arse.

That felt safe.

By August, Ieuan had become part of stock work across a wider stretch of country than he had ever regularly travelled.

He saw holdings where survivors returned.

Others taken over by kin.

Others still standing empty.

Men argued over animals whose owners were dead.

Officials tried to remember old obligations when half the people who knew them were gone.

The world had not ended.

It had become badly organised.

Ieuan found that almost offensive.

He had expected the death of everyone he loved to mean more to the world.

Instead the world needed cattle moved.

---

The first journey toward Llantrisant changed the size of everything.

Ieuan had known the name all his life.

Llantrisant was where things came from.

Orders.

Officials.

Stories.

People who could make rules about land they had never milked a cow on.

Seeing more traffic on the routes unsettled him.

Carts.

Pack animals.

Men speaking English more often than Ieuan heard it at home.

Different clothes.

Surviving families moving goods.

Religious men.

Servants.

People who had never heard of Hendre Gelli and did not care that Dafydd ap Hywel was dead.

Ieuan stayed close to the animals.

Animals at least explained themselves if you watched.

That evening the group stopped too far from Glynrhondda for Ieuan to return.

Rhys pointed toward a covered place near the stock.

"There."

Ieuan looked at the other men preparing to sleep.

His belly tightened.

He had been dry for nearly two weeks.

That meant nothing.

He knew that now.

"I'm sleeping by the ponies."

Owain said, "They snore."

"So do you."

"I do not."

Three men answered at once that he did.

They argued.

Ieuan carried his blanket nearer the animals.

Rhys noticed.

Said nothing.

Ieuan lay down with tack smell, horse breath and the warmth of bodies nearby.

He stared into darkness.

This was the first night since the plague began that he had chosen to sleep away from Glynrhondda.

Nobody forced him.

No one was sick.

He could have turned around that morning and gone home.

Only to work, he told himself.

Only to work.

A pony shifted beside him.

Ieuan closed his eyes.
