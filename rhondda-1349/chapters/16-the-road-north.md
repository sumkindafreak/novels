# Chapter Sixteen — The Road North

**6 July–18 September 1356**

The second campaign felt different before it began.

Ieuan noticed it in the horses first.

In 1355 he had ridden out worrying whether he belonged among armed servants at all. He had checked other men's tack, other men's loads, other men's horses, and waited for the moment someone remembered he was still only a groom with better equipment.

On **6 July 1356**, Tomas tightened Ieuan's girth and stepped back.

Ieuan checked it anyway.

"You do know I checked that," Tomas said.

"Aye."

"Then why?"

"Because I like arguments before sunrise."

Tomas sighed.

Ieuan adjusted one strap by less than a finger's width.

"There."

"It was fine."

"Now it's finer."

Iorwerth rode past.

"If the two of you are finished inventing improvements to leather, move."

Ieuan mounted.

Nobody handed him a spear as an emergency precaution.

It was his duty to carry one.

Nobody told him to keep close to the baggage because that was where his usefulness lay.

He rode with men expected to fight.

The promotion had stopped feeling borrowed.

That frightened him more than when it had.

---

The summer settled into work.

War could become boring.

Ieuan had not expected that.

Danger came and went around hours of routine.

Watches.

Patrols.

Water.

Armour that needed cleaning whether anyone struck it or not.

Food that arrived late.

Orders changed by men who never explained why.

Scouts disappeared up roads and returned with dust, sweat and information that reached Ieuan only after being cut into whatever piece he needed.

He learned not to crave the whole picture.

The whole picture belonged to princes.

His part was smaller.

Be in the right place.

Keep the horse sound.

Keep the spear usable.

Know where Tomas was.

Know where Iorwerth was.

Know which direction meant safety if somebody shouted a name Ieuan did not recognise.

The northern movement through France brought familiar things in unfamiliar arrangements.

Fields.

Rivers.

Villages that closed themselves ahead of the army.

Fortified places that might bargain rather than fight.

Men whose surrender became a transaction before the dust had settled.

At one small action, Ieuan watched a defender hand over his weapon and give his name.

The man was not stripped naked and killed.

He was guarded.

Questions asked.

His means discussed.

Iorwerth saw Ieuan watching.

"First time you've seen it close?"

"Aye."

"Remember it."

"Why?"

"Because if a man yields and you accept it, you've changed what happens next."

Ieuan looked at the prisoner.

"He becomes money."

Iorwerth's expression tightened.

"Among other things."

"That's what everyone says."

"Everyone says many stupid things."

Ieuan waited.

Iorwerth added, "A prisoner is also responsibility. If you accept him, you don't cut his throat five minutes later because you're tired."

"I know."

"Good."

Ieuan looked at the surrendered man again.

Alive had rules.

Dead did too.

War was full of lines that only existed because men agreed not to step across them at certain moments.

He wondered how many people had died because nobody heard the surrender in time.

---

By August the campaign had moved far enough north that distance stopped being something Ieuan could measure against Wales.

He knew Bordeaux was behind them.

He knew roads had carried them through country whose names he could pronounce badly and remember well enough for work.

He knew the Loire and Tours entered conversation more often.

He knew French forces were somewhere beyond the information reaching him.

Everything else was rumour.

A larger army.

A king moving to intercept them.

Roads closed.

Bridges threatened.

Food becoming harder to find.

The stories felt like April 1349.

That resemblance unsettled him before he understood why.

Bad news arrived through other people.

Someone had heard.

Someone had seen.

Scouts said one thing.

Travellers another.

Men with rank disagreed about what mattered.

Meanwhile the horses still needed water.

Then one day the danger stopped being far away.

Exactly as it had with Madog.

Ieuan realised the pattern and felt cold despite the heat.

Iorwerth noticed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"You've been looking north for ten minutes."

"They say King John is close."

"They say a great many things."

Ieuan looked at him sharply.

Iorwerth caught the expression.

"What?"

"Tad used to say that."

The older man waited.

Ieuan shook his head.

"Nothing."

"There it is again."

Ieuan smiled despite himself.

Then checked his horse.

Work still existed.

That helped.

---

The first half of September tightened around them.

Orders came faster.

Then stopped.

Then reversed.

Men packed and unpacked.

Scouts returned after dark.

The army moved through the Chauvigny and Poitiers region under increasing pressure from a French force larger than anything Ieuan wanted to imagine.

He did not see the king.

He saw the effects of a king nearby.

Officers speaking quietly.

Horses pushed despite fatigue.

Archers moved with purpose.

Men who normally joked before sleep checking equipment twice.

Iorwerth stopped mocking Ieuan for checking straps because he was doing the same with everything else.

On **17 September**, the atmosphere changed completely.

Ieuan woke to men already looking toward the approaches.

Tomas brought water and said, "Are they here?"

"Who?"

"The French."

Ieuan almost laughed.

"No, Tomas. The Welsh bishops."

The boy scowled.

"You know what I mean."

Ieuan looked beyond him.

"Aye."

"Are they?"

Ieuan listened.

Nothing dramatic.

No great horn saying *the enemy has arrived*.

Only men moving differently.

"Close enough."

Tomas swallowed.

Ieuan recognised the movement.

He had done it at fourteen whenever somebody said fever.

Checking for fear.

Was it here yet?

Could he feel it?

Was his mouth dry because of danger or because he had not drunk?

Was his hand shaking?

Did other men notice?

He stopped himself.

"Check the left rear shoe," he told Tomas.

"I already did."

"Do it again."

Tomas sighed.

Normal irritation returned.

That helped too.

---

On **18 September**, nobody seemed certain whether the next day would contain battle.

Messengers moved.

Men waited.

Negotiation existed somewhere above Ieuan's rank.

He knew only its symptoms.

Prepare.

Wait.

Don't move.

Be ready to move.

Eat now.

No, hold the food.

Check the horses.

Check the ground.

Ieuan hated waiting more than marching.

Waiting gave the mind too much land to wander over.

He found a priest when opportunity came and confessed.

Not everything.

No man confessed everything because memory itself was too large.

He confessed killing.

The burning bothered him more than he expected to say aloud.

The stolen food.

The anger at God that had never fully stopped since 1349.

He almost confessed the wetting out of habit and then wondered why that felt like sin at all.

He did not.

Afterward he sat with his equipment.

Sword.

Helmet.

Gauntlets.

Straps.

Mail.

Dafydd's knife.

The knife looked absurd beside the weapons made for war.

Ieuan opened it.

Checked the edge.

Folded it shut.

No reason.

He always did.

Iorwerth sat beside him.

"If we fight tomorrow," Ieuan said, "where are we?"

"Warwick's first battle."

"I know. I mean where."

Iorwerth looked toward the hedged ground and broken approaches that shaped the position.

"Where we're told."

Ieuan exhaled.

"Helpful."

"You keep wanting war to become a map."

"Maps are useful."

"Not when somebody is hitting you."

They sat in silence.

Ieuan said, "You frightened?"

Iorwerth considered lying.

"Aye."

Ieuan nodded.

"Good."

"Good?"

"Means I'm not doing it wrong."

Iorwerth laughed quietly.

"Sleep."

Ieuan tried.

He barely managed.

Every time he closed his eyes, some small sound pulled them open.

A horse shifting.

A man coughing.

Metal moving.

At some point before dawn, he stopped trying.

He got up.

Tomas was already awake.

"Couldn't sleep," the boy said.

Ieuan nodded.

"Check the horses."

Tomas looked offended.

"I've checked them."

"Again."

Ieuan began putting on armour.

First light reached the ground slowly.

Orders came.

Men-at-arms would fight dismounted.

Ieuan looked at Brân.

For years a horse problem had always given him something useful to solve.

Today he would leave the horse behind and stand where someone else told him.

He handed the reins to Tomas.

"Keep him clear."

"I know."

Ieuan almost said *watch the beast before you move it*.

He stopped.

Tomas knew.

Ieuan joined Iorwerth and the men of Warwick's vanguard.

There was no more work he could invent to postpone what came next.
