# Chapter Seven — The Waiting

**7–14 June 1349**

For the rest of Sunday, Ieuan kept looking at the blanket.

That was easier than looking at Gwenllian.

He had pulled it to her chin, then lower because she hated it near her face, then higher again because the air had turned cold. Twice he put the cup beside her. Twice he moved it away.

Once he touched her forehead and found warmth still trapped in the skin.

For half a heartbeat he thought that meant something.

Then he remembered Tad had stayed warm after he died too.

Ieuan sat back.

Rain whispered across the roof.

The cracked cup lay where he had thrown it.

The fire wanted feeding.

He watched it dim.

"You're going to make it go out," he said.

Gwenllian did not answer.

"Mam would blame you."

Nothing.

"She'd blame me really. She always did."

Ieuan rubbed both hands over his face.

"That's not true."

He waited.

The silence stayed the same.

By evening he had stopped expecting anyone to come.

That was not the same as accepting that no one would.

Every change in the wind made him look toward the entrance.

Every small animal sound on the slope became a footstep until it wasn't.

Meurig might arrive.

A neighbour.

Somebody from Hendre Gelli.

Mam, in the stupidest corner of his mind.

The light faded.

Ieuan fed the fire at last because darkness felt worse.

Then he sat beside his sister until sleep took him sitting up.

---

He woke before dawn with his neck twisted painfully and one hand still resting on the blanket.

Gwenllian had not moved.

Ieuan stared at her.

The practical thought came without permission.

Today.

He had to bury her today.

He hated himself for thinking it.

Then hated himself for the hours he had already waited.

"Sorry," he whispered.

He looked around for a proper spade.

There wasn't one.

The shelter had never needed one kept permanently. Tools came up with people when work required them and went back down again.

Ieuan had a smaller digging implement, the knife, and hands.

That would have to be enough.

He chose ground not far from the shelter where the soil looked deeper between stones.

He was wrong.

The first layer came away easily enough.

Below it the earth resisted.

Stone appeared everywhere.

Small stone.

Larger stone.

Roots.

Packed ground that seemed determined to prove Gwenllian did not belong there.

Ieuan dug until his palms split.

He stopped.

Looked at the hole.

It was not deep enough.

He dug again.

The morning rose around him while he worked.

Birds made noise in the scrub.

A cow called somewhere farther across the grazing.

The world behaved disgustingly normally.

When Ieuan finally returned to the shelter, Gwenllian looked smaller than she had the night before.

That was impossible.

He knew it was impossible.

Still she did.

He wrapped her in the blanket.

Then stopped.

It was one of the blankets they needed.

The thought made him feel sick.

He almost unwrapped her.

Then stared at his hands.

"No."

He left it around her.

The little carved animal lay beside the wall.

Gwenllian had kept it through the climb, fever and everything after.

Ieuan picked it up.

It was badly made.

Tad had cut one leg shorter than the others and Gwenllian insisted that proved it was a horse because horses were always standing strangely.

Ieuan put it into the blanket with her.

Then changed his mind.

He took it out.

He stood there holding it.

If he buried it, there would be nothing left of hers except memory.

If he kept it, he was taking from her.

Ieuan squeezed the wood until its rough edge hurt his palm.

Then he tucked it beside her hand.

"You wanted it."

The words cracked.

He lifted her.

A living Gwenllian had never been easy to carry because she wriggled, complained and told you where your arms were wrong.

Dead, she was simply weight.

Ieuan almost dropped her at the grave.

He lowered her badly and immediately apologised.

"Sorry. Sorry."

The grave was too narrow.

He had to widen it.

When he finally laid her properly, he stood above her and tried to remember what Meurig said at burials.

He knew pieces.

Latin shapes without meaning.

Welsh prayers.

The sign of the cross.

Words about earth.

Words about resurrection.

He said what he could.

Forgot half of it.

Started again.

"God knows what I mean," he muttered.

Then became afraid that saying that arrogantly might make the prayer worse.

He apologised to God.

Then to Gwenllian.

Then got angry that he was apologising at all.

"You should be at the church," he said.

The valley wind moved the grass.

"You're meant to be by Tad."

His throat closed.

"And Mam. Wherever they put Mam."

He did not know where his mother was.

That thought struck him almost as hard as the burial itself.

He began putting earth back.

Each shovelful made the grave more real.

When the blanket disappeared beneath the soil, Ieuan stopped.

He wanted to pull everything back out.

Instead he kept going.

By midday there was a low mound.

Too low.

Too obvious.

Too shallow.

He piled stones over it because animals existed and he knew what animals could do.

That knowledge nearly broke him.

He added more stones.

Then more.

Even when there was no need.

When he finished, he thought immediately:

*Not deep enough.*

The thought stayed.

---

On Tuesday he checked his armpits nineteen times.

He counted those too.

By Wednesday he stopped counting.

Every ache was plague.

His shoulders hurt from digging.

Plague.

His thighs hurt from climbing.

Plague.

His head hurt because he had eaten almost nothing.

Plague.

The skin at his neck felt warm after sitting near the fire.

Plague.

He pressed his fingers into the flesh beneath one arm until he made it sore, then panicked because the soreness was now proof of swelling.

He checked the other.

Then his groin.

Then his neck.

Nothing.

He waited for fever.

He had watched it take Madog.

Tad.

Mam.

Gwenllian.

It had always come quickly once it began.

Ieuan woke each morning surprised.

Then suspicious.

Perhaps this was the morning.

Perhaps the cold feeling in his hands meant it.

Perhaps the heaviness behind his eyes.

He ate little.

There was food.

That made no difference.

He could chew and not swallow.

He could swallow and feel sick.

Once he carried a bowl outside and sat beside Gwenllian's grave with it until flies found the food.

He threw it away.

"Sorry," he told her.

He was apologising constantly now.

For food.

For the grave.

For sleeping.

For not sleeping.

For being alive.

Then he became angry.

Not cleanly.

Not bravely.

The anger arrived like weather and left the same way.

He prayed.

Then accused God of taking too much.

Then apologised for accusing God.

Then asked why God needed an apology from a boy whose whole family was dead.

Then apologised for that too.

Eventually he stopped speaking aloud because the sound of his own voice irritated him.

The shelter became very quiet.

---

On the night of the tenth, Ieuan slept deeply.

He knew because he woke wet.

The shame arrived before thought.

His hand went beneath the blanket.

Damp.

His stomach dropped.

He sat up quickly and looked toward the other side of the shelter.

Nobody.

The movement stopped.

There was no Gwenllian to laugh.

No Mam to quietly point toward clean straw.

No Tad to sigh and say *again?*

Ieuan stared at the empty space.

For several breaths, he could not understand what feeling had replaced embarrassment.

Then he laughed.

One short sound.

It escaped him before he could prevent it.

Ieuan clapped a hand over his mouth.

His eyes went immediately toward the grave outside, though he could not see it through the wall.

"Sorry."

The laugh had been wrong.

Obscene.

His sister was dead and he had laughed because he'd wet himself.

Then he realised why.

He had slept.

Properly.

Deep enough for the old stupid thing to happen.

His skin was not burning.

No swelling.

No chills.

No pain except the pain he already knew.

Ieuan put his hand beneath one arm.

Nothing.

The other.

Nothing.

His neck.

Nothing.

He was alive.

Not safe.

Not protected.

Alive.

The bedding smelled terrible.

He laughed again.

This time it turned into crying so quickly there was no boundary between the two.

Ieuan bent forward and pressed his forehead into the blanket.

"You'd laugh," he whispered.

He could hear Gwenllian's answer perfectly.

*It was funny sometimes.*

He cried until he was exhausted.

Then, because there was nobody else, he changed the straw.

---

Hunger won two days later.

Not courage.

Not faith.

Hunger.

Ieuan woke with pain in his belly and realised he could smell food that did not exist.

Bread.

Cheese.

The thick grain Mam made in the mornings.

He sat up and inventoried what remained.

A little meal.

Some peas.

Almost nothing he wanted to eat.

Enough to survive another day if he forced it.

Not enough for long.

Below, Hendre Gelli still had stores.

Unless somebody had taken them.

Madog's holding might have grain.

Unless Nest had returned.

There were cattle.

Tools.

Cloth.

Everything people had left behind when the sickness scattered them.

Ieuan hated the thought of going down.

He hated the thought of staying more.

He remembered Tad checking supplies before winter and making him count sacks twice.

Not because Dafydd had ever said anything wise about it.

He simply counted before deciding what could be spared.

Ieuan counted now.

Then counted again.

"Food," he told the empty shelter.

That was all he was going for.

Food.

Then back.

He was not leaving Gwenllian.

He was not choosing a new life.

He was hungry.

There was a difference.

---

On Sunday morning, one week after Gwenllian died, Ieuan stood beside the grave.

The stones had not moved.

That relieved him.

Then frightened him because it meant he had truly expected something might disturb them.

He adjusted one stone anyway.

"I'm going down."

He felt stupid saying it.

"Just for food."

The grave offered no objection.

Ieuan carried Dafydd's knife, one vessel, cord and the small amount of food left.

He looked back at the shelter.

Then at Gwenllian's grave.

He did not say goodbye.

He would be back.

That was not hope.

It was a plan.

He started downhill.

The same track he had climbed with Gwenllian eleven days earlier seemed steeper in reverse.

Halfway down, he realised he was listening for her complaints.

The silence followed him all the way toward the houses.
