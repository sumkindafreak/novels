# Chapter Eighteen — Sir Ieuan

**20 September 1356–summer 1357**

The day after Poitiers, Ieuan woke because somebody was arguing about money.

For several seconds he lay still beneath a borrowed blanket and did not know where he was.

The habit of fourteen years returned before memory.

Check the bedding.

Dry.

Then everything else arrived.

France.

Battle.

Iorwerth.

Guillaume.

King John captured.

Ieuan sat up too quickly and pain answered from half a dozen places.

His shoulder had stiffened during the night. One knee objected to bending. His ribs hurt where armour had taken an impact he barely remembered.

A man nearby was saying, "His lord will pay."

Another said, "If his lord has coin."

"Land, then."

"Land doesn't fit in a purse."

Ieuan looked across the temporary camp.

Prisoners seemed to be everywhere now that the fighting had stopped.

Men who yesterday had come forward under French banners sat, stood, limped and negotiated under English and Gascon guard. Some retained servants. Some had lost everything except a name valuable enough to keep them alive.

Ieuan pushed himself up.

"Where's Iorwerth?"

Robert, sitting nearby with cloth wrapped around his head, pointed.

"Still alive. Still complaining."

"Good."

"He says the surgeon is a butcher."

"Better."

"Why better?"

"Dead men don't complain."

Robert considered.

"You should write a book of wisdom."

Ieuan looked at him.

"I can't write."

"Even better."

Ieuan went to find Iorwerth.

---

The wound was serious.

That became clearer once battle noise no longer made every injury seem temporary.

Iorwerth had taken a deep wound at the side where armour had not stopped enough of the blow. He had lost blood. Fever was the thing everyone watched for now.

Ieuan hated fever.

The old terror returned every time Iorwerth's skin felt warm.

"Stop touching my forehead," Iorwerth said.

Ieuan pulled his hand away.

"You're hot."

"We're in a tent."

"You're sweating."

"So are you."

"I'm not wounded."

"Your face is wounded."

Ieuan touched the bruise near his cheek.

"That's nothing."

"Then perhaps everything is nothing and we can sleep."

Ieuan sat beside him.

"You nearly died."

Iorwerth opened one eye.

"So did you."

"Different."

"Only because you're the one telling it."

Ieuan looked away.

Iorwerth's voice softened slightly.

"The Frenchman?"

"Alive."

"Good."

"Everyone keeps saying that."

"Because dead prisoners pay poorly."

Ieuan frowned.

"You're all vile."

"And yet you took him."

"He yielded."

"Aye."

Ieuan rubbed both hands over his face.

"I nearly killed him anyway."

Iorwerth was quiet.

"But you didn't."

"Only just."

"Only just is often the distance between one sort of man and another."

Ieuan looked at him.

"That sounded wise."

"Fever."

Ieuan laughed.

The sound hurt his ribs.

For the first time since the battle, he realised he was glad simply to be sitting beside someone alive.

---

Sir Guillaume de la Motte became more complicated after he stopped trying to kill Ieuan.

That irritated Ieuan.

Enemies were easier when they remained enemies.

Guillaume was perhaps thirty-four, from Poitou, with land sufficient to make his name worth attention and not enough to make anyone mistake him for a great lord. He had a wife. Two children.

Ieuan learned this because ransom required information.

Who he was.

Who would answer for him.

What he could reasonably pay.

Which kin or lord might provide surety.

Guillaume answered with the dignity of a man who found being priced insulting but preferable to dying.

"One hundred marks," a clerk eventually said during one discussion.

Ieuan stared.

"One hundred?"

The clerk looked at him.

"Marks."

"I know what marks are."

"Your face suggests otherwise."

Ieuan calculated without meaning to.

Years of wages.

Cattle.

Grain.

A roof.

A household.

Too much to imagine as coin.

Guillaume watched him.

"You think I carry it in my boot?"

His English was worse than Ieuan's French, so they met awkwardly between them.

Ieuan said, "Would save time."

Guillaume almost smiled.

Then his face tightened.

"My family will pay."

Ieuan heard the weight beneath the statement.

A hundred marks did not appear from nowhere because men had agreed on a number.

Land might be pledged.

Goods sold.

Kin involved.

Years shaped around repayment.

Ieuan thought of the woman whose cow he took in 1355.

Every gain in war had a house at the other end of it.

"It won't all come to you," Iorwerth told him later.

He was sitting up by then, which everyone treated as victory despite his temper becoming worse.

"I know."

"Do you?"

"Shares upward. Costs. Men who helped. Keeping him fed. Whatever else somebody invents."

"Good."

Ieuan glared.

"Stop saying good like Tad."

Iorwerth paused.

"I never met your father."

"Exactly."

"Then blame him for being sensible."

The full ransom would take time.

Guarantees.

Promises.

Payments in stages.

A recognised claim mattered before every coin arrived.

After obligations and costs, Ieuan's eventual benefit would be far less than the headline hundred marks.

Still enough.

Enough that men who had barely noticed his name before Poitiers now remembered it.

That was another kind of wealth.

---

The army moved south again.

Victory did not make the roads shorter.

Poitiers became a story behind them while the same practical problems resumed.

Horses needed feed.

Wounded men needed transport.

Prisoners needed guarding.

Equipment broke.

Ieuan found himself responsible for more people than before the battle.

Tomas still worked with his horse and gear, but now Ieuan also had Guillaume's custody to think about and Iorwerth's recovery whenever the wounded Welshman was moved.

One afternoon Guillaume watched Ieuan adjust a pack arrangement and said, "You were groom."

Ieuan turned.

"What?"

The French knight gestured toward the horse.

"Before. Horse man."

"Aye."

Guillaume nodded as if this explained something.

"You fight like horse man."

Ieuan frowned.

"What does that mean?"

Guillaume searched for words.

"You look where weight goes."

Ieuan stared at him.

Dafydd's voice crossed twenty years and a sea.

*Watch the beast before you move it.*

"My father taught me cattle."

Guillaume looked surprised.

"Knight?"

Ieuan laughed.

"No."

"Soldier?"

"No."

"What then?"

Ieuan looked at the road.

"My father."

Guillaume seemed to understand that answer better than the others.

They did not become friends.

Guillaume remained a prisoner whose ransom would change Ieuan's life.

Ieuan remained part of the army that had fought in Guillaume's country.

But hatred became impractical once men travelled enough miles together.

---

Iorwerth told the story of Poitiers badly.

That was Ieuan's opinion.

The first time Ieuan heard him give an account to a senior Warwick man, he almost interrupted three times.

"He stayed in the line," Iorwerth said.

Ieuan thought: *Because you would have killed me if I'd left it.*

"When the movement allowed, he came back for me with Robert."

Ieuan thought: *You were swearing too much to know who came.*

"He took the French knight cleanly and accepted surrender."

Ieuan thought: *I nearly stabbed him after he yielded.*

"Then got both of us clear."

Ieuan thought: *Robert did half the carrying and Guillaume carried more than he wanted.*

The senior man looked at Ieuan.

"Something wrong?"

Ieuan realised his face had been speaking.

"No."

Iorwerth said, "Bad liar."

The senior man smiled.

Ieuan hated them both.

The recommendations travelled through the military network the same way every other useful fact did.

Matthew de Soor's earlier connection explained where Ieuan had come from in service.

Warwick's men knew what he had done in 1355 and 1356.

The prisoner made his achievement visible in money.

The fact that Ieuan was from Glamorgan made one particular young lord notice.

---

Ieuan had seen Edward le Despenser before.

He had never properly spoken to him.

The first meaningful meeting happened after Poitiers, when the battle was already turning into claims, rewards and stories.

Edward was only months from his twenty-first birthday.

Ieuan found that fact strange every time he remembered it.

The lord of Glamorgan was younger than Iorwerth by nearly a decade and only about a year younger than Ieuan.

Yet the distance between them could have contained the whole Rhondda.

Ieuan entered expecting a short interview.

Edward looked at him for a moment.

"Ieuan ap Dafydd. Glynrhondda."

"Aye, my lord."

"Warwick's company."

"Aye."

"Through Matthew de Soor's people before that."

"Aye."

Edward looked down at a paper or note in front of him.

"You've answered three questions with one word."

Ieuan swallowed.

"Sorry, my lord."

"I didn't say it was wrong."

There was humour there.

Small.

Ieuan relaxed by less than a finger's width.

Edward asked about Poitiers.

Not for heroics.

For facts.

How Iorwerth was wounded.

How Guillaume yielded.

Who witnessed it.

How long Ieuan had served armed.

Then the questions changed.

Glynrhondda.

Cattle.

Routes.

Llantrisant.

Who administered what after the mortality.

Ieuan understood suddenly.

This was not a lord marvelling that a poor boy had become brave.

Edward was evaluating a man.

A Glamorgan man.

One who knew upland stock, estate work, military service and the routes between worlds that usually remained separate.

That understanding made Ieuan answer better.

Longer.

Honestly.

Edward finally said, "You'll return to Wales?"

Ieuan had not considered any other answer.

"Aye."

"Good."

The word struck him again.

Always that word.

---

Winter passed slowly.

Iorwerth healed slowly.

Guillaume's ransom arrangements moved more slowly than both.

Ieuan spent Christmas away from the Rhondda and found that more painful than he expected after so many years of travelling.

He was twenty-one.

He had slept in more places than he could count.

Still, on certain nights, he could close his eyes and know exactly where the little end ought to be.

In March 1357, Edward le Despenser reached the end of his long minority and began taking effective control of the inheritance that had been his in name since 1349.

Ieuan heard the news without understanding at first why it mattered to him.

Then a summons came.

Not royal.

Not grand enough to become a song.

Edward wanted him in Glamorgan service.

The offer, when it was explained, made Ieuan suspicious because all large offers did.

A life fee of **twenty pounds a year** in return for continued Despenser service.

Additional profitable responsibility tied to estate and pastoral holdings in Glamorgan — work that suited what Ieuan actually knew rather than gifting him a fantasy castle.

Military attendance when required.

His own ransom capital strengthening his ability to maintain horse, armour and household.

Ieuan stared at the numbers.

"Why?"

The man explaining them looked confused.

"Because Lord Despenser wishes to retain you."

"I know what it says."

"Then—"

"Why me?"

The man sighed.

"You have recommendations. You fought well. You are useful in Glamorgan. You know horses, estate routes and Welsh service. Must there be a prophecy?"

Ieuan almost laughed.

"No."

"Good. Sign—"

The man stopped.

Ieuan raised his brows.

"I can't write."

"Yes. I remembered at exactly the wrong moment."

Arrangements were made without requiring Ieuan suddenly to become literate for the convenience of the scene.

The world continued to be less romantic than stories promised.

He preferred it that way.

---

The knighthood was the part he had not expected.

At first Ieuan thought he misunderstood.

Edward spoke plainly.

"Your service at Poitiers has been recommended beyond the ordinary. You have means now to maintain the rank. I intend to dub you."

Ieuan stared.

He had learned several languages badly enough to survive military life.

None offered him an answer.

"My lord—"

Edward waited.

Ieuan's bad lying habit saved him by making any invented modesty impossible.

"I don't know what to say."

"That may be the first reassuring thing I've heard about you."

Ieuan looked at him.

Edward smiled.

Then became serious.

"This is not charity."

Ieuan's spine stiffened.

"I didn't—"

"I know. I am telling you because other men will say it."

Ieuan said nothing.

"You were not born to the rank."

"No."

"You have served into it. Whether every gentleman in Glamorgan enjoys that fact is not my concern."

Ieuan thought of Robert asking whether stable boys were being dressed as gentlemen.

"No, my lord."

Edward looked at him.

"You may use more than one word now."

Ieuan laughed despite himself.

"I'll try."

---

The ceremony was quieter than battle.

That surprised him.

Ieuan had imagined knighthood as noise because everything associated with knights in his life had been noisy.

Horses.

Armour.

Banners.

Orders.

Steel.

The actual preparation felt controlled.

Religious observance and confession mattered.

Spurs.

A sword girded as part of the new rank.

Witnesses.

Men who understood the social meaning far better than Ieuan did while standing inside it.

He wore good clothing that felt less natural than armour.

That annoyed him.

Before entering, he checked his bedding from the night before.

Dry.

He laughed quietly at himself.

Twenty-two years old.

About to be knighted.

Still checking.

Dafydd's little knife rested among his personal things.

Ieuan picked it up.

For a moment he considered leaving it.

A knight had a sword now.

Then the thought offended him.

He put the knife back at his belt where it belonged.

Not as a relic.

As a knife.

The accolade itself passed almost too quickly.

Ieuan knelt before Edward le Despenser.

The sword came down in the formal act of dubbing.

The words spoken around it would blur in Ieuan's memory afterward.

What remained sharp was physical.

Knees against the floor.

Weight of the sword.

The strange pressure of everyone watching.

Then the moment he was told to stand.

Ieuan rose.

Someone addressed him differently.

"Sir Ieuan."

He nearly looked behind himself.

A few men smiled because they saw it.

Ieuan felt heat climb his neck.

Twenty-two years old and still capable of blushing like the boy Gruffudd once mocked outside church.

Edward extended a hand.

Ieuan took it.

"You look disappointed," Edward said quietly.

"No, my lord."

"Terrified?"

Ieuan thought about the answer.

"A little."

"Good."

Ieuan laughed.

Of course.

Good.

---

He returned to the Rhondda in summer.

Not immediately.

Knighthood did not release him from paperwork, obligations, estate arrangements, horses, money or men who wanted things signed by someone who could not sign them.

Eventually the road turned familiar.

Then the mountains did.

Ieuan rode a good horse.

A horse worth more than everything Hendre Gelli had once contained, if childhood memory could be trusted.

His armour travelled with him.

His sword hung at his side.

Men now called him *sir* without irony.

The valley did not care.

That comforted him.

The Rhondda Fawr lay below exactly where it always had.

Green slopes.

Woodland.

Water moving through the bottom.

Smoke from holdings that had survived, returned or been built into new lives.

He rode first toward Hendre Gelli.

Stopped above it.

The place had changed again.

People lived there.

Children's voices came from near the yard.

Ieuan could have announced himself.

He did not.

The house had not waited for him.

It should not have.

He turned uphill.

The final climb he made partly on foot because the ground was easier that way and because old habit demanded it.

He led the horse toward Gwenllian's grave.

The stones were still there.

Ieuan stood beside them.

For a long time he said nothing.

What could he tell an eight-year-old about eight years?

France.

Fire.

A king captured.

A French knight who paid not to remain a prisoner.

A lord who had made her brother a knight.

None of it sounded more important than the wooden animal buried beneath those stones.

Ieuan crouched and reset one loose rock.

"I'm back."

The words were enough.

His horse shifted behind him.

Ieuan glanced over.

The animal's ears had turned toward something lower on the slope.

A sheep perhaps.

Movement in brush.

Ieuan watched.

Waited.

Then smiled.

"Aye," he said softly. "I see it."

He stood.

From far below, a church bell carried through the valley.

Once.

Then again.

Ieuan did not know immediately why it rang.

A service.

A death.

A marriage.

Some ordinary reason belonging to people whose lives were continuing.

At fourteen, he had heard a bell and kept eating because he believed the worst thing in his world was wet straw beneath him.

At twenty-two, Sir Ieuan ap Dafydd stood on the mountain with a sword at his side, his father's little knife at his belt and his sister beneath the earth.

The bell rang again.

This time, he listened.
