# Chapter Four — Under the Arm

**23–28 May 1349**

Dafydd worked through the morning because Dafydd always worked through the morning.

That was what frightened Ieuan.

If his father had collapsed in the yard, if he had cried out or fallen senseless, there would have been no argument about what was happening. A thing that dramatic would have forced everyone to stop pretending.

Instead Dafydd moved more slowly.

He took longer over the gate.

He rested one hand against the wall while drinking.

When Angharad asked if he was cold, he told her the morning was cold.

When she asked if he was hot, he told her he had been working.

When Ieuan stared too long at his left arm, Dafydd said, "If you keep looking at me like that I'll charge you rent."

Ieuan did not laugh.

"Let Mam see it."

"There is nothing to see."

"Then she can look at nothing."

Dafydd's mouth tightened.

"Go check the upper gate."

"I checked it yesterday."

"Then it will be very impressed when you check it again."

"Tad—"

"Ieuan."

That tone had always ended things.

This time it did not.

Ieuan stood where he was.

For several breaths father and son looked at one another.

Dafydd had won every version of this contest for as long as Ieuan could remember.

Then Angharad came out of the house, took one look at them and said, "Inside."

Dafydd did not move.

"I have work."

"So do I. Mine is apparently getting you inside before you make me drag you."

"I'd like to see you try."

Angharad folded her arms.

Dafydd sighed.

"Traitor," he said to Ieuan.

Ieuan's relief was so sudden it almost felt like laughter.

He did not let it out.

Inside, Dafydd sat near the hearth.

Angharad touched his forehead.

Then his neck.

Then looked at him.

"Shirt."

"No."

"Dafydd."

"It's under my arm. You'll make it hurt."

The room went still.

Gwenllian, who had been winding thread around two fingers, stopped.

Ieuan forgot to breathe.

Angharad said, very quietly, "Show me."

Dafydd looked toward the door.

For a moment Ieuan thought he would simply leave.

Then his father pulled the cloth aside enough for Angharad to see.

Ieuan saw too.

The swelling sat high beneath the arm, red and stretched, larger than anything he had allowed himself to imagine when Madog was ill.

Dafydd covered it immediately.

"It's from lifting."

Nobody answered.

"Could be."

Angharad's voice was careful.

That scared Ieuan more than if she had cried.

Dafydd looked at her.

"Could be."

She held his gaze.

"Aye."

Gwenllian whispered, "Is it the sickness?"

Ieuan said, "No."

Too quickly.

Everyone looked at him.

He hated them for it.

"It might not be," he added.

Angharad closed her eyes for half a second.

Then she began giving orders.

Water.

Clean cloth.

More fuel.

Gwenllian to fetch a vessel and then stay out of the way.

Ieuan to move the cattle because they still needed moving.

"I'm staying."

"You're not."

"He might need—"

"The cattle need."

"Mam—"

"Do you think fever empties the troughs for us?"

He stared at her.

She softened only slightly.

"Go."

Ieuan went.

He hated every step away from the house.

---

The first job Dafydd usually did was simple.

Ieuan ruined it.

He moved the red cow too soon and had to bring her back around because he had forgotten the calf. One of the smaller cattle pressed through a weak point in the temporary fence and he spent twenty minutes swearing under his breath while trying to turn it without sending the rest after it.

When he finally had them where they belonged, he stood in the pasture breathing hard.

"Watch the beast before you move it," he muttered.

The words made him angry.

He knew them.

Knowing did not make one pair of hands into two.

He checked the water.

The lower vessel wanted filling.

He filled it.

Then remembered the firewood Angharad had asked for.

He left the cattle.

Halfway down he realised he had not secured the gate properly.

He ran back.

It was still shut.

He tied it.

Then stood with his hands on his knees, furious at the gate for requiring him to exist in two places at once.

When he returned to Hendre Gelli, Dafydd was lying down.

That stopped Ieuan in the doorway.

His father did not lie down during the day.

Not unless he had been working in the upper ground since before dawn and even then he would call it sitting with his eyes shut.

Now he lay on the family's warmer bedding, face turned toward the wall.

Angharad wrung out a cloth.

"Did you fill the lower water?"

"Aye."

"Gate?"

"Aye."

"The red cow?"

"Done."

"Good."

Ieuan looked at Dafydd.

"Is he asleep?"

"Sometimes."

"What does that mean?"

"It means sometimes he's asleep."

"Mam."

She looked at him.

He saw how tired she already was.

The question died.

Gwenllian sat near the far wall, hugging her knees.

Ieuan sat beside her.

"He's going to be all right," he whispered.

She nodded.

He added, "The swelling could be from lifting."

She nodded again.

"Madog was older."

Gwenllian looked at him.

"You said it wasn't the sickness."

"It isn't."

"Then why are you saying all that?"

He had no answer.

"Because," he said finally, "you keep asking."

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"No I wasn't."

Ieuan stared at the floor.

Gwenllian leaned her shoulder against his.

They sat like that until Angharad told him to fetch more water.

---

By the next morning, Dafydd could no longer pretend.

The fever had taken the colour from his face and then put too much back into it. Sweat darkened his hair. The swelling hurt badly enough that even shifting his arm made him draw breath through his teeth.

Meurig came.

So did an older woman from farther down the valley who knew remedies and had already been in other sick houses.

She smelled of smoke and something bitter.

Ieuan did not know whether he trusted her.

He trusted anyone who walked through the door willingly.

She looked at Dafydd's arm and made a small sound.

"What?" Ieuan demanded.

Angharad said, "Don't."

"What did she mean?"

"Nothing."

"Everyone's always saying nothing."

The woman looked at him.

"It means it's bad, boy."

Angharad's mouth hardened.

Ieuan almost thanked the woman.

At least someone had said it.

They tried what could be tried.

Cloths.

Water.

Herbs Ieuan could not name even after being told.

Prayer.

Keeping Dafydd warm when he shook and cooler when the heat became unbearable.

Nothing stayed ahead of the fever for long.

Meurig heard his confession when Dafydd was clear enough.

Ieuan was sent outside for that.

He sat on a stone near the yard and dug at the earth with the tip of a stick until Gwenllian came out.

"What did Tad do?"

Ieuan looked at her.

"What?"

"He's confessing. What did he do?"

"Everyone confesses."

"But what did he do?"

Ieuan considered several possibilities.

"He stole a pig."

Gwenllian's eyes widened.

"When?"

"Before you were born."

"Whose pig?"

"The bishop's."

"The bishop has pigs?"

"Loads."

She stared at him.

Ieuan kept his face straight for as long as possible.

Then Gwenllian hit him with both hands.

"You're lying!"

"Quiet."

"You are!"

"I know."

"That's a sin."

"Then I'll confess."

For a few seconds she looked like herself again.

Then Dafydd cried out inside.

The smile vanished.

---

On Monday Ieuan took over nearly everything outside.

Not well.

Enough.

There was a difference.

He knew when the cattle needed moving.

He knew which fence would fail first.

He knew where the tools were and which were worth carrying uphill.

He did not know how long the grain had to last if nobody could go down safely.

He did not know how much fuel Angharad would need if Dafydd kept shaking.

He did not know whether to release one animal to graze farther if he could not manage them all.

He did not know when someone became too ill to drink.

Every time he entered the house, Dafydd looked smaller.

Late in the day, his father opened his eyes while Ieuan was tying a cord near the door.

"Wrong."

Ieuan turned.

"What?"

Dafydd lifted one hand weakly.

"That knot."

Ieuan looked down.

"It's fine."

"It'll pull loose."

"It won't."

Dafydd shut his eyes.

"Then leave it."

Ieuan stared at him.

Anger flashed so quickly he almost enjoyed it.

"You're lying there half dead and you're still telling me how to tie a bloody knot."

Angharad stopped beside the hearth.

Gwenllian's head snapped up.

Dafydd opened his eyes again.

Ieuan wanted the words back.

He wanted them gone before his father had properly heard them.

Instead Dafydd said, "Aye."

Ieuan swallowed.

"I didn't mean—"

"Fix it."

He looked down.

The knot was wrong.

He untied it and did it again.

When he finished, Dafydd watched.

Ieuan pulled hard.

It held.

His father gave the smallest nod.

Then closed his eyes.

Ieuan stood there for a long time.

That night he wet the bed.

---

He woke before dawn and knew immediately.

For a few seconds the old panic returned complete and stupid.

Wet wool.

Wet straw.

Tad finding out.

Mam seeing.

Gwenllian laughing.

Then he heard Dafydd breathing in the main room.

A rough, uneven sound.

Everything else became small.

Ieuan sat up.

Angharad was awake beside his father.

She did not turn when Ieuan moved.

Normally she would have known.

Normally she seemed able to hear wet straw from another county.

This time she did not notice at all.

Ieuan gathered the bedding himself.

The straw came away in dark clumps.

He carried it outside.

He washed the cloth as best he could in water so cold it made his hands ache.

He found clean straw.

He put everything back.

Nobody helped.

Halfway through, he looked toward the main room and felt a hot surge of resentment.

Mam should have helped.

The thought was so childish that he hated himself immediately.

Angharad had not slept.

Her shoulders were bowed. One hand rested against Dafydd's chest, not tenderly but as if measuring whether it still moved.

Ieuan looked away.

He finished the bedding.

That was the morning the little end stopped feeling like a punishment.

It became simply another job he had to manage.

---

Dafydd worsened through Tuesday.

He spoke to people who were not there.

Once he told Ieuan to bring in a tool that had been lost two winters earlier.

Once he called Gwenllian by Angharad's name.

Once he began explaining how to mend a fence none of them recognised.

Then, near evening, he became clear.

So suddenly that Ieuan thought the fever had broken.

Dafydd looked around the room.

At Angharad.

At Gwenllian asleep near the wall.

At Ieuan crouched beside a split leather strap.

"What are you doing?"

Ieuan almost laughed.

"Mending it."

"Badly."

"I haven't finished."

"You've cut that too short."

Ieuan looked.

He had.

"It'll reach."

"No."

"It will."

Dafydd's eyes held his.

Ieuan stopped.

He laid the strap down.

"What then?"

His father took a breath.

"Use the old piece from the red halter. Not the rotten end. Other end."

Ieuan went to the storage place.

The red halter was there.

He cut the piece.

It fit.

He looked toward Dafydd.

His father nodded.

"Better."

Ieuan sat beside him.

He waited for something else.

A speech.

A blessing.

An instruction to look after Mam and Gwenllian.

Something a dying father ought to say because stories always made dying people suddenly understand which words would matter forever.

Dafydd only said, "Did you water them?"

Ieuan's throat tightened.

"Aye."

"All of them?"

"Aye."

"Good."

His eyes closed again.

That was the last completely sensible conversation Ieuan had with his father.

---

Dafydd died on Wednesday morning.

No bell rang at the exact moment.

No light changed.

Nothing in the valley marked it.

He breathed.

Then he did not.

Angharad sat beside him with both hands around one of his.

Ieuan stood near the foot of the bedding and waited for the next breath because the gap between breaths had become long before.

He waited.

Angharad lowered her head.

Ieuan understood.

Gwenllian did not.

"Mam?"

Angharad did not answer.

"Mam?"

Ieuan said, "He's dead."

The words came out flat.

Gwenllian stared at him as though he had done something cruel.

"Don't say it like that."

Ieuan looked at his father's body.

He did not know another way to say it.

For the next several hours he became very useful.

Useful was easier than being a son.

He fetched cloth.

He found someone willing to help.

He moved animals.

He carried messages.

He asked whether Meurig could come and was told the priest was already elsewhere with another sick household.

He found a neighbour who would help prepare the body but would not enter the house for long.

He took Dafydd's knife from where it lay because cord needed cutting.

He used it.

He put it in his own belt without thinking.

Nobody told him not to.

That evening, when he reached for it again, he realised it was still there.

He nearly took it out.

Then left it.

---

They buried Dafydd the next day.

Ieuan had imagined graves before.

He had never imagined the work.

Earth was heavy.

A body was heavier.

A man who had lifted sacks, held animals, repaired roofs and seemed to take up exactly the proper amount of space in every room became an impossible weight once he could no longer help carry himself.

There were fewer people than there should have been.

Meurig came late and looked grey with tiredness.

The prayers were shortened because another house needed him.

Ieuan resented that.

Then felt guilty.

Then resented having to feel guilty.

When the first earth struck the wrapped body, Gwenllian made a small choking sound.

Angharad put an arm around her.

Ieuan kept shovelling.

He did not stop until someone took the tool from his hand.

"Enough," the man said.

Ieuan looked at the grave.

It was not enough.

Nothing about it was enough.

But the earth reached the proper height eventually.

People left.

Ieuan stood until Angharad called him.

On the walk home, Dafydd's knife knocked against his hip.

He noticed every step.

At Hendre Gelli there was still work.

Of course there was.

The cattle had not attended the burial.

The fire had not mourned.

A pot still needed filling.

Ieuan walked into the house and saw the place where Dafydd had lain.

For one strange instant he expected his father to tell him what he had forgotten.

Angharad moved past him.

Then stopped.

Her shoulders jerked once.

Ieuan thought she was crying.

She was not.

She wrapped both arms around herself.

"Mam?"

"I'm tired."

Ieuan stared at her.

Angharad shivered again.

The room was warm.
