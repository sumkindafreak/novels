# Chapter Fourteen — The Return

**November–2 December 1355**

The return began in darkness.

That became the shape of November.

Wake before light.

Check horse.

Check tack.

Move.

Eat while moving if food existed.

Stop only long enough for the animals to drink, for men to piss, for a strap to be tied, for somebody important to decide where the next stretch ended.

Then move again.

Daylight shortened around them almost visibly.

At Narbonne there had still been enough afternoon to believe a day's work might finish before dark.

By late November, light felt borrowed.

The road consumed it.

Ieuan's hands cracked at the knuckles.

His legs ached from riding and walking interchangeably.

Wet leather never properly dried.

Men became quicker to anger because tiredness made every inconvenience feel personal.

Horses stopped hiding what they thought of the campaign.

Brân bit Robert.

Ieuan regarded that as sound judgement.

"Your bloody horse!" Robert shouted.

"He isn't mine."

"Then why does he only listen to you?"

"Taste."

Robert raised a hand.

Iorwerth said, "If you two fight, I'm giving the winner both night watches."

They stopped.

That was discipline.

Not honour.

Fear of extra work.

---

Three days later, Ieuan saw a horse was finished.

The animal belonged to a better-born armed servant whose name Ieuan knew and did not like.

A chestnut gelding.

Strong at the start.

Now dull.

Its head hung lower than it should. The gait had shortened. One foreleg carried heat near the fetlock, and the horse had stopped eating properly at the previous halt.

Ieuan watched for half a mile.

Then rode up.

"You need to lighten him."

The rider looked at him.

"What?"

"Take the pack off the back. Move it to another animal."

"He's fine."

"He's not."

"He walked all morning."

"Aye. Badly."

The man's expression hardened.

"You telling me how to ride my horse?"

Ieuan swallowed the first answer.

That was progress.

"I'm telling you if you keep the load, you may not have one by tomorrow."

"He'll manage."

Iorwerth appeared behind Ieuan.

"Will he?"

The man turned.

"The Welsh groom thinks he's dying."

Ieuan's face heated at *groom*.

Iorwerth ignored it.

"Ieuan?"

"Not dying. Finished with that load. Heat in the leg. Not eating. Going short."

Iorwerth dismounted.

Checked.

The rider complained.

Iorwerth said, "Move the load."

"We don't have space."

"Then lose the gear or lose the horse."

The gear moved.

The chestnut lasted.

At the next halt, the rider passed Ieuan without speaking.

Then stopped.

"He ate."

Ieuan looked up.

"Good."

The man walked on.

That was the whole apology.

Ieuan accepted it.

---

The army was being shadowed by then.

Everyone knew.

Not always seeing the enemy made it worse.

Rumours moved along the column faster than horses could.

French ahead.

French behind.

A crossing threatened.

Stragglers taken.

A small party hit.

Most reports were wrong by the time they reached Ieuan.

Enough were true.

Men kept weapons closer.

Orders became sharper.

The baggage and remount groups stopped feeling like the safer part of the army.

They felt like meat hanging behind a cart.

Late in the month, on a narrow stretch of poor ground, one exhausted horse went down in front of them.

It did not collapse neatly.

Nothing ever did.

The animal stumbled, tried to recover, turned sideways and fell across the route with a pack dragging beneath it.

The horse behind panicked.

Then the next.

Men shouted.

Someone tried to pull the fallen animal forward.

Ieuan saw at once what would happen.

"Stop!"

Nobody did.

"Stop pulling him!"

The lead man turned.

"Get him up!"

"You won't. Not with the pack under him. Move the rear line off!"

"Where?"

Ieuan pointed toward a patch of firmer shoulder beside the track.

"There. Two at a time. Unload the bay first."

A servant hesitated.

"Do it!"

This time people listened.

There was no time to ask why a tenant boy from Glynrhondda was giving orders.

Ieuan slid from Brân and reached the fallen horse.

The animal's eye rolled white.

"Easy."

Meaningless word.

Useful tone.

He got the pack straps free.

Another man pulled the load clear.

Behind them, hoofbeats sounded where there should not have been hoofbeats.

Iorwerth shouted something.

Men turned.

An alarm moved through the escort.

Mounted figures came hard from the rear and side, not a great army, not banners filling the horizon.

A small violent opportunity.

Exactly the kind men waited for when a large column stretched thin.

Ieuan's first instinct was the horses.

Get them moving.

Get them clear.

He grabbed Brân's rein.

"Ieuan!"

Iorwerth's voice.

Ieuan looked.

"Hold here!"

"The remounts—"

"Lower men move them. Hold!"

Every part of Ieuan built in 1349 screamed to take what mattered and get away.

Survive first.

Move.

Don't wait for somebody else to save you.

Iorwerth had spent years teaching him something harder.

Stand where you're told when leaving puts someone else in danger.

Ieuan shoved the rein into a younger servant's hand.

"Take him. Follow the grey. Don't let them bunch."

The boy stared.

"Go!"

He went.

Ieuan took his spear.

The attack reached them.

---

Later, Ieuan could not remember the man's face.

That troubled him.

He remembered a brown sleeve.

A horse's chest slamming sideways.

Mud.

Someone shouting in French.

The weight of his spear.

The attacker came through a gap toward the men trying to clear the last pack animals.

Ieuan stepped because training told him where to stand.

Not because he was brave.

There was no time for bravery.

The man struck at him.

Ieuan moved badly but enough.

His own weapon went forward.

Resistance.

A horrible moment where the spear stopped against something living.

Then went farther.

The man's sound was smaller than Ieuan expected.

That was what stayed.

Not a scream.

A breath.

The attacker fell.

Ieuan nearly followed the spear down because he had not released properly.

Someone struck his shoulder.

He turned.

Another man.

Then Iorwerth was there.

Steel.

Noise.

The next few seconds became pieces without order.

A retreating horse.

Robert bleeding from the eyebrow.

A man on the ground trying to crawl.

Ieuan's hand slick on the shaft.

Then the attackers broke away.

Some men chased.

Ieuan took two steps after them.

Stopped.

The horses.

He turned back.

The fallen animal still blocked half the route.

One pack lay open.

A remount had twisted its reins around a branch.

Ieuan went to work.

"Leave him!" someone shouted, meaning the dead man.

Ieuan had not realised he was still looking.

He dragged the remaining pack free.

Checked the fallen horse.

Broken leg.

Nothing to do.

He knew what came next.

Another man did it.

Ieuan was grateful for that and ashamed of the gratitude.

They got the remounts moving.

Only then did he notice Iorwerth watching him.

"What?"

Iorwerth wiped his sword.

"Nothing."

"You're looking."

"You've said that since you were seventeen."

Ieuan looked toward the animals.

"We moving?"

"Aye."

"Then move."

Iorwerth smiled once.

They moved.

---

That night Ieuan tried to clean the spear.

He had cleaned weapons before.

Blood was not new.

Animal blood.

Old stains on equipment.

Men bleeding after small fights.

This was different because he knew exactly why the blood was there.

His hand.

His movement.

A man alive.

Then not.

Ieuan scrubbed too hard.

The cloth tore.

He swore.

Iorwerth sat nearby eating.

"You'll take the finish off before the blood."

Ieuan stopped.

"How do you eat?"

Iorwerth chewed.

"With difficulty when people ask questions."

"You know what I mean."

Iorwerth looked at the spear.

"First?"

Ieuan nodded.

"Aye."

"Did you remember yours?"

"Some of it."

"Did it stop bothering you?"

Iorwerth considered.

"It changed."

Ieuan waited.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

Iorwerth put the food down.

"If you feel nothing, I'd worry. If you decide feeling means you cannot stand next time, I'd worry more."

Ieuan looked at the weapon.

"He might've had children."

"Likely."

"Wife."

"Likely."

Ieuan stared.

"You're not helping."

"I'm not going to tell you he was a monster so you sleep better. He was trying to kill you or get through you. You stopped him. Tomorrow there may be another."

Ieuan's stomach twisted.

"That's war?"

"Part of it."

Ieuan thought of the girl near Carcassonne.

The cow taken from the woman.

The fire.

The dead man.

"I thought it'd feel different."

Iorwerth picked up his food again.

"Everyone does."

Ieuan cleaned the spear.

Then ate because morning would come whether he deserved appetite or not.

---

The incident travelled farther than Ieuan expected.

Not the killing.

The horses.

Men talked about how the remount group had nearly jammed and how the Welsh horse servant had cleared it while the escort fought.

Ieuan found this insulting.

He had killed a man and people were impressed by pack order.

Then he understood.

Men killed in war.

Not everyone kept expensive military animals from being lost while doing it.

A senior retainer asked him what happened.

Ieuan answered.

Another asked the next day.

Then someone told him he would stand armed watch that night instead of helping with the usual feed.

"Who's doing the horses?" Ieuan asked.

"Someone else."

He did not like that answer.

At another halt, a younger servant was told to take Brân while Ieuan reported to the escort.

Ieuan almost corrected him three times.

He stopped himself twice.

The third time the boy had actually tied the rein badly.

---

On **2 December**, they reached the relative safety of La Réole and the winter phase began.

Ieuan had imagined relief.

Instead there was inventory.

Horses needing shoes.

Horses needing rest.

Lost equipment.

Men owed wages.

Repairs.

Orders.

Garrisons.

The endless afterlife of a campaign.

Ieuan went automatically toward the horse lines on the first morning.

A younger servant blocked him.

"I was told to do these."

Ieuan frowned.

"By who?"

The boy named a senior man.

"They're Brân and the two greys."

"I know."

"You don't know the grey on the left rubs if—"

"I know."

Ieuan stared.

From behind, Iorwerth said, "If you're finished fighting a fifteen-year-old over horse shit, you're wanted on the armed watch."

Ieuan turned.

"Why?"

"Because somebody has made a terrible mistake."

"What mistake?"

Iorwerth walked away.

"Thinking you're ready for responsibility."

Ieuan looked back at the horses.

For years, advancement had been something he chased.

Now it appeared to be happening without asking him.
