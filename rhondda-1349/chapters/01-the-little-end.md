# Chapter One — The Little End

**2 April 1349**

Ieuan knew before he opened his eyes.

He always knew.

There was a particular warmth beneath him that no blanket ever made, followed almost at once by the cold creeping in around it. By the time he was properly awake the warmth had gone altogether, leaving damp wool against his thigh and the sour smell rising from the straw beneath his hip.

For a few breaths he lay absolutely still.

The house was dark.

The fire in the main room had fallen low during the night, but a dull red glow reached through the gap beyond the hanging cloth that separated Ieuan's sleeping place from the rest of the house. The little end was always colder. Wind found its way beneath the eaves and through cracks that nobody seemed able to stop for more than a week, and the packed floor held the night's damp as stubbornly as the hillside outside.

No one moved.

Ieuan slid one hand beneath the blanket.

Wet.

He shut his eyes again.

Fourteen years old.

He was fourteen years old and had pissed himself like Gwenllian used to when she was little.

Carefully, he pushed the blanket away.

The straw whispered.

He froze.

Nothing from the main room.

He tried again, moving one handful at a time. The worst of it had clumped beneath him. He gathered the damp straw against his chest, then immediately regretted it when the smell came stronger.

If he could get it outside before Tad woke, perhaps—

"You're making more noise trying to be quiet than you would if you just stood up."

Ieuan almost dropped the straw.

His mother stood on the other side of the hanging cloth.

Angharad had wrapped a woollen covering around her shoulders and her hair hung loose down one side of her face. She did not look angry. That somehow made it worse.

"I was only—"

"I can see what you were only doing."

"It isn't much."

She looked at the bundle in his arms.

"No. Hardly enough to make the whole house smell of roses. Take it out."

Ieuan glared at her.

She had already turned away.

That was Mam's way of saving him from things: refusing to look as though there was anything worth saving him from.

He shoved his feet into his shoes, lifted the wet straw and slipped through the outer door.

The morning had not properly arrived.

A grey light sat behind the eastern ridge, barely enough to separate mountain from sky. The ground around Hendre Gelli was slick from rain, and the cold struck Ieuan through his thin sleeping shirt before he had taken three steps. Somewhere close by, one of the cattle shifted heavily and blew through its nose.

Ieuan carried the straw away from the house and threw it onto the waste heap.

He stood there a moment longer than necessary.

The valley below was mostly darkness. A few distant roofs and holdings could be guessed from their shapes if he knew where to look. Farther down, the river was invisible but not silent, a low rush beneath everything else.

He rubbed his hands on his shirt and went back inside.

Angharad had coaxed the hearth into life. She pointed with her chin toward a small bundle of dry straw she had set aside.

"Put that down before your father wakes."

"He doesn't need to know."

"Then stop saying it loudly."

Ieuan picked up the clean straw.

A small voice came from the warmer sleeping space.

"I already know."

He turned.

Gwenllian's face appeared above the edge of the bedding, hair everywhere and one cheek marked with lines from the cloth beneath her.

"Go back to sleep."

"You wet again."

"No, I didn't."

"Mam's giving you straw."

"Because the old straw was bad."

Gwenllian smiled.

"Bad because you pissed on it."

Ieuan crossed the space in two steps.

"Shut your mouth."

Her smile vanished.

He knew at once he had gone too far.

That made him angrier.

"Ieuan." Angharad did not raise her voice.

"She started it."

"And you are twice her size. Put the straw down."

He returned to the little end and dropped it harder than necessary.

Behind him Gwenllian muttered something.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Say it."

"I said your face looks like a goat's arse."

Angharad made a sound that might have been a cough.

Ieuan spun around.

Gwenllian was grinning again.

Then Tad's voice came from the main room.

"If the two of you have finished deciding which end of a goat your brother resembles, there's work outside."

Ieuan's stomach dropped.

Dafydd was sitting up.

His father looked first at Ieuan, then at the fresh straw, then at the dark bundle Angharad had laid near the hearth to dry later.

Nothing moved in his face for a moment.

Then he sighed.

"Again?"

Ieuan stared at the floor.

"It wasn't much."

"It is never much when you aren't the one fetching straw."

"I fetched it."

"Your mam fetched it. You carried it six steps."

Heat climbed Ieuan's neck.

Dafydd pulled on his tunic.

"Put the wet cloth where it can dry. Not too near the fire. Last time you nearly scorched it. Then eat. The red cow's been calling since before dawn."

That was all.

Ieuan hated him for not saying more.

He hated him for saying anything.

He hated Gwenllian for knowing.

Most of all he hated the stupid, treacherous body that did this while he slept and left him to answer for it in the morning.

He carried the cloth nearer the hearth.

"Not there," Angharad said.

He moved it.

"Too close."

He moved it again.

"There."

"I knew that."

"Of course you did."

Breakfast was not a thing anyone sat down to enjoy.

Angharad ladled thick grain from the pot into wooden bowls while Dafydd checked a length of cord near the door and complained that somebody had put a tool back wet. Gwenllian spent half her meal insisting she ought to be allowed to go farther up with the cattle because she was nearly nine, which she was not, and because Elen was allowed, which Ieuan said was also not true.

"Elen is thirteen," he said.

"So?"

"So you are eight."

"I'm nearly nine."

"You're not nearly anything."

"I'm nearer nine than seven."

Ieuan opened his mouth, then stopped.

Technically she was right.

Gwenllian saw the hesitation and smiled triumphantly into her bowl.

Dafydd slid the repaired cord across to Ieuan.

"When you've finished, take this. The brindled one pushed the upper gate again yesterday."

Ieuan forgot Gwenllian.

"On my own?"

Dafydd looked at him.

"No. I'll send the Bishop of Llandaff to help you."

Gwenllian snorted grain through her nose.

"I meant—"

"I know what you meant. Yes. On your own. Check the bank as well. If she's found one weak place she'll find another."

Ieuan lowered his eyes before his father could see how much the instruction pleased him.

"I can do it."

"That's why I told you."

There it was.

Not praise. Dafydd almost never praised anything that ought to have been done properly in the first place.

But he had told Ieuan.

Not Mam. Not a neighbour. Not himself.

Ieuan.

He ate faster.

Outside, morning had spread properly across the valley by the time they reached the higher grazing.

The Rhondda Fawr ran below them, its sides dark with patches of woodland and new spring growth. The slopes around Hendre Gelli were familiar enough that Ieuan could have crossed some of them with his eyes half shut, though Dafydd would have called him an idiot for trying. Tracks curved around banks and wet ground rather than running straight at anything. Water appeared where water always appeared. Stones that looked loose were firm; stones that looked firm sometimes rolled beneath a careless foot.

That was simply knowing where you lived.

The red cow was calling again.

"She wants the calf," Ieuan said.

"She can want all she likes."

"We should put them together."

"Later."

"She'll keep calling."

"Then we'll know where she is."

Ieuan rolled his eyes when Dafydd turned away.

They reached the upper gate.

The brindled cow stood just beyond it, broad head lowered, staring at the opening as though someone had personally insulted her by putting a gate there.

"Move her through," Dafydd said.

Ieuan went around to her side and clapped his hands.

"Come on."

The cow did not move.

He stepped closer.

"Come."

Nothing.

He slapped her flank.

The cow swung her hindquarters toward him.

Ieuan jumped back.

Dafydd laughed once.

"Very good. Now you've made her angry as well as stubborn."

"She was already stubborn."

"Was she?"

"Look at her."

"I am looking. You aren't."

Ieuan frowned.

"I'm standing right here."

Dafydd came beside him.

"Stop trying to move her. Watch her first."

"I know what a cow looks like."

"Apparently not this one."

Ieuan folded his arms.

Dafydd waited.

That was another thing his father could do better than anyone: make silence feel like an instruction.

Ieuan looked again.

The cow's head was not pointed toward the gate. She kept shifting her attention uphill, ears turning toward something beyond the bank.

"She's looking at the calf."

"Aye."

"So move the calf."

"Where?"

Ieuan almost said *through the gate*.

Then he stopped.

He walked a few paces and looked at the line from the cow to the opening.

The calf was uphill and behind them. Every time Ieuan stepped toward the cow, he was placing himself between her and it.

He scratched his nose.

"If we bring the calf down there, she'll follow it through."

Dafydd nodded.

"Watch the beast before you move it."

Ieuan went for the calf.

The whole thing took less than a minute once he stopped fighting it.

When the brindled cow finally walked through the gate as if it had been her own idea all along, Ieuan looked back at his father.

Dafydd was cutting a frayed piece from the gate cord with his knife.

The knife was nothing special. Dark wooden handle, iron blade worn narrower than it must once have been, edge bright from sharpening. Ieuan had seen it open sacks, trim leather, cut food, clean mud from things that should not have had mud in them and perform a hundred other jobs no one remembered afterward.

Dafydd saw him looking.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Then take the calf back up."

Ieuan did.

They worked the rest of the morning on the gate and the bank beside it. Ieuan was halfway through driving a stake when he spotted someone coming along the lower track.

Madog ap Rhys walked with the steady, slightly forward lean of a man who had spent most of his life climbing slopes and saw no reason to hurry now. He was older than Dafydd by more than ten years, perhaps fifteen, though Ieuan had never asked. Asking adults their ages seemed pointless. Old was old.

Madog raised one hand.

"Dafydd."

"Madog."

Ieuan kept working while the two men talked, which was the best way to hear things adults might decide a boy did not need to hear.

"You've been down?" Dafydd asked.

"Llantrisant side. Not far."

"How is it?"

Madog shrugged.

"Wet."

Dafydd gave him a look.

"You climbed up here to tell me the ground is wet?"

"Thought you might have forgotten."

Ieuan smiled at the stake.

Madog spat into the grass.

"There's talk again."

Dafydd stopped pulling the cord.

"What talk?"

"The sickness."

Ieuan slowed his hammering.

Madog glanced at him, then apparently decided fourteen was old enough to have ears.

"A man I spoke with said there are houses east where no one is left to open the door. Says they've buried whole families."

Dafydd snorted softly.

"A man you spoke with."

"Aye."

"Who spoke with another man?"

"Probably."

"And that one spoke with the Virgin herself."

Madog smiled, showing the gaps where two teeth were missing.

"Perhaps she has better news."

Dafydd pulled the knot tight.

"People die every spring."

"Not like he's saying."

"People say many things when they want listening to."

Madog looked down the valley.

"He said priests are dying too."

That quietened Dafydd.

Ieuan stopped hammering completely.

Priests dying was different somehow.

He could not have explained why. Priests were men. Meurig coughed, complained of cold mornings and got mud on his shoes like anyone else. But priests stood at the places where people entered and left the world. At baptisms. At marriages. At graves.

If they died too, who stood there then?

Madog rubbed his jaw.

"Nest says it's judgement."

"Nest says rain is judgement if the washing is out."

"She says that too."

Dafydd went back to the gate.

"If it comes here, then we deal with what comes here. Until then I've got cattle that don't know they're meant to fear God's wrath."

Madog nodded.

"That's about what I thought."

The men moved on to talk about an animal Madog wanted Dafydd to look at later.

Ieuan resumed hammering.

The sickness already felt less important.

Something else had occurred to him.

Madog's daughter Elen was thirteen.

Elen spoke to Gruffudd ap Ieuan.

Gwenllian spoke to Elen.

Gwenllian knew about the bedding.

Ieuan hit the stake too hard and split the top.

Dafydd looked over.

"Planning to drive it through to hell?"

"It was rotten."

"It wasn't."

"It split."

"Because you hit it like it owed you money."

Madog laughed.

Ieuan's face burned.

For the next hour, pestilence vanished completely from his mind.

By afternoon Dafydd had gone down toward Madog's holding, leaving Ieuan with a list of jobs and the solemn instruction not to invent any more.

Ieuan repaired a section of cord near the yard, checked the poultry water because Gwenllian said Mam had asked him twice, and then discovered the cord he needed had tangled beneath a bundle of old harness.

He pulled at it.

It tightened.

He pulled harder.

"Watch the beast," Gwenllian said from behind him.

Ieuan turned slowly.

She stood holding a small basket, looking unbearably pleased with herself.

"It's rope."

"Looks frightened."

"Go away."

"Mam said bring these in."

"Then bring them in."

"I am."

"Good."

"Your rope's winning."

Ieuan yanked at it again.

Nothing.

He needed a knife.

Dafydd's lay on the wooden work surface where he had left it after trimming cord earlier.

Ieuan hesitated only because he knew Tad noticed when anything was moved.

Then he picked it up.

The handle was smooth where years of use had worn it.

The blade slid beneath the snarled strand and cut it cleanly.

Gwenllian peered into her basket.

"Mam wanted the small pot too."

"No, she didn't."

"She did."

"She said basket."

"She said basket and small pot."

"I was standing there."

"You were sulking about your straw."

Ieuan glared at her.

Gwenllian looked suddenly wary.

The morning flashed between them.

Ieuan looked away first.

"Which pot?"

She pointed.

It was exactly where he had walked past it twice.

He grabbed it.

"Don't tell Elen."

Gwenllian blinked.

"Tell her what?"

"You know."

"About the pot?"

"About this morning."

She made a face.

"Why would I tell Elen you pissed your bed?"

Ieuan hissed at her to lower her voice.

"Because you tell everyone everything."

"No, I don't."

"You told Nest Mam burnt the onions."

"She asked why they tasted burnt."

"You told Madog Tad fell in the stream."

"He did fall in the stream."

"Gwenllian."

She shifted the basket against her hip.

"I won't tell."

He studied her.

"Promise?"

"Yes."

"On—"

"I said I won't."

There was real annoyance in her voice now.

Ieuan almost smiled.

"All right."

She started toward the house.

"Goat's arse."

He threw a piece of cut cord at her.

She squealed and ran.

Ieuan laughed despite himself, then quickly looked around to make sure no one had heard why.

He cleaned Dafydd's knife on his sleeve, folded it shut and placed it exactly where he had found it.

By evening the clouds had lowered again.

Rain began as a soft tapping on the thatch and thickened until water ran from the roof edge in shining threads. The cattle had settled. Gwenllian sat close to Angharad and picked at a loose thread in her sleeve until Mam slapped her fingers away for the third time. Dafydd worked a leather strap near the fire.

Ieuan's damp cloth hung well back from the flames.

Nobody had mentioned it for hours.

That should have made him feel better.

Instead he was thinking about tomorrow night.

Would it happen again?

Would he wake before anyone?

What if Gwenllian forgot her promise?

What if Gruffudd found out?

He imagined Gruffudd saying it once near Ystradyfodwg and everyone knowing by midday.

He imagined Elen laughing.

He imagined Tad hearing somebody outside the family use it against him.

That was worst of all.

Dafydd looked up.

"You've been staring at the same piece of bread long enough to grow another crop from it. Eat."

Ieuan ate.

Somewhere beyond the rain, faint at first and then clearer as the wind shifted, a bell sounded down the valley.

One stroke.

Then another.

Ieuan barely lifted his head.

Church bells rang. That was what bells did.

Angharad paused in winding a length of thread around her fingers, listening long enough to decide whether the sound required anything of them.

Apparently it did not.

Dafydd went on working the strap.

Gwenllian leaned into Mam's side.

Ieuan reached for another piece of bread.

The bell rang again.

He kept eating.
