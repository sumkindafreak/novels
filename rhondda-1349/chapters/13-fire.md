# Chapter Thirteen — Fire

**5 October–8 November 1355**

For the first two days, war looked like marching.

Ieuan almost found that reassuring.

He understood marching.

Horses needed water whether a prince rode ahead of them or not. Loads shifted. Straps rubbed. Men lied about how far they could go without rest. Pack animals found mud as reliably in France as they did in Glamorgan.

The army stretched along roads in a moving order Ieuan was still learning to read.

Men-at-arms.

Archers.

Servants.

Baggage.

Remounts.

Animals carrying more wealth than Hendre Gelli had ever held.

At twenty, Ieuan was proud to be there.

He knew that later he would be ashamed of the pride.

At the time, he felt it plainly.

He rode with armed household servants now, though nobody mistook him for a knight. His spear was real. His helmet was not decorative. Men expected him to hold if trouble came.

That mattered.

Iorwerth noticed the way Ieuan kept looking at the larger divisions ahead.

"You'll fall off if you stare at banners instead of your horse."

Ieuan looked down at Brân.

"He knows where he's going."

"So do geese. Nobody gives them armour."

Ieuan smiled.

"You jealous?"

"Of a goose? Frequently."

The column moved on.

By the third day, the orders changed.

Not dramatically.

A few words.

A different urgency.

Men spread toward holdings along the route.

Livestock was to be taken.

Food collected.

Anything useful brought into the column.

What could not be carried might be destroyed.

Ieuan heard the instructions and understood every practical part of them.

That was the problem.

---

The first house they emptied was not grand.

That made it worse.

Stone and timber.

A yard.

Two cows.

A stack of grain.

A small cart.

Bedding rolled against a wall.

A wooden bowl on a table.

Ieuan stopped in the doorway.

For one second he was fourteen again.

Madog's house.

Nest's rope.

Mam's hidden cheese.

The moral weight of taking a sack because nobody answered.

Here, people answered.

A woman stood outside with two children while armed men searched the holding.

Her face was white with fury.

Not helplessness.

Fury.

One child stared at Ieuan.

A boy perhaps ten.

Old enough to understand they were being robbed.

Ieuan looked away first.

"Take the dun cow," a man called.

Ieuan moved.

The cow resisted the unfamiliar men.

Of course she did.

He watched her head.

Read the weight.

Moved her without striking.

The woman spat something at him in French too quick for him to catch.

He understood the tone.

Ieuan did not answer.

He took her cow.

---

That night, he ate food taken from people whose names he did not know.

Nobody forced him to.

That mattered too.

He could have refused and been hungry.

He ate.

Iorwerth sat near the fire repairing a strap.

Ieuan stared at the bread in his hand.

"You going to apologise to it?" Iorwerth asked.

Ieuan looked up.

"What?"

"You've been staring at that piece for five minutes."

Ieuan tore it and ate.

Iorwerth said nothing more.

Ieuan was grateful.

He hated being grateful.

---

The first burning came two days later.

The settlement had already been stripped of what the army wanted.

Grain carried.

Animals driven off.

Anything useful loaded.

Then torches appeared.

Ieuan knew fire as a household danger.

A spark in dry thatch.

A pot tipped badly.

A hearth left wrong.

This was different.

Fire used deliberately.

A roof caught.

Smoke poured low before wind pulled it sideways.

Horses panicked at once.

That saved Ieuan from thinking.

He became useful.

A mare pulled hard enough to rip skin from a man's hand. Ieuan caught the lead farther down, turned the animal away from the smoke and got her moving behind a calmer horse.

Another pack animal refused a narrow exit because men carrying plunder crowded it.

"Move them!" Ieuan shouted.

"Who are you ordering?"

"Anyone who wants the horse not to kick their teeth in!"

They moved.

The horse followed.

Flames ran along the roofline.

Ieuan smelled burning grain.

That was what broke through.

Not screams.

Not weapons.

Grain.

He had spent days in 1349 deciding whether a handful of peas justified entering another person's house.

Now men burned more food in an hour than his family might have eaten in months.

Ieuan kept working.

Smoke followed them down the road.

Later Iorwerth handed him a water skin.

"Drink."

Ieuan did.

"You're quiet."

"I'm always quiet."

"No. You become quiet when you're trying not to feel something."

Ieuan looked at him.

"And you know that because?"

"Because I have eyes."

Ieuan passed the water back.

Iorwerth did not ask more.

That was why Ieuan trusted him.

---

The farther they rode, the richer the country seemed in places.

Better vineyards.

More substantial buildings.

Stores worth taking.

Men became excited by plunder in ways Ieuan first found disgusting and then slowly understood.

A good piece of equipment meant money.

A captured animal meant money.

Cloth.

Wine.

Metal.

Everything had value.

War taught people to see value faster than suffering.

Ieuan was not immune.

Near Carcassonne, he found a pair of serviceable gloves among permitted goods and took them.

His old ones were nearly finished.

The new pair fit badly but were better.

He wore them that evening.

Then stared at his hands.

Someone had owned these.

A man with a house.

Maybe a servant.

Maybe a soldier.

Ieuan kept the gloves.

Moral purity did not keep hands warm.

---

A difficult crossing several days later gave him something cleaner to think about.

Rain and traffic had turned the approach into churned ground. One pack horse balked near the water. Another tried to push around it. Men behind kept forcing forward because men behind never believed a blockage was real until they stood inside it.

Ieuan slid from Brân.

"Stop the third one!"

A servant hesitated.

Ieuan pointed.

"Take him off the load line. Now!"

The man obeyed.

Ieuan moved the frightened horse sideways, not forward, creating enough space for another animal to pass.

A senior retainer shouted, "Do what the Welshman says!"

The words struck Ieuan harder than they should have.

Not *boy*.

Not *groom*.

The Welshman.

Men followed his judgement because the road needed moving.

Within minutes the jam cleared.

The army continued.

Ieuan remounted.

Pride surged immediately.

He had helped burn houses and still wanted praise.

Human beings, he decided, were made of contradictions because God had become tired halfway through making them.

---

Near a settlement east of Carcassonne, Ieuan saw the girl.

She was perhaps fourteen.

Maybe fifteen.

The age Gwenllian would have been if she had lived.

That calculation happened instantly.

Ieuan hated himself for it.

The girl carried a smaller child by one arm and dragged him away from the road while an older woman shouted at both.

She looked back once.

At the mounted men.

At the smoke.

At Ieuan.

He did nothing.

There was nothing to do.

She did not need rescuing by a Welsh soldier who was part of the army frightening her in the first place.

She disappeared behind a wall.

Ieuan rode on.

For the rest of the day he kept thinking:

Gwenllian would be fourteen.

Then correcting himself.

No.

She would have been fourteen.

The difference mattered.

---

By the time the army reached Narbonne on **8 November**, Ieuan had stopped seeing the expedition as the grand thing he had imagined from Wales.

It was still impressive.

That was the uncomfortable truth.

The scale.

The speed.

The discipline required to move so many men and animals hundreds of miles.

The fact that a force could cut through a wealthy region and leave destruction behind it.

He understood why men admired it.

He also understood what admiration ignored.

Brân had lost weight.

So had Ieuan.

His new gloves were already stained.

His spear felt less like a symbol than another thing that needed carrying.

The outward ride had been called successful.

Men talked of what had been burned, taken and damaged.

Ieuan sat beside Iorwerth that evening while horses chewed scarce fodder.

"We turn tomorrow?" he asked.

"Soon."

"Same road?"

"Not all of it."

Ieuan looked at the animals.

"They're tired."

"Aye."

"Fodder's worse."

"Aye."

"And we're carrying more."

Iorwerth looked at him.

"Aye."

Ieuan rubbed one hand over Brân's neck.

"Return'll be harder."

"Now you're learning war."

Ieuan looked back toward Narbonne.

The outward march had felt like achievement.

The road home looked like survival.
