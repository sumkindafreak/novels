# Chapter Twelve — The Sea

**Spring–5 October 1355**

War began in Wales with lists.

Ieuan found that disappointing.

He had expected more shouting.

Instead there were animals to count, loads to weigh, fodder to find, tack to inspect, men arguing over whether a horse was sound enough to travel and other men arguing that it was because there were not enough horses to reject every questionable one.

Names moved through the work like weather.

Warwick.

The Prince of Wales.

Gascony.

Sir Matthew de Soor.

Men Ieuan had known only as distant ranks became the reason a pack horse needed to be ready by sunrise.

On **26 April 1355**, Matthew de Soor and a Welshman named Ieuan ap Ruyn were among those documented gathering supplies in Wales for Warwick's Gascon service.

Ieuan ap Dafydd never met the other Ieuan.

If he heard the name, it passed without importance.

Wales held many Ieuans.

What mattered was that the roads were suddenly full of war before war itself was visible.

---

"Too old," one man said.

Ieuan put his hand on the mare's neck.

"No."

"Look at her teeth."

"I did."

"Then you've seen she's old."

"Old isn't lame."

"She won't keep up."

Ieuan walked the mare forward.

Watched.

Turned her.

Ran his hand down the leg.

"She'll keep up if you don't load her like a cart."

The man snorted.

"You paying for her?"

"No."

"Then—"

Matthew's senior servant said, "The boy keeps her."

The man shut his mouth.

Ieuan did not correct *boy*.

At twenty, he had learned that sometimes winning mattered more than the name attached to you while you did it.

He marked the mare for a lighter load.

Iorwerth watched from a few paces away.

"Getting sensible," he said in Welsh.

Ieuan looked up.

"Don't sound so disappointed."

"I had money on you dying stubborn."

"You'll lose it."

"We'll see."

---

Before leaving Wales, Ieuan returned to Glynrhondda.

He did not ask permission.

He arranged the work so there was a day to spare and rode Brân north and west through country that had once seemed impossibly large when he walked it behind Rhys.

The horse had aged with him.

Not much.

Enough.

Ieuan knew every stiffness now.

Every habit.

Every place Brân preferred to piss, which Ieuan regarded as proof horses were not more dignified than people despite the money spent on them.

Hendre Gelli appeared below the slope.

Ieuan did not go there first.

He climbed.

The grave remained.

He had improved it over the years when he could.

More stones.

A better line around the earth.

Nothing grand enough to attract attention.

He had no right to grand.

He sat beside Gwenllian's grave with the horse grazing nearby.

"France," he said.

It sounded stupid in the mountain air.

"Across the sea."

He imagined her immediate answer.

*I know what across means.*

Ieuan smiled.

He pulled Dafydd's knife from his belt and checked the hinge and edge.

The little folding blade had been sharpened so many times it seemed narrower than ever. He owned better knives now.

He carried this one.

"Rhys says the ships are worse than the French."

He paused.

"Iorwerth says Rhys hasn't seen the French."

Wind moved across the grass.

Ieuan looked at the stones.

"I'll come back."

That was not a promise he believed might fail.

Not then.

He truly expected to return.

He stood.

At Hendre Gelli afterward, he saw the house from the track and realised something had changed without permission.

He no longer imagined moving back into it.

The thought did not feel like betrayal.

That was worse.

It felt ordinary.

Ieuan rode on.

---

The south coast smelled wrong.

Salt.

Tar.

Fish.

Wet timber.

Too many men.

Too many horses.

Ships stood where Ieuan's mind still expected solid ground to continue.

He had seen the sea before only from distances that allowed it to remain scenery.

Standing beside it was different.

It moved when nothing should move.

The horizon refused to explain where land had gone.

Ieuan disliked it immediately.

The horses agreed.

Special hurdles and arrangements had been made to keep animals apart and upright aboard ship, with planks and gangways for loading.

None of that persuaded the first big gelding that walking onto a floating wooden box was sensible.

The animal stopped halfway up the ramp.

Behind, men shouted.

That made everything worse.

"Stop shouting!" Ieuan shouted.

Nobody listened because shouting at men not to shout rarely worked.

The gelding backed.

A man behind tried to drive it forward.

Ieuan saw the horse's eye.

White around the edge.

Panic.

"Back him off!"

"We need him on!"

"Back him off first!"

The horse's hind feet slipped.

Ieuan lunged for the lead.

Iorwerth grabbed another man's shoulder and hauled him clear before he was crushed against the side.

For several heartbeats everything became hooves, timber and swearing.

Ieuan did not pull forward.

He moved with the gelding backward until its feet found ground.

The animal trembled.

Ieuan put one hand along the neck and waited.

"We're losing time," someone said.

Ieuan ignored him.

He walked the gelding past the ramp once.

Turned.

Let it smell the timber.

Another horse went up ahead, calmer.

The gelding watched.

Ieuan waited for the breathing to change.

Then moved.

This time it went aboard.

A servant muttered, "Could have dragged the bastard."

Ieuan looked at him.

"And if he breaks a leg?"

The man shrugged.

"Not my horse."

Ieuan understood again that war was mostly people damaging things they did not own.

---

At sea, dignity disappeared first.

Rank lasted longer.

Not much longer.

Men who could sit a horse beautifully vomited over rails.

Men who spoke three languages prayed in whichever one they thought God might hear fastest.

Servants slipped on wet boards.

Horses kicked partitions.

One animal refused water until Ieuan wet his hand and let it smell before bringing the vessel slowly.

The air below decks where animals were kept was hot, foul and close.

Urine, dung, wet timber, sweat, sickness.

Ieuan almost laughed at the thought that he had spent half his life terrified somebody would smell wet bedding on him.

A whole ship now smelled like the little end had become king of the world.

The thought was funny enough that he looked around guiltily for Gwenllian.

Habit.

He hated that habit.

The first night, Ieuan barely slept.

Not from the sea.

From fear of wetting the bedding among men packed close enough to hear him breathe.

He lay awake listening to the hull complain.

Eventually exhaustion took him.

He woke dry.

Relief came so hard it embarrassed him despite nobody knowing why.

Iorwerth, awake nearby and green around the mouth, said, "You look pleased with yourself."

"I am."

"Why?"

Ieuan looked toward the pitching deck.

"I haven't thrown up."

Iorwerth closed his eyes.

"I hate you."

Ten minutes later Ieuan was sick over the side.

Iorwerth laughed until he had to be sick again too.

---

At some point during the crossing, Wales disappeared.

Ieuan had known it would.

That did not prepare him.

He went up when the weather allowed and looked back.

No ridge.

No mountain.

No dark line where land met sky.

Only water.

He stood gripping the rail.

Gwenllian's grave existed somewhere beyond the curve of a world he could no longer see.

The thought made distance real for the first time.

Not miles.

Not days.

Water.

If he changed his mind, he could not walk home.

Ieuan looked forward instead.

---

Gascony was familiar in all the wrong ways.

There were fields.

Animals.

Roads.

Rain.

People arguing over loads.

And then everything else was different.

Speech moved faster than Ieuan could catch.

Stone buildings sat differently in the land.

Vines spread where he expected pasture.

Men from England, Wales and Gascony filled spaces with different accents and habits.

Military equipment appeared in quantities Ieuan had never imagined.

Horses came off ships exhausted, stiff and sometimes injured despite every precaution.

For several days Ieuan hardly thought about France as war.

He thought about legs.

Water.

Feed.

Sores.

Tack.

Getting animals standing properly after confinement.

This was where he saw Edward le Despenser for the first time.

Not met.

Saw.

A young knight among men of rank, scarcely older than Ieuan himself.

Someone said the name.

"Despenser. Lord of Glamorgan."

Ieuan looked again.

That was strange.

A lord of Glamorgan should have looked older.

Larger.

Something.

Instead he was a man Ieuan might have passed on a road if clothes, horse and company did not proclaim otherwise.

Ieuan remembered 1349 dimly.

Hugh dead.

A boy inheriting things Ieuan could not picture.

He wondered if this was that boy.

Then a horse coughed and Ieuan stopped thinking about lords.

---

Iorwerth found him checking a girth days later.

"Warwick's people are moving soon."

"I know."

"You always know."

"People keep telling me."

Iorwerth crouched near the horse's foreleg.

"You're going with the armed servants. Not only remounts."

Ieuan looked at him.

"Who said?"

"Matthew's people."

"Why?"

"Because you're useful with horses and less useless with a spear than you were."

Ieuan tried not to smile.

"That's almost praise."

"Don't ruin it."

Ieuan checked the strap again though it did not need checking.

"Will I fight?"

Iorwerth looked at him.

"If someone tries to kill you, I recommend it."

"You know what I mean."

"No. You mean you want someone to promise war will give you a moment where everything becomes clear and you discover what sort of man you are."

Ieuan frowned.

"I didn't say that."

"Good, because it's nonsense."

Iorwerth stood.

"Most days you'll be tired. Hungry. Wet. You'll worry about a horse, lose a strap, be told to move something three times and then move it back. If fighting comes, it'll be faster than practice and uglier than songs."

Ieuan looked toward the larger camp.

"Still want to go?" Iorwerth asked.

Ieuan thought of Hendre Gelli.

Gwenllian's grave.

The boy at fourteen waiting beside it to die.

"Aye."

Iorwerth nodded.

"Then stop checking that girth. It's fine."

---

On **5 October 1355**, the army rode out from Bordeaux.

Banners moved above men.

Horses crowded the roads.

Pack animals stretched behind.

Ieuan had imagined this moment for months.

The beginning of war.

It felt almost exactly like moving cattle on a scale too large to understand.

He settled Brân beneath him.

Checked the horse ahead.

Checked the load behind.

Iorwerth looked over.

"Ready?"

Ieuan nearly said yes.

Instead he remembered every time confidence had punished him.

"No."

Iorwerth smiled.

"Good."

The column moved.
