# Pass One: Glamorgan Material Culture Comparators

This file asks a practical question:

> What ordinary physical things should exist around the protagonist's home without turning the set into a generic medieval-film prop store?

The most useful current comparison is **Cosmeston**, a large excavated medieval settlement in the Vale of Glamorgan with substantial thirteenth- and fourteenth-century archaeology.

Cosmeston is lowland/manorial South Glamorgan, not upland Glynrhondda. It therefore cannot be copied directly, but it gives us valuable evidence for the broader regional material world.

---

# 1. Cosmeston: what was actually excavated

The Vale of Glamorgan Council records that excavation beginning in 1978 revealed **13th–14th-century stone buildings** and developed into a long-running research programme.

Finds included:

- a metal knife;
- substantial quantities of medieval pottery;
- animal bone;
- quern/grinding stones;
- tracks and livestock-related landscape features;
- manor/demesne features including orchard, fishponds and dovecote.

Source:
- Vale of Glamorgan, History and Reconstruction: https://www.valeofglamorgan.gov.uk/en/enjoying/Coast-and-Countryside/cosmeston-lakes-country-park/cosmeston-medieval-village/History-and-Reconstruction.aspx

Evidence rating: **A for Cosmeston; B/C when transferred to a modest upland Rhondda household.**

---

# 2. An important myth correction: Cosmeston was not simply erased by the Black Death

Older public interpretations often described Cosmeston as a deserted medieval village abandoned because of the Black Death.

Ceramic and excavation research has complicated this substantially.

A Cardiff University doctoral study of South Glamorgan settlement ceramics states explicitly that the interpretation of Cosmeston as a village abandoned as a result of the Black Death **is not correct**. Continued medieval and later activity is archaeologically visible across the site.

Source:
- Alice Forward, *The ceramic evidence for economic life and networks from 12th- to 17th-century settlement sites in South Glamorgan*: https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/56805/1/2014forwardaphd.pdf

### Novel rule

Do **not** use Cosmeston as proof that plague automatically emptied every Glamorgan village permanently.

Use it for material culture and settlement archaeology, not a simplistic plague-abandonment narrative.

---

# 3. Pottery: ordinary, useful and breakable

Medieval South Glamorgan produced and used substantial quantities of locally/regional made pottery.

The Cosmeston and nearby Llandough evidence includes:

- cooking pots/jars;
- jugs;
- bowls;
- local Vale wares;
- some imported/regional pottery in higher-status contexts.

A Llandough later-medieval assemblage was dominated by cooking pots/jars, with Vale wares and locally distinct fabrics strongly represented.

Source:
- archaeological publication comparing Llandough and Cosmeston assemblages: https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archiveDS/archiveDownload?t=arch-769-1%2Fdissemination%2Fpdf%2Fvol49%2F49_001_092.pdf

### Family-house implication

Our protagonist's family can plausibly possess a **small number of coarse ceramic vessels**, especially cooking/storage forms and perhaps a jug.

Do not give them a matching dinner service.

A pot matters because if it cracks, they cannot order another one online or casually replace it the same afternoon.

---

# 4. Status is visible in objects

Cosmeston's manor produced more elaborate tablewares, including the famous ram-headed aquamanile and imported ceramics.

Recent scholarship uses ceramics from South-East Welsh lordly households to show networks of status and consumption.

Source:
- *Material Connections in Medieval Lordly Households. New Ceramic Evidence from South-Eastern Wales*: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00766097.2025.2578116

### Novel implication

The protagonist should experience a genuine material shock when he later enters a wealthier household.

His childhood home might contain:

- one or two valued jugs/pots;
- wooden vessels;
- simple iron tools;
- baskets/sacks;
- coarse cloth;

whereas a lordly household may contain:

- multiple specialised ceramic forms;
- finer/glazed wares;
- imported goods;
- more metalwork;
- conspicuous table ritual.

This allows social mobility to be shown through **objects before titles**.

---

# 5. The knife

A metal knife is an utterly ordinary but important object.

The Cosmeston excavations recovered a medieval knife. Agricultural households also require cutting tools for daily work.

Our protagonist's knife can credibly function as:

- food preparation tool;
- rope/twine cutter;
- animal-work tool;
- general utility blade;
- eventually a survival possession.

### Story rule

Do not introduce his knife like a fantasy dagger.

It should begin as an **ordinary household/work tool**.

If it later helps keep him alive, that is because mundane tools become precious when normal society disappears.

---

# 6. Grinding and grain processing

Quern stones at Cosmeston reinforce the physical labour behind grain consumption.

The family does not magically possess flour because grain exists.

Cereal food requires labour somewhere in the chain:

- threshing;
- winnowing;
- drying/storage;
- grinding/milling;
- mixing/cooking/baking.

Whether the family uses a local mill, hand quern or combination depends on final tenure/economic details.

Do not assume they personally hand-grind every grain without evidence; lordly milling rights can matter.

But the boy should understand that **grain and bread are not the same thing**.

---

# 7. Animal bone and meat

Animal bone from a site proves consumption/processing but not a universal household menu.

For Glynrhondda, local landscape evidence gives cattle unusually strong importance, with sheep also significant.

The family's meat consumption should therefore be governed by economics rather than modern assumptions.

Livestock may be worth more alive because they provide:

- milk;
- calves/lambs;
- labour/status/value;
- manure;
- future sale/payment.

Killing a cow for dinner is not an ordinary casual event for a modest household.

Pork/preserved meat and smaller animals may be more routine where available.

---

# 8. Building difference: Cosmeston versus Glynrhondda

Cosmeston contained substantial stone buildings.

That does **not** mean our upland tenant house should suddenly become a miniature stone manor.

We now possess better contemporary upland building comparison from **Gelligaer Common**, where platform houses used turf/stone construction, timber ridge structures and thatch.

Therefore:

- **Gelligaer remains our primary architectural comparator**;
- **Cosmeston is primarily a material-culture/economic comparator**.

This distinction must stay explicit.

# 9. Proposed childhood possessions

Not all are locked, but the following is a restrained working inventory.

## High confidence / strong reconstruction

- utility knives;
- coarse pottery cooking vessel(s);
- wooden bowls/spoons/containers;
- baskets and sacks;
- rope/cordage;
- agricultural tools;
- bedding straw;
- woollen cloth/blankets;
- animal tack/handling equipment;
- fire/cooking implements.

## Plausible but household-dependent

- glazed jug as a valued possession;
- chest or lockable storage box;
- iron shears;
- spindle/spinning equipment;
- hand quern;
- better tableware acquired second-hand/trade.

## Avoid casually

- abundance of metal utensils;
- glass drinking vessels;
- decorative weaponry;
- stacks of books;
- elaborate furniture;
- matching ceramics;
- manor-house luxury objects.

# 10. The plague transforms object value

The survival chapters can use material culture very effectively.

Before plague:

- taking a neighbour's pot is theft;
- entering somebody else's chest is a serious violation;
- livestock has an owner;
- cloth and tools belong to particular families.

After mass mortality:

- the same objects remain exactly where they were;
- ownership has become morally and legally confusing;
- surviving people desperately need them.

Welsh plague-era court evidence proves that survivors really did enter dead people's houses and remove household goods and livestock.

This means the boy's survival can be built around **ordinary objects whose social meaning has collapsed**.

---

# 11. A powerful repeated object idea

Pass Two may choose one humble family possession to travel through the novel.

Possible examples:

- mother's glazed jug;
- father's knife;
- sister's small wooden spoon;
- a blanket;
- an iron buckle;
- a simple religious object if evidence supports it.

The object should not be magical or secretly valuable.

Its value comes from memory.

If the boy later stands in a wealthy household surrounded by better versions of everything he once owned, the cheap damaged object from home can mean more than all of them.

---

# 12. Evidence grades

### A

- Cosmeston contains 13th–14th-century Glamorgan settlement archaeology.
- pottery, animal bone, knife and quern/grinding stones were recovered.
- recent scholarship rejects the simplistic interpretation that the Black Death alone caused complete Cosmeston abandonment.
- ceramics demonstrate social/economic differences across South Glamorgan sites.

### B

- coarse pottery and modest metal/wood tools in a Glynrhondda tenant household.
- household possessions being materially limited and repair-oriented.
- noticeable material contrast between tenant and lordly households.

### C

- exact number of family vessels;
- precise heirloom object;
- whether they own a quern versus rely on milling;
- exact tools kept in the house.

These can be chosen in Pass Two without violating the archaeological picture.
