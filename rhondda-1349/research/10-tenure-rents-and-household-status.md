# Pass One: Tenure, Rents and Household Status

This file tests an assumption made early in planning: that the protagonist's father can simply be called a **poor free tenant**.

## Current conclusion

That phrase is too precise for the evidence currently in hand.

Until a Glynrhondda rental, court roll or equivalent record close to **1349** identifies the relevant forms of local tenure, the safer working description is:

> **a modest Welsh tenant household in Glynrhondda, holding and working land within the marcher Lordship of Glamorgan while retaining elements of upland Welsh custom.**

This preserves the social position the story needs without inventing a legal category.

---

## 1. Glynrhondda was inside a powerful marcher lordship

Richard de Clare seized Meisgyn and Glynrhondda in 1246. Llantrisant castle and borough became the administrative centre for the two districts.

Later inquisitions continue to list **Llantrisant with the district/locality of Glynrhondda** among the possessions and revenues of the lords of Glamorgan.

The protagonist's family therefore does not live beyond authority. Even in a dispersed upland community there are:

- rents or renders;
- courts and officials;
- claims over land and pasture;
- obligations attached to tenure;
- an administrative route toward Llantrisant/Cardiff.

Exactly what this family owes remains to be established.

---

## 2. But the uplands were not simply an English manor transplanted into Wales

The social/legal geography of Glamorgan was uneven.

The lowland Vale experienced intensive Anglo-Norman settlement and manorial organisation earlier. Upland territories retained Welsh population, landholding traditions and legal/customary features for longer, even as marcher lords extended seigneurial control.

Heneb specifically notes the persistence of Welsh clan/customary systems, land tenure and inheritance in the upland Penychen landscape compared with areas farther south under earlier Norman domination.

### Novel implication

Do not make the father behave like a textbook English villein simply because that is the most familiar model in popular medieval history.

Likewise, do not portray him as an entirely autonomous smallholder owing nothing to a lord.

The interesting reality sits between those clichés.

---

## 3. What near-period lordship records prove

The surviving material currently located establishes the **framework** much better than it establishes individual Glynrhondda holdings.

### 1307–1314 Llantrisant

Inquisitions and later summaries show Llantrisant as a functioning borough and administrative centre with burgage rents. The borough had around 145½ rent-paying burgages in the 1307 inquisition of Joan de Clare, with several still vacant after warfare; by 1314 the number of burgages had grown substantially.

This does not tell us the rent of a farm in Ystradyfodwg, but it proves a monetised administrative/rental world immediately south of Glynrhondda.

### Later medieval inquisitions

Fifteenth-century inquisitions still group Llantrisant and Glynrhondda as a lordship/district with an assessed annual value. Other Glamorgan entries distinguish messuages, land, meadow, wood, pasture and rents and record Welsh tenants by patronymic names.

These later entries are useful for **institutional continuity**, not for assigning a 1440s acreage or rent to a fictional 1349 family.

---

## 4. What later Glynrhondda records tell us — and their limit

The National Library of Wales preserves extensive Bute/Glamorgan estate material for Glynrhondda, but the readily catalogued manorial leases located so far begin mainly in the **late sixteenth century**, with rentals surviving much later.

Examples demonstrate that later Glynrhondda holdings could include named tenements, parcels measured in acres, upland ground and mixed property rights.

This is valuable for tracing long-term landscape continuity and medieval place-name survivals.

It is **not** permission to copy a 1583 lease backwards into 1349.

---

## 5. Penrhys creates a separate tenancy possibility

Heneb states that from the early fourteenth century the Cistercians at Penrhys ceased direct large-scale sheep farming and divided the manor into **more than thirty leased holdings**.

This is unusually close to our period and proves that leased agricultural holdings existed in the immediate Rhondda landscape.

However, we do not yet possess:

- the c.1349 tenant list;
- holding sizes;
- precise rents;
- terms of lease;
- evidence that our chosen Ystradyfodwg household belonged to the Penrhys manor.

Therefore the protagonist's father should **not automatically be made a Penrhys tenant** merely because that documentation is convenient.

---

## 6. How much wealth should the family have?

Until tenure is resolved, the safest economic profile is **modest but viable**.

They may plausibly possess/use:

- a dwelling and curtilage/garden area;
- access to meadow or hay ground;
- small arable plots where terrain permits;
- pasture/common grazing rights or customary access;
- a few cattle central to household wealth;
- perhaps sheep/pigs/poultry depending on final model;
- agricultural tools;
- storage vessels;
- woollen bedding/clothing of varying quality;
- enough stored grain/pulses to make theft after their deaths meaningful.

Avoid both extremes:

- they are **not destitute landless beggars**;
- they are **not prosperous yeoman farmers in the early-modern sense**.

Their tragedy is stronger if they have built a small, functional life worth losing.

---

## 7. Cattle as wealth and responsibility

The medieval Rhondda archaeological landscape strongly supports cattle-centred seasonal pastoralism.

For this family, cattle can function as:

- food through milk/dairy;
- portable wealth;
- a reason for seasonal upland work;
- a source of manure;
- a major winter fodder burden;
- something thieves may take after plague deaths;
- an impossible responsibility for a fourteen-year-old suddenly left without adults.

Do not give the family a large herd without evidence. A small number of valuable animals is more appropriate to the working social level and creates sharper survival choices.

---

## 8. Labour obligations versus cash rent

**Not locked.**

Possible medieval obligations can include cash rents, produce/renders, customary work and court attendance, but the exact blend varies enormously by lordship and tenure.

Until direct Glynrhondda evidence is located, avoid scenes such as:

- 'Father owes exactly twelve days' boon-work';
- 'their rent is 8d at Michaelmas';
- 'the lord owns every calf';

unless research later establishes a local basis.

Safe early-chapter language can simply show that:

- land is not held without obligation;
- father worries about what is owed;
- officials/stewards represent a distant authority;
- Llantrisant is associated with that authority;
- dates such as Michaelmas may matter administratively without specifying invented sums.

---

## 9. Military obligation — do not use as the shortcut to knighthood

Research into Welsh military recruitment shows that tenure-based military obligations existed in parts of medieval Wales, but the exact obligations of Welsh populations in Glamorgan are poorly understood and fourteenth-century military recruitment increasingly relied on paid/contractual mechanisms.

Therefore the boy should **not** become a soldier because his father's farm conveniently owes knight service.

His later military trajectory must arise through household/retinue service in Pass Three.

This preserves the extraordinary nature of his rise.

---

## 10. Revised social-status rule

Previous wording:

> poor free Welsh tenant

Revised Pass One wording:

> **modest Welsh tenant household in the uplands of the Lordship of Glamorgan**

Possible later refinement:

- customary tenant;
- free tenant;
- leaseholder;
- member of a Welsh kin-based holding;
- another locally documented category.

Do not choose among these until evidence supports it.

---

# Research gap

The priority archival target is now:

**a rental, account, extent, survey or court material for Glynrhondda/Llantrisant/Ystradyfodwg between roughly 1300 and 1360.**

Particularly valuable information would be:

- named Welsh tenants;
- holding types;
- rents/renders;
- court obligations;
- pasture/common rights;
- inheritance practice;
- references to hendre/hafod holdings;
- changes caused by the 1349 mortality.

Failure to locate such a record does not prevent the novel; it simply means the household remains a careful regional reconstruction rather than a copy of one documented tenancy.
