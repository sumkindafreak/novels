# Pass One: The Lordship of Glamorgan in 1349

This file establishes the political/administrative backdrop immediately above the protagonist's tenant world.

A major coincidence makes 1349 unusually useful for the novel:

> **the Lordship of Glamorgan changes hands immediately before the Black Death crisis reaches the region.**

---

# 1. Hugh le Despenser dies on 8 February 1349

Hugh le Despenser (often called Huchon in contemporary/family contexts), lord of Glamorgan, died on **8 February 1349**.

His surviving Inquisition Post Mortem is calendared in the *Calendar of Inquisitions Post Mortem, 1347–1352*.

His cause of death is **not securely known**.

Some later writers have wondered whether plague killed him, but this must not be presented as fact.

### Novel rule

Hugh's death can be part of the background political chronology, but do not state that he died of the Black Death unless new direct evidence appears.

Sources:
- National/archival summaries of Hugh's 1349 IPM
- Cardiff historical records

---

# 2. His heir is a child: Edward le Despenser

Hugh died without children.

His heir was his nephew **Edward le Despenser**, son of Hugh's deceased brother Edward.

The 1349 IPM describes Edward as approximately **twelve years old and more**.

This means the lordship does not immediately pass into the hands of an adult resident magnate capable of personal rule.

Instead, Edward's inheritance is subject to **royal custody during his minority**.

This is important for both atmosphere and the later novel arc.

The protagonist and the great lord of his region are roughly the same generation, but their 1349 experiences are opposite:

- one boy loses his household and inheritance through plague;
- the other boy inherits one of the great marcher lordships through aristocratic succession.

This parallel is thematically powerful, but should not be forced into an implausible childhood meeting.

---

# 3. Glamorgan is under custodial administration

Research on the South Wales marcher elite records that in **1349** Sir Roger de Berkerolles was used on crown business in south-east Wales and, together with **Simon Basset**, was appointed custodian of the Lordship of Glamorgan during Edward le Despenser's minority.

Source:
- Swansea University thesis discussing Berkerolles and Despenser patronage, citing Patent/Close Rolls: https://cronfa.swansea.ac.uk/Record/cronfa42675/Download/0042675-02082018162513.pdf

### Novel implication

The authority above the protagonist's family in 1349 can be understood as:

- the marcher lordship still exists fully;
- rents/courts/rights do not disappear;
- but the lord himself is a minor;
- custodians and officials administer the estate in his stead.

Do not exaggerate this into administrative chaos. Medieval minority administrations were normal legal mechanisms.

The interesting point is simply that the lordship is undergoing a **real transition of personnel and custody at exactly the moment plague arrives**.

---

# 4. The 1349 Inquisition Post Mortem is a major surviving source

Hugh's 1349 IPM is especially valuable because comparable excerpts show it contains detailed descriptions and valuations of Glamorgan property.

For example, a Glamorgan archaeological report quoting the IPM records the manor of **Sully** in 1349 as containing:

- 220 acres of demesne arable;
- 41 acres of meadow;
- 247 acres of pasture;
- 18 free tenants;
- 28 customary tenants;
- annual value £33 5s. 1d.

Source:
- Glamorgan-Gwent archaeological report appendix citing Hugh le Despenser's 1349 IPM: https://www.walesher1974.org/her/groups/GGAT/media/ReportPDF/137_A097WindmillSiteHayesRoadBarryFE.pdf

### Why this matters

It demonstrates that the 1349 lordship survey is detailed enough to distinguish:

- demesne land;
- meadow;
- pasture;
- free/customary tenants;
- monetary values.

The research challenge is recovering the equivalent detailed section, if preserved, for **Llantrisant/Glynrhondda** rather than assuming Sully's lowland tenure structure applies to the uplands.

This is now the highest-value unresolved archival target in Pass One.

---

# 5. Glynrhondda and Llantrisant remain administratively paired

Later Despenser-derived IPMs explicitly describe:

> Llantrisant, its castle/vill/lordship, together with the lordship/district of Glynrhondda.

One later inquisition gives the combined Llantrisant/Glynrhondda complex with appurtenances, advowsons and knight's fee.

Source:
- Mapping the Medieval Countryside: https://inquisitionspostmortem.ac.uk/view/inquisition/20-621/625.html

This later evidence cannot supply our exact 1349 rents, but it reinforces the long-term administrative relationship already identified in the landscape/history sources.

### Novel implication

Llantrisant remains the sensible direction for:

- lordship officials;
- courts/administration;
- serious market contact;
- external political news;
- formal disputes or obligations affecting the protagonist's family.

---

# 6. Llantrisant immediately before plague

A national gazetteer of medieval markets/fairs records Hugh le Despenser granting/confirming Llantrisant liberties on **4 May 1346**, including the right to a fair on **1 August**.

Source:
- Gazetteer of Markets and Fairs to 1516: https://archives.history.ac.uk/gazetteer/wales.html

Local historical work also reports a high number of burgages by 1349, although exact figures and the claim that plague drove subsequent decline should be checked against the underlying primary survey before being used quantitatively.

### Novel implication

The wider world outside Glynrhondda is not economically dead before the epidemic.

Llantrisant is a functioning borough/administrative node, which makes the contrast after mortality more effective.

---

# 7. A new long-arc possibility: Roger de Berkerolles

Do **not** lock this into the plot yet.

Sir Roger de Berkerolles is historically interesting because:

- he belongs to the Glamorgan marcher elite;
- he was involved in Despenser patronage;
- he appears in crown business in south-east Wales;
- he was appointed one of the custodians of the young Edward le Despenser's lordship in 1349.

This makes him, his household or his administrative network a potentially more plausible eventual bridge from a post-plague Glynrhondda youth into lordship service than having the young Edward le Despenser personally discover the protagonist.

### Pass Three rule

Investigate Berkerolles and the custodial/affinity network before deciding who first employs the boy.

Do not convert this research lead into plot fact during Pass One.

---

# 8. Thematic parallel worth preserving

### February 1349

Edward le Despenser is roughly twelve and unexpectedly becomes heir to Glamorgan.

### Spring 1349

Our fictional protagonist is fourteen and continues ordinary tenant-family life in Glynrhondda.

### May 1349

The protagonist begins losing everything.

### 1356

Edward le Despenser is historically associated with the Battle of Poitiers.

The fictional boy's trajectory may eventually bring these two social worlds together.

The contrast is strong precisely because they should **not** begin as friends or equals.

---

# 9. Do not overstate administrative effects of plague

We currently cannot prove how quickly plague disrupted the actual collection of Glynrhondda rents or the local lordship court.

Welsh evidence elsewhere proves that plague caused:

- dead tenants;
- vacant land;
- reduced rents;
- succession problems;
- uncultivated holdings.

But any specific shutdown of Llantrisant/Glynrhondda administration must be supported or fictionalised cautiously.

The safest model is progressive strain rather than instant disappearance of authority.

---

# 10. Pass One archival target

The highest-value unresolved document is now clear:

> **the Glamorgan section of Hugh le Despenser's 1349 Inquisition Post Mortem, especially any entry detailing Llantrisant and Glynrhondda tenants, rents, land or value.**

The IPM is known to survive and to contain substantial Glamorgan detail.

If its Glynrhondda entry can be recovered, it may allow us to refine:

- household tenure language;
- rent/service obligations;
- regional value;
- tenant categories;
- lordship structure on the literal eve of the plague.

Until then, the existing “modest Welsh tenant household” description remains safer than a narrower legal label.

---

# Evidence grades

### A

- Hugh le Despenser died 8 February 1349.
- his nephew Edward was the minor heir.
- the inheritance was administered during minority.
- Roger de Berkerolles and Simon Basset were involved as custodians in 1349.
- Hugh's 1349 IPM contains detailed Glamorgan manorial information.
- Llantrisant and Glynrhondda are demonstrably linked within the marcher lordship structure.

### B

- lordship personnel changes affect the administrative atmosphere experienced by Glynrhondda tenants.
- Llantrisant functions as the family's most relevant lordship/borough connection.

### C / future plot work

- any direct interaction between the protagonist and a named custodian;
- Berkerolles household taking him into service;
- exact effect of lordship minority on his family's rent/obligations;
- any childhood encounter with Edward le Despenser.

These belong to Pass Three unless a direct record changes the evidence.
