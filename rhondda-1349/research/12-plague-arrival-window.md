# Pass One: Plague Arrival Window in 1349

This file answers a deceptively difficult question:

> **When should the first plague deaths occur in the protagonist's part of Glynrhondda?**

The documentary answer is not precise enough to give us an exact local date. The story therefore needs a historically defensible working window rather than a fabricated certainty.

---

# 1. Hard anchors

## Glamorgan: 1349

Museum Wales and Margam historical material place the Black Death in **Glamorgan in 1349**, with the plague reducing the monastic community at Margam Abbey.

Sources:
- Museum Wales, Margam timeline: https://museum.wales/media/28919/AGC-NMC-Art-Learning-Resource-Margam-House_1-12.pdf
- Margam Country Park chronology: https://www.margamcountrypark.co.uk/about/historic-margam/chronology-of-key-events/

Evidence rating: **A/B institutional secondary evidence for Glamorgan in 1349.**

## Wales: spring 1349

Modern historical summaries based on Welsh evidence commonly place the pestilence in Wales by **spring 1349**, with some summaries using March as a broad arrival point.

The evidence for the plague's exact route and progression through Wales is explicitly described by historians as sparse.

Evidence rating: **B for a broad spring-Wales chronology; do not convert it into a precise Glynrhondda date.**

---

# 2. The Bristol Channel context

The plague was active in the major Bristol region in 1348–49. Bristol was one of medieval Britain's major ports and was deeply connected to the western maritime economy.

For South Wales, the Bristol Channel created multiple plausible routes of introduction through:

- shipping;
- merchants;
- coastal towns;
- religious houses;
- servants;
- officials;
- livestock/commodity movement;
- ordinary travellers.

The novel does not need a single named 'patient zero' arriving from Bristol.

In fact, that would probably be too neat.

### Safer story model

News travels faster than certainty.

People in Glynrhondda may hear that:

- people are dying across the water;
- Bristol is afflicted;
- coastal settlements are fearful;
- deaths have appeared nearer Cardiff/Margam/the Vale;

before anybody they personally know becomes sick.

---

# 3. Lowland versus upland

Later Welsh historical synthesis suggests that the epidemic may have affected lowland, densely connected communities more severely than some upland areas, although plague clearly penetrated inland Wales.

This matters for Glynrhondda.

The valley is **not immune because it is remote**.

But remoteness can alter timing and contact patterns.

A plausible progression is:

1. maritime/lowland Glamorgan becomes visibly affected;
2. markets, religious contacts, officials and travellers carry news and risk inland;
3. Llantrisant and the lower approaches become increasingly fearful;
4. scattered Glynrhondda holdings hear rumours before seeing illness;
5. the first local household cases appear after a delay.

This is a story reconstruction, not a documented epidemiological map.

---

# 4. Penrhys as a contact point

Penrhys is especially useful because it is a documented religious/travel location with:

- a Marian shrine;
- chapel;
- Ffynnon Fair;
- hostelry;
- Cistercian estate activity;
- ridgeway access.

It therefore gives the story a believable reason for **outside news and strangers to reach the uplands**.

However:

- there is no evidence currently in hand that a known plague victim brought the disease via Penrhys;
- there is no evidence currently in hand for a specifically documented 1349 plague pilgrimage there.

If sick or frightened pilgrims appear in the novel, that remains **C — plausible fiction**.

---

# 5. Recommended working window

## Current recommendation

**Rumours intensify during March–April 1349.**

**First deaths in the protagonist's immediate community: approximately late April to June 1349.**

This is deliberately a window rather than an exact date.

### Why this works historically

- plague is established in Wales/Glamorgan during 1349;
- a spring arrival allows time for coastal/lowland infection and inland spread;
- the Rhondda's relative isolation can plausibly create a short delay;
- the route does not depend on a single fictional carrier.

### Why this works with the agricultural world

Late spring also intersects naturally with:

- cattle moving toward higher grazing;
- increased dairying;
- repair/use of upland shelters;
- depleted winter stores;
- long but increasingly busy working days.

The family has survived winter and begins to believe the year is improving exactly as the mortality arrives.

That emotional reversal is useful but is **not** the reason the date is chosen; the historical window must remain defensible first.

---

# 6. Strongest story option: May 1349

If a single month is needed for chapter planning, **May 1349 is currently the strongest working choice**.

Do not yet label it a documented local fact.

Possible shape:

### March

Rumours from outside the valley are contradictory.

Some people dismiss them as travellers' exaggerations, punishment stories or another ordinary sickness.

### April

News becomes harder to dismiss.

A traveller/pilgrim/official reports deaths much closer to home.

There may be disruption to normal journeys or market activity.

### Early–mid May

A neighbouring household becomes the first local example the boy personally witnesses.

### Mid–late May

Father develops symptoms.

The family catastrophe begins.

### Late May / early June

Mother and sister sequence unfolds according to plausible exposure/incubation rather than one person instantly infecting the next.

### June onward

The boy's survival story begins in earnest as pasture/hay work should be accelerating but human labour is collapsing.

This creates the haunting image of a landscape becoming greener and more abundant while the population disappears.

# 7. Medical chronology for the family

Modern plague biology provides the timing framework, even though the characters cannot understand it.

## Bubonic plague

Typical incubation is approximately **2–8 days** after infection.

Common onset can include:

- sudden fever;
- chills;
- weakness;
- headache;
- painful swollen lymph nodes/buboes.

Untreated infection can progress rapidly and may become septicemic or pneumonic.

Source:
- CDC Signs and Symptoms: https://www.cdc.gov/plague/signs-symptoms/index.html

## Transmission rule

Bubonic plague should not be written as if every touch of a sick person automatically transmits it.

Important routes include infected fleas/animals; pneumonic plague can spread by close-contact respiratory droplets.

Source:
- CDC How Plague Spreads: https://www.cdc.gov/plague/causes/index.html

# 8. Proposed family death logic

The exact days remain for Pass Two, but the sequence should use **shared environmental exposure plus different timing**.

### Father

He becomes the first visibly sick family member.

This does not prove he was the first infected.

### Mother

She tends him closely, but if she later develops bubonic disease the narrative should not automatically claim she caught it from touching him.

She may have shared the same infected household environment.

If father's disease becomes pneumonic, close-contact transmission becomes another possible route.

### Sister

The younger sister initially appears healthy enough to flee with the protagonist.

She becomes ill several days later, fitting the incubation window.

This delay is medically plausible and emotionally devastating because the boy briefly believes he has saved her.

### Protagonist

He remains asymptomatic.

His different sleeping location/bedding exposure may contribute, but the text never declares a single proven cause.

# 9. Seasonal survival consequences of a May outbreak

A May outbreak makes survival difficult in a specific way.

## Food

- winter stores are running low;
- the main grain harvest is months away;
- fresh growth exists but does not replace stored calories;
- dairy may be increasingly available if he can manage livestock;
- abandoned stored grain/pulses become extremely valuable.

## Livestock

- cattle can feed increasingly on pasture;
- upland grazing becomes useful;
- young animals increase the workload;
- unattended stock begins straying between holdings.

## Shelter

- exposure is less immediately lethal than deep winter;
- heavy rain, cold nights and wet clothing still matter;
- an upland hafod becomes much more usable as refuge.

## Bodies

Warmer conditions increase the practical urgency of burial and handling the dead.

Do not turn this into gratuitous decomposition imagery; use smell, flies, haste and exhausted burial labour selectively.

## Agriculture

The tragedy becomes visible in unfinished work:

- fields not weeded;
- fences unrepaired;
- cattle wandering;
- hay work approaching with too few people;
- crops growing while owners are dead.

# 10. What remains unknown

We still do not know:

- the exact first plague case in Cardiff;
- the exact first plague case in Llantrisant;
- the exact first plague case in Glynrhondda;
- the exact first plague case in Ystradyfodwg;
- the precise route by which *Yersinia pestis* entered the Rhondda.

Unless new documentary evidence appears, the novel must **not pretend to know these things**.

---

# 11. Current status

### Locked

- Glamorgan outbreak year: **1349**.
- plague reaches the protagonist's community during 1349.

### Strong working recommendation

- rumours: **March–April 1349**;
- local family/community outbreak: **May 1349**, with a defensible broader window of late April–June.

### Still C-level story choice

- exact day of first local death;
- exact carrier/route;
- exact order of neighbouring-household cases.

May 1349 should be treated as the planning default unless stronger local evidence overturns it.
