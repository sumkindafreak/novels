# Pass One: 1349 Calendar and Seasons

This file builds a **working seasonal calendar** for a poor rural household in Glynrhondda. It is not yet a chapter timeline. The aim is to know what work, food pressure, weather exposure and religious rhythms are plausible at different points of the year before deciding exactly when the family's plague story begins.

## Evidence rule

The agricultural calendar below combines:

- **A — local Rhondda evidence:** a strongly pastoral medieval landscape, cattle-dominant stock, sheep around Penrhys, hendre/hafod seasonal movement, hay/pasture needs, ridge travel and scattered holdings;
- **B — broader medieval British/Welsh evidence:** haymaking, cereal harvest, threshing, winter slaughter, oats/barley as important Welsh grains, peas/beans and preserved foods;
- **C — story reconstruction:** the precise jobs performed by this particular family on a particular week.

Do not write a rigid modern farm calendar into the novel. Weather, altitude and household resources can move tasks by weeks.

---

## January — hunger, fodder and maintenance

Likely pressures:

- cattle and other stock rely heavily on stored fodder and whatever winter grazing remains;
- hay, straw and grain stores are being consumed rather than replenished;
- daylight is short and wet/cold exposure is dangerous;
- tool, harness, fence and building repairs make sense when field work is limited;
- salted/smoked meat and stored pulses/grain matter more than fresh produce.

Story texture:

- smoke-heavy interior life;
- muddy approaches and soaked clothing;
- anxiety over how much fodder is left;
- animals brought closer to shelter;
- darkness making a neighbouring holding feel much farther away.

## February — lean stores, preparation

Likely pressures:

- surviving stored food may be monotonous;
- animals remain vulnerable after a hard winter;
- preparation for spring cultivation and lambing/calving can begin depending on stock and local practice;
- hedges, banks, fences and tools need attention.

Story texture:

- this is an excellent month for showing how **not-starving** requires year-round planning;
- a household can look materially secure in autumn and frightened by February.

## March — spring work begins

Likely work:

- increasing field preparation where arable land exists;
- manure spreading and soil work are plausible general medieval tasks;
- stock begins making greater use of new growth;
- births among livestock can sharply increase labour;
- upland routes remain wet and exposed.

For our family, the boy should already be competent with animals at fourteen. He is not a modern child occasionally helping his parents; useful labour is part of household survival.

## April — grazing improves, workload expands

Likely work:

- pasture growth improves;
- dairy work increases where cows/ewe milk are used;
- spring-sown crops require attention where grown;
- repairs to upland structures/routes/enclosures become easier;
- movement between lower and higher grazing begins to become more plausible.

Story use:

This can be a deceptive month: the household feels as if it has survived winter, but food reserves may still be thin before the new agricultural year truly pays back.

## May — livestock and upland movement

The Rhondda's medieval hendre/hafod pattern makes late spring and summer movement particularly important.

Plausible activities:

- driving cattle toward higher grazing;
- checking boundary banks/enclosures;
- dairying;
- watching young stock;
- gathering useful woodland products;
- moving equipment between permanent and seasonal sites.

Do not assume the entire family permanently relocates to a second full-sized home. A hafod may be a much more modest seasonal holding.

## June — hay and the long working day

Medieval manuscript calendars commonly associate June with haymaking. Historic England notes long-standing hay meadow practice, and hay is especially important to a cattle-keeping household because winter survival depends on stored fodder.

Plausible jobs:

- cutting meadow grass;
- turning and drying hay when weather permits;
- carrying/stacking hay;
- dairy work;
- grazing management;
- repair and preparation for cereal harvest.

A wet spell can be economically frightening because hay must dry.

### Religious note: Tyfodwg

A later tradition gives St Tyfodwg/Dyfodwg a feast on **25 June**, but the Cult of Saints in Wales database lists his feast day as unknown. Do **not** treat 25 June as a securely documented fourteenth-century Ystradyfodwg observance unless stronger evidence emerges.

## July — hay, weeds, first harvest pressures

Depending on weather and crop mix:

- haymaking may continue;
- earlier grains begin approaching harvest;
- gardens/pulses provide more fresh food;
- stock must be kept out of crops;
- long daylight makes very long working days plausible.

This is a good month to contrast abundance in the landscape with exhaustion in the people maintaining it.

## August — harvest

Medieval calendars repeatedly associate August with cereal harvest and threshing. A Rhondda household should not be portrayed as a major wheat producer without local evidence; Museum Wales notes that Wales's wet mountainous conditions favoured **oats and barley** over wheat in later traditional agriculture.

Plausible work:

- cutting grain;
- binding and carrying sheaves;
- protecting grain from rain;
- beginning threshing/storage;
- gathering beans/peas and other crops as ready;
- maintaining livestock at the same time.

Failure here means winter hunger.

## September — storage and Michaelmas

Likely pressures:

- threshing and winnowing;
- storing grain dry;
- gathering nuts and seasonal wild foods;
- assessing which animals can be kept through winter;
- preparing lower holdings for winter use.

**Michaelmas (29 September)** was an important medieval accounting/rent term in Britain. We must verify the exact obligations of Glynrhondda tenants before assigning our family a Michaelmas rent payment, but it is a strong candidate for the social calendar.

## October — autumn preparation

Likely work:

- bringing stock down from higher grazing where seasonal movement applies;
- repairing winter shelter;
- collecting fuel;
- storing grain/pulses/nuts;
- autumn sowing where appropriate;
- slaughtering or selling animals that cannot economically be overwintered may begin.

Religious/local note:

A later tradition gives St Gwynno's feast as **26 October**, but this should remain background until verified against stronger medieval liturgical evidence.

## November — slaughter, salting, darkening year

General medieval husbandry makes autumn/early-winter slaughter practical because fodder is finite and cold conditions assist preservation.

Plausible activities:

- butchery;
- salting/smoking meat;
- rendering fat;
- preserving hides;
- intensive fuel gathering;
- bringing valuable tools/materials under cover.

The boy should understand that an animal's death produces work before it produces food.

## December — winter survival

English Heritage notes medieval calendar imagery showing livestock slaughter in December and emphasises that winter survival depended on preparation throughout the year.

Plausible life:

- short working window outdoors;
- indoor repair and processing work;
- feeding stock from accumulated stores;
- rationing fuel and food;
- Christian feast observance providing moments of communal relief amid winter scarcity.

# Food by season — working model

## Most reliable year-round foods

- stored grain/oats/barley preparations;
- dried peas/beans;
- dairy while animals are producing;
- ale;
- preserved meat where the household can afford/produce it.

## Seasonal supplements

- eggs;
- fresh greens;
- berries;
- crab apples/other local fruits where evidenced;
- hazelnuts;
- fresh meat around slaughter;
- fish where access and rights permit.

Wild food should supplement agriculture, not replace it.

# Plague-story implications

The exact month of plague arrival in the family's immediate community remains **unlocked**.

When chosen, season will radically alter survival:

- **spring outbreak:** old stores depleted; new food not ready;
- **summer outbreak:** food growing but intensive work abandoned; corpses and animals decay quickly;
- **harvest outbreak:** standing grain everywhere but too few people to bring it in;
- **autumn outbreak:** houses may contain full stores, making scavenging more viable;
- **winter outbreak:** exposure and fuel become immediate killers.

This choice should be made from the strongest available Glamorgan chronology, not merely for convenience.
