# Pass One: Urine, Medicine and Bed-Wetting

This file tests one of the novel's most unusual ideas against medieval medical evidence.

The conclusion is encouraging:

> **Bed-wetting and inability to control urination were recognised medical problems, and urine itself carried major diagnostic meaning in medieval medicine.**

However, there is currently **no evidence that urine protected against plague** or that medieval Welsh people specifically used a survivor's urine as a plague cure.

That distinction must remain absolute.

---

# 1. Uroscopy was mainstream medieval medicine

By the fourteenth century, **uroscopy** — examining a patient's urine — had become an integral diagnostic/prognostic tool in learned medieval medicine.

Practitioners examined urine for features such as:

- colour;
- clarity;
- thickness/viscosity;
- sediment;
- sometimes odour;

and interpreted those signs through humoral medicine.

The characteristic urine flask (`matula`) became so closely associated with physicians that it functioned as a visual symbol of the profession.

Sources:
- Henry Connor, “Medieval uroscopy and its representation on misericords”: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4953881/
- Wellcome Collection, “Health and the medieval church”: https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/health-and-the-medieval-church

Evidence rating: **A for the importance of uroscopy in fourteenth-century medical culture.**

---

# 2. Medieval Welsh medical texts specifically discuss urine

Diana Luft's study of **medieval Welsh medical manuscripts** identifies a corpus contained in four fourteenth-century manuscripts, including British Library Additional 14912, Cardiff 3.242, Oxford Rawlinson B467 and Jesus 111 / the Red Book of Hergest.

The Welsh material includes a uroscopy text known as **`Ansoddau'r Trwnc` — “The Qualities of Urine.”**

Its medical framework treats urine colour/texture as a sign of the body's humoral condition and disease.

Source:
- Diana Luft, “Uroscopy and Urinary Ailments in Medieval Welsh Medical Texts”: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK540246/

Important dating caution:

The manuscripts are late-fourteenth-century witnesses to a body of medical tradition. They strongly establish that these ideas belonged to medieval Welsh medical culture, but they do **not** prove that a specific healer in Glynrhondda in May 1349 possessed one of these manuscripts.

Evidence rating: **A for the texts; B when reconstructing ordinary local awareness in 1349.**

---

# 3. Bed-wetting itself appears in the medical tradition

The medical tradition discussed by Luft contains an explicitly identified remedy for **bed-wetting (`submeiulos`)** preserved in an older Latin/Old English medical tradition.

The remedy recommends hare brain mixed with wine.

The historical importance for the novel is **not** whether the remedy worked — it did not provide a scientifically valid treatment.

The importance is that:

- bed-wetting was recognised as a condition requiring treatment;
- it was not conceptually invisible to medieval medicine;
- people could seek remedies for it;
- the protagonist's family need not view his problem only as laziness or moral failure.

Source:
- Luft / NCBI Bookshelf, discussion of bed-wetting and urinary control: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK540246/

Evidence rating: **A for existence of the remedy/tradition; B/C for whether this particular family knows it.**

---

# 4. Medieval Welsh remedies for inability to control urine

The Welsh corpus contains multiple remedies for excessive/uncontrolled urination.

Examples discussed by Luft include preparations involving:

- burned swine legs/haunches made into powder and added to food/drink;
- burned goat tallow made into powder and added to pottage/stew.

These recipes have parallels in contemporary English collections, showing that they belonged to a wider medieval medical culture rather than being uniquely Welsh eccentricities.

Again, these are historically interesting treatments, **not medically recommended treatments**.

Evidence rating: **A for the recorded recipes.**

# 5. What the family might think about the boy's wetting

The story should avoid a single modern interpretation.

Different people can understand the same problem differently.

## Mother

She may treat it as:

- an embarrassing bodily condition;
- something he cannot fully help;
- a problem requiring practical management;
- perhaps something for which an herbal/folk remedy has been attempted.

## Father

He may be less sympathetic, especially because the boy is fourteen and otherwise performs adult-level work.

His frustration can be economic/practical as much as moral:

- bedding is valuable;
- straw must be replaced;
- cloth takes time and fuel/weather to dry;
- the accident affects shared sleeping arrangements.

## Sister

She may tease him because it is intimate family knowledge, not because medieval society has a formal diagnosis for enuresis.

## Healer/practitioner

A practitioner influenced by humoral medicine may see urine as medically informative and may offer a remedy for urinary control.

Whether our family ever consults such a person remains a Pass Two decision.

# 6. Urine and the plague-survivor superstition

This is where we must be especially disciplined.

## Historically supported foundation

People in the fourteenth century could plausibly believe that urine revealed important information about a person's health and bodily constitution.

## Not historically documented for our case

We currently have no evidence that:

- urine prevented plague;
- plague survivors' urine was drunk as a protective treatment in Glynrhondda;
- bed-wetting was believed to create plague immunity.

Those claims must never appear in narration as fact.

## Best fictional development

The superstition can develop **after people notice the boy survives**.

A plausible sequence is:

1. everyone knows or discovers that the boy wets himself;
2. he survives while close family members die;
3. frightened survivors desperately search for an explanation;
4. somebody connects his unusual urinary condition to his survival;
5. the existing cultural idea that urine reveals bodily health gives the rumour a small foothold;
6. desperation transforms observation into supposed remedy/protection.

This is psychologically believable fiction rooted in a real medical culture.

It is not a documented plague treatment.

---

# 7. A stronger subplot than “people just drink his urine”

The superstition should escalate rather than begin absurdly.

## Stage 1 — curiosity

Someone asks why he did not become ill.

The separate bedding arrangement becomes known.

## Stage 2 — medical interpretation

A local practitioner or frightened adult wants to **look at his urine**, treating it as evidence of his constitution.

This is directly compatible with uroscopic thinking.

## Stage 3 — rumour

The story mutates:

> the boy who cannot hold his water cannot catch the mortality.

## Stage 4 — desperate imitation

People begin inventing their own protective rituals around urine.

This is C-level fiction and can vary in intensity.

## Stage 5 — guilt

Someone relying on the supposed protection becomes sick anyway.

The protagonist, still only fourteen, may feel that he caused the death even though he never possessed a cure.

This gives the superstition lasting psychological consequences without asking the reader to believe urine really protected him.

---

# 8. The protagonist's own belief

He should not immediately conclude scientifically that altered sleeping exposure saved him.

He does not know fleas transmit plague.

His mental chain may be painfully simple:

- Father slept there and died.
- Mam slept there and died.
- Sister slept there and died.
- I was put apart because I pissed the bedding.
- I lived.

For years he may privately believe:

> **the shame saved me.**

That belief can survive even when no one else believes the superstition.

It becomes emotionally more important than the factual biological explanation.

---

# 9. Should the wetting continue after age fourteen?

Historically we do not need to cure it neatly when the plague begins.

From a character perspective, continuing episodes are powerful.

Possible trajectory:

- frequent enough at fourteen to affect sleeping arrangements;
- reduced frequency as he grows older;
- strongly associated with stress, exhaustion and traumatic periods in the fictional character arc;
- occasional recurrence during later military life.

### Medical caution

The novel should not claim a specific modern diagnosis unless the story gives evidence for one.

The cause remains **unknown**.

Do not retro-diagnose him with a neurological, urinary, endocrine or psychological condition simply to tidy the premise.

---

# 10. Language in prose

The narration can use “bed-wetting” for reader clarity if written in modern narrative English.

Characters themselves may use blunt bodily language rather than clinical terms.

His own shame may centre on simple concepts:

- wetting the straw;
- wetting himself;
- failing to hold his water;
- waking wet;
- spoiling bedding.

Avoid modern words such as:

- enuresis;
- bladder dysfunction;
- urinary disorder;

inside ordinary character dialogue unless presented in authorial historical explanation outside the fiction.

---

# 11. Current rules to lock

- The boy's bed-wetting is a **real condition**, not deliberate behaviour.
- The underlying cause remains unknown.
- Medieval medicine recognised uncontrolled urination and bed-wetting as problems.
- Fourteenth-century medical culture gave urine significant diagnostic meaning.
- The family may plausibly have attempted a remedy, but the exact remedy remains optional.
- Urine does **not** biologically protect him from plague.
- Any plague-protection belief surrounding his urine is an emergent fictional superstition.
- The narrator never validates that superstition as medically true.
- His own belief that the humiliation somehow saved him may become psychologically central.

---

# 12. Evidence summary

### A — documented

- uroscopy was integral to fourteenth-century medicine;
- medieval Welsh medical texts discuss urinary diagnosis and ailments;
- inability to control urination appears in Welsh/English medical recipe traditions;
- a historical medical remedy explicitly labelled for bed-wetting survives in the wider tradition.

### B — strong reconstruction

- a modest Welsh household might know that urine has medical significance;
- a local practitioner could examine urine or offer a urinary remedy;
- the condition could cause practical domestic shame and altered bedding arrangements.

### C — fictional

- exactly which remedy the protagonist's family tries;
- who first associates his wetting with plague survival;
- anyone requesting his urine specifically because he survived plague;
- the later folklore surrounding him.

The C-level material is acceptable precisely because the novel will make no claim that it was a documented plague practice.
