# Locked Authenticity Rules

These are strong enough to treat as constraints unless later research provides a reason to revise them.

## Setting and chronology

- Opening plague year in Glamorgan: **1349**.
- Opening region: **Glynrhondda**, Lordship of Glamorgan.
- Protagonist begins at **14**.
- **Story outbreak month: May 1349.** This is a fictional planning lock inside a historically defensible late-April–June window; it is **not** a claim that the first Ystradyfodwg plague case is documented in May.
- Julian calendar is used for 1349.
- Easter Sunday in 1349 falls on **12 April**.
- Long-range ending target: **Battle of Poitiers, 19 September 1356**, protagonist roughly 21–22.

## The plague

- The disease is plague caused by *Yersinia pestis*.
- Urine does **not** provide biological immunity.
- Bed-wetting may alter sleeping arrangements, bedding turnover and exposure.
- His survival retains an element of uncertainty/luck.
- Bubonic plague is not casually transmitted person-to-person merely by touching a patient.
- Pneumonic plague can spread by close-contact infectious droplets.
- Family deaths must respect plausible incubation and disease progression.
- Characters do not use germ theory.
- Do not routinely call the epidemic “the Black Death” in dialogue; prefer period concepts such as pestilence/mortality.

## Rhondda world

- Do not write modern Rhondda towns into 1349.
- Use real medieval geography where known: Glynrhondda, Ystradyfodwg, Llanwynno, Penrhys, Ffynnon Fair, Castell Nos, ridge routes and Llantrisant.
- Permanent family home is a **fictional modest holding on the lower western side of the Rhondda Fawr in the Ystradyfodwg–Gelli orbit**, above the better river-terrace land.
- Upland work/refuge uses the real medieval pastoral landscape toward **Mynydd Gelli / Mynydd Ton** as its model.
- The valley is rural, scattered and heavily pastoral.
- Cattle are central to medieval upland settlement evidence; do not reduce the economy to sheep.
- Ridgeway travel matters.
- The exact house coordinate and holding boundaries remain fictional and must not overwrite known archaeology.

## Housing

- No modern bedrooms or modern domestic layout.
- **Gelligaer Common late-13th/early-14th-century native Welsh houses are the primary close architectural comparator.**
- Useful material vocabulary includes turf/stone components, timber ridge structure, thatch, open hearth, earth/limited paved flooring and practical drainage.
- Later surviving Welsh longhouses may be used as secondary comparators, not copied as exact 1349 Rhondda evidence.
- No automatic chimney, glazed windows or separate modern bedrooms for a modest tenant household.
- Boy's separated pallet is caused by repeated bed-wetting and must remain physically plausible inside the final reconstructed house.
- Materials/layout must be labelled internally as reconstruction where direct evidence is absent.

## Bed-wetting and medicine

- The bed-wetting is genuine and involuntary.
- Its underlying cause remains **unknown**; do not retro-diagnose a modern condition without evidence.
- Medieval medicine recognised urinary-control problems and bed-wetting.
- Fourteenth-century medicine made substantial diagnostic use of **uroscopy**.
- Medieval Welsh medical texts contain remedies concerning urinary control.
- Any belief that the protagonist's urine protects against plague is **fictional superstition**, never narrator-endorsed fact.
- His own psychological belief that his shame somehow saved him is permitted and may persist even though the biological cause of survival remains unknown.

## Food and survival

- Survival is based mainly on agriculture, stored food, livestock and abandoned resources, not year-round woodland foraging.
- No potatoes, tomatoes, maize or other post-Columbian foods.
- Hunger, food preservation, fuel and fodder must follow season.
- Cattle/pasture work and hendre–hafod seasonal movement shape his pre-plague skills.
- Abandoned property/livestock scavenging is historically supported in plague-era Welsh records.
- A May outbreak means winter stores can be thin while the main harvest is still months away.

## Material culture

- The childhood household owns relatively few, practical, repairable objects.
- Coarse pottery, wooden vessels, utility knives, baskets/sacks, rope, bedding, cloth and agricultural/animal-handling equipment are more appropriate than abundant decorative objects.
- Gelligaer is the primary upland building comparator; **Cosmeston** and other South Glamorgan sites are broader material-culture/economic comparators.
- Do not repeat the obsolete/simple claim that Cosmeston is proven to have been entirely abandoned because of the Black Death; later ceramic research rejects that interpretation.
- Material contrast between the boy's tenant childhood and later lordly household life should be visible through ordinary objects.

## Religion

- Christianity is part of the protagonist's worldview, not scenery.
- **Ystradyfodwg** is the family's ordinary parochial church centre.
- **Penrhys** is a Cistercian grange/Marian sacred place, not a substitute parish church.
- A specific 1349 plague pilgrimage to Penrhys is **plausible fiction, not documented fact**.
- The family can have one final Easter together on **12 April 1349** before the May story outbreak.
- Burial should remain organised/ritualised as long as circumstances permit; avoid automatic Hollywood corpse-pit imagery.
- Exact local Rogation routes, feast ceremonies and sermons are reconstruction unless specifically documented.
- No securely identified Ystradyfodwg priest for 1349 has yet been recovered; a fictional Welsh-speaking priest/chaplain is acceptable if Pass Two needs one.

## Language and naming

- Protagonist starts as primarily Welsh-speaking.
- Anglo-Norman belongs mainly to elite/administrative contexts; Latin is important in formal Church/record culture.
- English becomes more important as his world expands.
- Welsh patronymic naming (`ap`, `ferch`) is preferred over casually assigning modern hereditary surnames.
- Final protagonist/family names are not yet chosen.
- Strong current male pool: **Ieuan, Madog, Dafydd, Hywel, Llywelyn, Gruffudd**.
- Strong current female pool: **Gwladus, Gwenllian, Margred, Angharad**.
- Wladus/Gwladus is directly attested in a plague-era Welsh court record; this proves period use, not Glynrhondda locality.

## Social status

- Family is described as a **modest Welsh tenant household in the uplands of the Lordship of Glamorgan**.
- Do not call the father a specific 'free tenant', villein or other narrow legal category until/if local evidence supports it.
- The household is subject to lordship authority/rents/custom but should not be modelled as a generic English manor peasant household.

## Social rise

- No secret noble ancestry.
- No random knight instantly adopting him because he is “special.”
- Advancement must come through labour, household service, skill, patronage, military usefulness and time.
- Post-plague labour scarcity may create openings but does not erase medieval hierarchy.
- Knighthood must remain socially exceptional and fully earned.

## Tone

- First movement: survival horror.
- Middle: grief, isolation, work, adaptation and social transition.
- Final movement: military service and war horror.
- Medieval life is neither relentless mud caricature nor romantic pageant.
- Violence, disease and death should be specific and human rather than gratuitously gory.

## Research discipline

Every major detail used in finished prose should be tagged in planning as one of:

- **A — documented/local**
- **B — strong period/regional reconstruction**
- **C — fiction designed to fit evidence**

If a detail is uncertain, the research bible says so instead of laundering it into fact.
