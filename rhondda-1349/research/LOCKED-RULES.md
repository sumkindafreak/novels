# Locked Authenticity Rules

These are strong enough to treat as constraints unless later research provides a reason to revise them.

## Setting and chronology

- Opening plague year in Glamorgan: **1349**.
- Opening region: **Glynrhondda**, Lordship of Glamorgan.
- Protagonist begins at **14**.
- Long-range ending target: **Battle of Poitiers, 19 September 1356**, protagonist roughly 21–22.
- The exact opening month in Glynrhondda is **not yet locked**.

## The plague

- The disease is plague caused by *Yersinia pestis*.
- Urine does **not** provide biological immunity.
- Bed-wetting may alter sleeping arrangements, bedding turnover and exposure.
- His survival retains an element of uncertainty/luck.
- Bubonic plague is not casually transmitted person-to-person merely by touching a patient.
- Pneumonic plague can spread by close-contact infectious droplets.
- Family deaths must respect plausible incubation and disease progression.
- Characters do not use germ theory.
- Do not routinely call the epidemic “the Black Death” in dialogue; prefer period concepts such as pestilence/mortality.

## Rhondda world

- Do not write modern Rhondda towns into 1349.
- Use real medieval geography where known: Glynrhondda, Ystradyfodwg, Llanwynno, Penrhys, Ffynnon Fair, Castell Nos, ridge routes and Llantrisant.
- The valley is rural, scattered and heavily pastoral.
- Cattle are central to medieval upland settlement evidence; do not reduce the economy to sheep.
- Ridgeway travel matters.
- The exact family holding may be fictional but must fit known landscape patterns.

## Housing

- No modern bedrooms or modern domestic layout.
- Later surviving Welsh longhouses may be used as comparators, not copied as exact 1349 Rhondda evidence.
- Materials/layout must be labelled internally as reconstruction where direct evidence is absent.

## Food and survival

- Survival is based mainly on agriculture, stored food, livestock and abandoned resources, not year-round woodland foraging.
- No potatoes, tomatoes, maize or other post-Columbian foods.
- Hunger, food preservation, fuel and fodder must follow season.
- Abandoned property/livestock scavenging is historically supported in plague-era Welsh records.

## Religion

- Christianity is part of the protagonist's worldview, not scenery.
- Penrhys is a real medieval pilgrimage/healing location.
- A specific 1349 plague pilgrimage to Penrhys is currently **plausible fiction, not documented fact**.
- Burial should remain organised/ritualised as long as circumstances permit; avoid automatic Hollywood corpse-pit imagery.

## Language and naming

- Protagonist starts as primarily Welsh-speaking.
- Anglo-Norman belongs mainly to elite/administrative contexts; Latin is important in formal Church/record culture.
- English becomes more important as his world expands.
- Welsh patronymic naming (`ap`, `ferch`) is preferred over casually assigning modern hereditary surnames.
- Final protagonist/family names are not yet chosen.

## Social rise

- No secret noble ancestry.
- No random knight instantly adopting him because he is “special.”
- Advancement must come through labour, household service, skill, patronage, military usefulness and time.
- Post-plague labour scarcity may create openings but does not erase medieval hierarchy.
- Knighthood must remain socially exceptional and fully earned.

## Tone

- First movement: survival horror.
- Middle: grief, isolation, work, adaptation and social transition.
- Final movement: military service and war horror.
- Medieval life is neither relentless mud caricature nor romantic pageant.
- Violence, disease and death should be specific and human rather than gratuitously gory.

## Research discipline

Every major detail used in finished prose should be tagged in planning as one of:

- **A — documented/local**
- **B — strong period/regional reconstruction**
- **C — fiction designed to fit evidence**

If a detail is uncertain, the research bible says so instead of laundering it into fact.
