# Pass One: Plague and Religion

## 1. Historical timing

For this novel, **1349 is the correct opening plague year for Glamorgan**.

Museum Wales records the Black Death reaching Glamorgan in 1349 and devastating the community at Margam Abbey. Wider English evidence places the first arrival in England in 1348, with catastrophic mortality through 1349.

We should not assign an exact week for Glynrhondda until a source supports that precision. The story can begin with rumours arriving before local deaths.

## 2. What the disease is

Modern science identifies plague as infection by **Yersinia pestis**.

The CDC identifies three principal clinical forms relevant to the novel:

### Bubonic plague

- sudden fever, headache, chills and weakness;
- one or more painful swollen lymph nodes (buboes);
- commonly follows infected flea exposure;
- incubation usually about 2–8 days.

### Septicemic plague

- fever, chills and extreme weakness;
- abdominal symptoms/shock may occur;
- bleeding and tissue death can occur;
- may arise directly or from untreated bubonic plague.

### Pneumonic plague

- rapidly developing pneumonia;
- fever, weakness, chest pain, cough, breathing difficulty;
- may produce bloody/watery sputum;
- can arise after plague spreads to the lungs or after inhaling infectious droplets;
- direct close-contact droplet spread is the principal person-to-person route.

## 3. The bed-wetting rule

**Urine does not cure plague and does not make the protagonist biologically immune.**

His bed-wetting changes behaviour and exposure:

- he may be made to sleep separately;
- his bedding may be changed/discarded more frequently;
- he may spend less time in the family's most crowded sleeping space;
- he may therefore encounter a different flea/rodent environment and less close contact if pneumonic disease develops.

This can reduce risk without guaranteeing safety.

The novel should preserve uncertainty. He can believe the wetting saved him. Other characters can believe it. The narrator must never establish magical urine protection as fact.

## 4. Family infection sequence

A medically plausible sequence is:

1. Father becomes ill first, perhaps after the household/environment has already been exposed.
2. He develops fever and a painful bubo.
3. Mother nurses him while sharing the same household exposure.
4. Mother falls ill.
5. The boy escapes with his sister while she still looks healthy.
6. Several days later she develops fever/bubo, consistent with a bubonic incubation period.

Important: do not automatically write 'Mother caught it from touching Father' unless Father develops pneumonic involvement. Shared infected fleas/environment are sufficient.

## 5. Welsh Black Death evidence

William Rees' study of Welsh records documents severe disruption after the pestilence:

- rents collapsed where tenants died;
- mills lost value because tenants were dead;
- land lay uncultivated;
- heirs were unable to take up holdings;
- administrative dues were remitted because survivors were too few or impoverished;
- land and tenements changed hands extensively.

One court-roll example is especially useful for the novel: **Madoc ap Ririd and his brother Kenwric** were accused of entering houses by night after deaths during the pestilence and stealing household goods, old iron, three oxen and three cows.

This gives direct documentary support for abandoned-property scavenging/theft in plague-era Wales.

## 6. Death and burial

Avoid the cliché that medieval people instantly abandoned ritual and threw everyone chaotically into pits.

The East Smithfield emergency Black Death cemetery in London contained both individual graves and trench burials, but Historic England describes the interments as orderly. This is London evidence, not a template for rural Glamorgan, but it shows that organised Christian burial persisted under extreme pressure.

For Glynrhondda, the safest dramatic progression is:

- normal parish burial while mortality is low;
- shortened or rushed rites as deaths increase;
- graves prepared in advance or multiple burials in rapid succession;
- fewer mourners because households are sick or frightened;
- possible absence/death/exhaustion of clergy;
- desperate concern about burial in consecrated ground and prayers for the dead.

Do not invent a mass grave at Ystradyfodwg unless the story clearly treats it as fictional emergency practice rather than archaeological fact.

## 7. How characters understand the plague

They do not know about bacteria or flea vectors.

Possible medieval explanatory frameworks include:

- divine punishment;
- sin and repentance;
- corrupted air/miasma;
- astrological/planetary influence;
- local rumours and folk explanations;
- saints, relics, holy wells and intercession.

A character can hold several explanations at once without seeing a contradiction.

## 8. Religion as worldview

Religion must be embedded in daily thought rather than used as decorative scenery.

The boy's world includes:

- baptism and Christian burial;
- Mass and the parish church;
- confession and sin;
- saints and feast days;
- prayer for the dead;
- fear of judgement and concern for souls;
- Marian devotion;
- pilgrimage and holy places.

He is not a modern sceptic wearing medieval clothes. Even if anger eventually damages his faith, the argument takes place inside a deeply Christian mental world.

## 9. Penrhys and healing

Penrhys had a medieval Cistercian grange, chapel, Marian shrine, hostelry and healing well, Ffynnon Fair.

This creates an ideal historically grounded setting for desperation during plague.

Plausible fiction:

- frightened people travelling there to pray;
- offerings or vows;
- rumours of cures;
- contradictory stories about who recovered;
- the protagonist seeing faith become both comfort and panic.

Unproven claim to avoid:

- asserting that a documented plague pilgrimage to Penrhys occurred in 1349 unless evidence is found.

## 10. The urine superstition subplot

This can be used, but only as **character belief**, never authorial medicine.

Possible progression:

1. People realise the boy survives repeated proximity to death.
2. Someone knows or discovers that he wets himself.
3. A causal superstition forms.
4. Desperate people ask for his urine, bedding or some related 'protection'.
5. Someone relying on it still dies.
6. He carries guilt even though he never had the power to save them.

This subplot fits a culture operating without germ theory and intensifies the boy's shame, but it should be researched further against documented medieval urine/medical practices before final drafting.

## 11. Tone rule

The plague chapters should frighten through:

- speed of deterioration;
- helplessness;
- ordinary rituals failing under scale;
- empty houses and loose animals;
- silence where neighbours used to be;
- bells, smoke and human absence;
- uncertainty over whether fever means death;
- hunger, exposure and injury after the epidemic.

Do not rely on cartoon gore. The horror is that the world remains recognisable while the people disappear.
