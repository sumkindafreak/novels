# Pass One: House Reconstruction

This file reconstructs the protagonist's family home and upland shelter as cautiously as the evidence permits.

The key rule is that **the family's exact house is fictional**. Later industrial development destroyed or buried much of the medieval valley-floor settlement evidence in the Rhondda. We therefore build from the closest strong archaeological comparators and clearly distinguish direct evidence from reconstruction.

---

## 1. Best close comparator: Gelligaer Common

The strongest currently located domestic comparator is the excavated native Welsh medieval settlement on **Gelligaer Common**, in the Glamorgan uplands east of the Rhondda.

Coflein records a cluster of medieval house platforms excavated in 1938. Pottery from the settlement dates occupation to the **late thirteenth / early fourteenth century**, making it unusually close to the novel's 1349 opening.

The excavated central building had:

- a rectangular platform cut into the hillside;
- walls of turf and stone/slabs approximately 1 m thick;
- a hearth at the uphill/hood end;
- opposing entrances in the long walls;
- uprights supporting a ridge pole;
- a thatched roof;
- irregular paving toward the lower end;
- an internal drainage gutter along the uphill wall;
- coarse pottery and a glazed jug;
- some evidence of iron working.

The surrounding group contained simpler rectangular buildings as well.

### Evidence rating

**A for the Gelligaer houses themselves.**

**B when used as a material/form comparator for Glynrhondda.**

Sources:
- Coflein NPRN 15319: https://coflein.gov.uk/en/site/15319
- Heneb, Gelligaer Common: https://heneb.org.uk/hcla/gelligaer-common/

---

## 2. Why Gelligaer matters to our story

Gelligaer is not Glynrhondda, but it is much more useful than copying a surviving fifteenth- or sixteenth-century farmhouse.

It gives us a **contemporary South-East Welsh upland building vocabulary**:

- turf;
- stone/slab walling;
- timber roof structure;
- thatch;
- open hearth;
- earth/partly paved floors;
- deliberate drainage;
- rectangular buildings built into slopes.

This strongly supports a home world that is rough and practical without being a cartoon mud hut.

---

# 3. The permanent family home: safest reconstruction

The family's permanent holding is currently placed in the **Ystradyfodwg–Gelli orbit**, on or just above the better river-terrace land rather than high on an exposed platform-house site.

Direct archaeology for a 1349 valley-floor house here has not survived clearly enough for an exact plan.

The safest fictional reconstruction is therefore:

- a modest rectangular dwelling;
- timber structural members;
- walls using locally available turf, timber, clay/wattle and/or stone footing/material depending on the final design;
- a steeply pitched thatched roof;
- an open hearth rather than a chimney fireplace;
- one principal domestic living/sleeping space, perhaps with a smaller service/storage division;
- packed earth and/or limited stone/slab flooring;
- very small openings with shutters rather than glazed domestic windows;
- immediate yard/storage/animal structures clustered close to the house;
- drainage treated as a constant practical concern.

### Do not lock yet

We do **not** yet have sufficient evidence to say:

- the main house is definitely a classic later Welsh `tŷ hir` longhouse;
- cattle definitely share the same roof as the family;
- the family owns multiple substantial buildings;
- the wall material is exclusively wattle-and-daub or exclusively stone;
- there is a lofted upper storey.

Those details remain reconstruction choices.

---

# 4. Interior experience

Whatever final layout is chosen, the house should feel materially different from a modern dwelling.

## Light

Most daylight enters through:

- the open doorway;
- small shuttered openings;
- firelight.

Domestic glazing should not be casually given to a modest upland tenant household.

## Smoke

The open hearth means smoke is a normal feature of the room. Roof-level escape and drafts remove some smoke, but the interior can remain smoky and soot-darkened.

Do not give the house a later chimney unless evidence changes.

## Heat

The fire is the centre of warmth, cooking, drying and evening work.

The house is not uniformly warm. Moving away from the hearth matters in winter.

## Floor

A packed earth floor with strategic paving or slabs around hard-wearing/wet areas is more defensible than a fully paved domestic floor.

## Smell

Normal smells include:

- wood/peat smoke depending on final fuel choice;
- damp wool;
- leather;
- food and ale;
- livestock nearby;
- manure outside;
- wet straw;
- human bodies in a small space.

This is ordinary domestic life, not evidence that the family is uniquely filthy.

---

# 5. Furniture and possessions

Keep the household materially modest.

Plausible categories include:

- wooden chests;
- stools/benches;
- a table board or simple working surface;
- cooking pot/cauldron;
- wooden bowls/spoons;
- coarse pottery;
- perhaps one better glazed jug as a valued object;
- baskets and sacks;
- agricultural tools;
- knives;
- blankets and woollen cloth;
- straw bedding;
- storage vessels;
- harness/rope and animal equipment.

The Gelligaer excavations produced little material culture, which Heneb interprets as suggesting a lowly/impoverished population. Do not fill the protagonist's house with decorative medieval clutter merely because it looks attractive in an illustration.

---

# 6. Bedding and the bed-wetting problem

This is central to the premise, so the sleeping arrangement must work both historically and biologically.

## What should be avoided

Do not give each family member a separate bedroom.

Do not state that urine kills plague bacteria/fleas or makes him immune.

Do not make his alternative sleeping place a magical flea-free zone.

## Best current story solution

The family normally sleeps within the principal domestic space on shared/closely grouped bedding.

Because the fourteen-year-old still wets himself, his accidents create several problems:

- shared bedding is spoiled;
- woollen cloth is difficult to wash and dry;
- straw must be replaced;
- smell causes friction;
- his younger sister can be disturbed;
- his father increasingly regards him as too old for the problem.

The family therefore gives him a **separate straw pallet at the far/lower end of the house or in a directly adjoining service/byre-end shelter**, physically separated from the main sleeping group.

His bedding is also stripped and replaced more often than theirs.

### Why this can matter without becoming pseudo-science

If the main household sleeping area contains the infected rodent/flea exposure that initiates the family outbreak, a different pallet position, different bedding turnover and less close sleeping contact could alter his exposure.

This does not guarantee safety. It simply creates a plausible difference between him and the rest of the family.

His survival remains luck plus altered exposure.

### Further refinement still needed

When the final plan is drawn, prefer a sleeping location that is:

- separate enough to matter;
- still believable for a modest household;
- not directly beside stored grain/food where rodent exposure would obviously undermine the premise;
- not presented as scientifically proven protection.

---

# 7. The upland hafod / survival refuge

The Rhondda evidence strongly supports seasonal upland settlement associated particularly with cattle.

The boy should therefore know a simpler upland structure before the plague.

This is one of the best survival devices available to the novel because he does not need to discover a convenient abandoned cabin after the catastrophe.

He already knows:

- where it is;
- how to reach it in poor visibility;
- where water runs nearby;
- where cattle are normally contained;
- which roof section leaks;
- where fuel is gathered;
- what can be stored there.

## Safe physical model

The upland refuge can draw more directly on platform-house archaeology:

- rectangular;
- dug/cut into the slope at the upper end;
- turf/stone walling;
- timber ridge structure;
- thatched roof;
- open hearth;
- earth/stone floor;
- crude drainage;
- minimal furniture.

It need not be a second comfortable family home.

It is a working agricultural shelter.

---

# 8. Proposed house plan for later visualisation

Not final dimensions, but the working plan should contain:

1. **Main hearth/living zone** — food preparation, warmth, family work.
2. **Grouped family sleeping area** — parents and younger sister close together.
3. **Boy's separated pallet** — lower/far end or lightly divided service-end space because of bed-wetting.
4. **Storage/chest area** — valuable cloth, tools and dry supplies kept off the wettest floor areas.
5. **Door and draft path** — important to smoke, temperature and the boy's sleeping discomfort.
6. **Adjacent yard/animal area** — livestock close enough to dominate sound/smell/work, without yet locking whether cattle share the same roof.
7. **Water/drainage route** — the hillside and Rhondda rainfall have to matter.

A later drawing should label every element as A/B/C evidence rather than pretending the plan was excavated at Ystradyfodwg.

---

# 9. Story consequences

This reconstruction gives several scenes naturally:

- his mother quietly pulling wet straw out before his father wakes;
- winter blankets that cannot dry quickly;
- humiliation because his sister knows exactly why his pallet is separated;
- waking colder than everyone else because he sleeps nearer a draft/service end;
- resentment at being treated like a child while doing adult farm work;
- the separated pallet later becoming the detail he obsessively believes saved him;
- after the family dies, the house becoming physically unbearable because every familiar place still has an owner in his mind.

Most importantly, **the bed-wetting changes his position inside an ordinary household rather than becoming a supernatural shield.**

---

# 10. Current confidence summary

### A — documented/local or close contemporary evidence

- Rhondda has medieval platform houses/hafodau in the surrounding uplands.
- Gelligaer has excavated late-13th/early-14th-century native Welsh platform houses.
- Gelligaer evidence includes turf/stone walls, hearth, ridge-pole supports, thatched roof, opposing entrances, partial paving and drainage.

### B — strong reconstruction

- similar material vocabulary available to a modest Glynrhondda tenant household;
- open-hearth, low-light, single-main-space domestic life;
- straw bedding and clustered sleeping;
- simple upland agricultural shelter known to the family.

### C — story invention

- exact family house footprint;
- exact wall mix;
- exact position of animal space;
- exact boy's pallet location;
- exact reason his exposure differs.

These C-level details are permitted because they are designed around the evidence rather than presented as recovered fact.
