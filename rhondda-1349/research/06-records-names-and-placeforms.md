# Pass One: Records, Names and Place-Forms

This file records what surviving medieval material can safely tell us about how people and places around Glamorgan were named. It deliberately separates **regional attestation** from **Glynrhondda-specific proof**.

---

## 1. The valley's name in medieval records

Modern `Rhondda` should not be assumed to be the spelling a fourteenth-century clerk would write.

Secondary summaries of the Cardiff Records preserve forms including:

- `Rhoddeni` — 1203
- `Rotheni` — 1213
- `Glyn Rhoddni` — 1268
- `Glenrotheney` — 1314
- `Glynroddne` — 1314
- `Glynroddney` — 1348

This is useful because **1348 is almost exactly our opening period**.

### Novel rule

Narration can use **Glynrhondda** for readability. Documents, officials or chapter epigraphs may occasionally preserve period spellings such as `Glynroddney` when justified.

Do not treat spelling variation as evidence that these were different places. Medieval clerks writing Welsh names in Latin/French/English administrative contexts produced substantial orthographic variation.

The Royal Commission's Historic Place Names of Wales database also preserves multiple recorded forms under the Rhondda head-name, reinforcing that spelling varied over time.

---

## 2. Welsh personal names: patronymics, not modern surnames

For a rural Welsh family in 1349, the default naming model should be **given name + patronymic**, not a fixed inherited surname.

Examples of the structure:

- `Dafydd ap Ieuan` — Dafydd, son of Ieuan
- `Llywelyn ap Rhys` — Llywelyn, son of Rhys
- `Gwenllian ferch Dafydd` — Gwenllian, daughter of Dafydd

`ap`/`ab` and `ferch` can vary in spelling and scribal rendering. Latin or Anglo-Norman records may anglicise/latinise the given names.

A long chain can be used when legal identity or lineage matters, but ordinary dialogue should not constantly recite genealogies.

---

## 3. Genuine South-East Welsh names close to our period

A December 1317 document connected with the Despenser struggle in Glamorgan names Welsh witnesses for the commonalties of Gwynllwg and Machen. These are **not Glynrhondda tenants**, but they are excellent contemporary regional examples:

- Llewelyn ap Ivor
- Howel ap David
- Howel ap Ieuan
- Meuric Vachan
- Meuric ap Reynolf
- Meuric ap Madoc ap Howel
- Howel ap Meruagh
- Wrenok ap Ieuan

The document was dated at Cardiff.

### What this gives us

Secure regional evidence for forms equivalent to:

- Llywelyn
- Iefor/Ifor
- Hywel
- Dafydd/David
- Ieuan
- Meurig
- Madog/Madoc

and for the use of a descriptive byname such as **Fychan/Vachan** ('younger/small').

### What it does not give us

It does **not** prove that a man with any of these exact names lived in Ystradyfodwg in 1349.

---

## 4. Plague-era Welsh names in documentary material

William Rees's study of the Black Death in Wales quotes and identifies individuals in plague-period records with names including forms such as:

- Madoc ap Ririd
- Griffith ap Madoc
- Llewelyn
- Jeuan/Ieuan
- David ap Madoc
- Wladus/Gwladus

Again, location must be checked before reusing any exact historic person.

These records reinforce that our fictional family should sound Welsh without needing stereotypical modern Welsh surnames.

## 7. Ystradyfodwg / Tyfodwg

The place-name Ystradyfodwg is associated with Tyfodwg/Dyfodwg in medieval and later tradition.

However, the saint's history is not straightforward enough to turn legend into certainty. Modern scholarly saint databases mark Tyfodwg's feast day as **unknown**, although later antiquarian tradition gives 25 June.

### Novel rule

The family can know the **place-name and local religious tradition** without the narrator asserting uncertain saint biography as fact.

---

## 8. Maerdy and Bodrhyngallt: administration fossilised in place-names

Heneb highlights two particularly useful Rhondda place-name survivals:

- **Maerdy** preserves the idea of a medieval reeve/steward's house or administrative settlement;
- **Bodrhyngallt** preserves the name of a `rhyngyll`, an official known from Welsh law traditions.

This matters because it reminds us the valley was not socially unstructured. Even a dispersed pastoral landscape had administration, obligations and remembered centres of authority.

### Novel use

The boy may grow up knowing places by their practical/social significance without understanding the marcher bureaucracy above them.

---

## 9. Llantrisant as the outside administrative world

Coflein records Llantrisant as the administrative centre for Meisgyn and Glynrhondda. It had around 150 burgages in 1307 and possibly more than 200 by 1349.

That makes Llantrisant the strongest nearby setting for:

- officials;
- markets/trade;
- legal business;
- lordship administration;
- news from beyond the valleys;
- military connections.

The family need not visit often for Llantrisant to loom large in their understanding of authority.

---

## 10. Naming rules to lock before characters are created

1. No modern fixed surname for the protagonist unless a specific social/documentary reason is found.
2. His everyday identity should be Welsh and patronymic.
3. Spellings in narration should favour readable modern Welsh forms while documentary spellings can differ.
4. Do not make every man `Dafydd`, `Rhys` or `Llywelyn`; variety matters even when common names recur.
5. Do not assign a famous historical lineage to the protagonist.
6. A descriptive byname may emerge later in life — especially in military service — but should be earned organically.
7. The eventual knightly form of his name may differ from what his mother called him at fourteen.

That last point could become emotionally powerful: the world may eventually know a knight by an anglicised or formalised name while his memories preserve the Welsh name spoken at home.
