# Pass One: Landscape and Settlement

## 1. Political geography in 1349

The boy's home region should be called **Glynrhondda** in the research bible, rather than assuming modern town identities. Heneb's Rhondda historic-landscape study describes Glynrhondda as one of the medieval territorial units of the Glamorgan uplands. Richard de Clare seized Meisgyn and Glynrhondda in 1246; by the later 1330s marcher rule over the area was well established. The territory was absorbed into the Lordship of Glamorgan and ruled by the de Clares and their successors.

This makes the novel's opening world politically Welsh in population and language, but under a powerful marcher lordship whose administrative and military networks connect the valley to Llantrisant, Cardiff and the wider English crown.

## 2. What the Rhondda looked like

The modern industrial Rhondda must be mentally removed. The medieval landscape was dominated by:

- the deep valleys of the Rhondda Fawr and Rhondda Fach;
- wooded steeper slopes, particularly in parts of the Fach;
- open upland pasture and ridgeways;
- scattered permanent lower holdings;
- seasonal upland settlement;
- irregular fields and stock enclosures;
- streams, rivers, wet ground and steep changes in elevation.

The surviving medieval settlement archaeology is strongest on the uplands because later industry destroyed or buried much valley-floor evidence.

## 3. Hendre and hafod

Heneb identifies medieval platform houses, longhuts and **hafodau** across the Rhondda uplands. These are strongly associated with pastoral farming, especially cattle, and in many cases seasonal summer occupation.

For the novel, the safest reconstruction is:

- a more permanent lower/winter holding or settlement in the valley system;
- access to upland grazing and possibly a seasonal hut/platform-house site;
- regular movement of animals and people between lower and upper ground.

Do not assume every family personally owned two substantial houses. The hendre–hafod system is a landscape/economic pattern, not a licence to give a poor family an implausibly large property portfolio.

Known medieval upland settlement locations cited by Heneb include Carn-y-wiwer, Craig Tir Llaethdy, Craig Rhondda-fach, Cwm Cesig, Cwm Lan, Mynydd Ty'n-tyle, Nant-y-Gwiddon, Mynydd y Gelli, Twyn Disgwylfa, Cwm and Nant Saerbren, Garreg Lwyd/Blaenrhondda, Blaenycwm and several named hafod sites.

## 4. Ystradyfodwg and Llanwynno

During the medieval period Glynrhondda and Meisgyn were connected ecclesiastically to Llantrisant. Heneb identifies Ystradyfodwg and Llanwynno as medieval church centres within this system.

Ystradyfodwg is especially useful because the surrounding river terraces offer comparatively flatter agricultural ground and are associated with a dense pattern of earlier and later settlement evidence. The boy can plausibly know this church as a major fixed point in his world.

## 5. Penrhys

Penrhys is one of the strongest real locations for the novel.

In the medieval period it contained a Cistercian monastic grange associated with Llantarnam Abbey, together with:

- a chapel;
- a Marian shrine;
- the healing well Ffynnon Fair;
- a hostelry;
- access to an established ridgeway route.

Heneb states that the Cistercians had practised large-scale sheep farming there but, from the early fourteenth century, divided the manor into more than thirty holdings and leased the land.

### Story implications

Penrhys can credibly function as:

- a place where strangers enter the boy's otherwise local world;
- a source of rumours from outside the valley;
- a religious destination during fear and sickness;
- a location where healing belief, desperation and plague panic intersect;
- a ridge crossing between the two Rhondda valleys.

We do **not** yet have evidence that plague victims specifically travelled to Penrhys in 1349. If they do so in the novel, that is plausible fiction and must be treated as such.

## 6. Castell Nos

Cadw classifies Castell Nos, north of Maerdy at the head of the Rhondda Fach, as a medieval defensive motte. It is unusual because a natural Pennant Sandstone outcrop was artificially scarped and steepened rather than an entirely artificial mound being raised.

Its position commands the Glamorgan side of the watershed toward Brecon and controls a major northern approach into the Glamorgan uplands. Cadw notes that it may have been used after the 1246 seizure of Glyn Rhondda by de Clare.

This gives the northern Rhondda a genuine medieval strategic dimension.

## 7. Ynysygrug / Ynyscrug

Heneb also identifies the remains of a medieval motte at Ynysygrug in the lower Rhondda landscape, probably connected with early Norman incursions. Much was later damaged by railway construction.

The novel does not need to turn every castle into an active garrison. Their existence matters because they show that routes and control of the valley were historically significant.

## 8. Roads and movement

The biggest modern trap is assuming that important travel always followed today's valley roads.

Heneb records long-lived ridgeway routes linking medieval and earlier sites. Penrhys was positioned with access to such a route, while Castell Nos watched a northern watershed approach. Valley-bottom arterial roads only became dominant later.

For the novel:

- climbing can be the normal way to get somewhere;
- ridge travel may be preferable to wet or obstructed valley floors;
- fog, rain and exposure make upland movement dangerous;
- the boy should possess intense local route knowledge before he has any wider geographical knowledge.

## 9. Agricultural character

The archaeological picture is strongly pastoral. Cattle are particularly important in the medieval upland evidence, with sheep also significant, especially on monastic estates. Mixed farming existed, but the Rhondda's topography makes livestock central.

A poor free tenant household can plausibly be involved in:

- tending cattle;
- some sheep;
- hay and fodder work;
- small-scale cultivation on suitable ground;
- maintaining boundaries and enclosures;
- gathering woodland products;
- seasonal movement to pasture;
- transport or occasional horse work if their status and obligations support it.

## 10. Working location for the protagonist

**Not yet locked**, but the strongest current option is a fictional holding in the Rhondda Fawr within the orbit of Ystradyfodwg and Penrhys.

Advantages:

- real church centre nearby;
- access to Penrhys shrine and travellers;
- realistic ridgeway escape routes;
- agricultural land on river terraces plus upland pasture;
- proximity to documented medieval platform-house landscapes;
- ability to look back across the valley during the plague and visually register the disappearance of hearth smoke.

The exact fictional holding should be chosen only after Pass One mapping is complete.
