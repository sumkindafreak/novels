# Pass One: Parish, Faith and Penrhys

This file tightens the religious geography of the opening chapters. The key distinction is between the **local parochial church at Ystradyfodwg** and the **Cistercian chapel/shrine at Penrhys**.

---

## 1. The ecclesiastical framework

Heneb's historic-landscape work places medieval Glynrhondda and Meisgyn inside the great parish of Llantrisant, thought to have developed from the reorganisation of the Diocese of Llandaff under Bishop Urban in the early twelfth century.

Llantrisant administered the territory through a network that included:

- Llantrisant itself;
- Ystradyfodwg;
- Llanwynno;
- Llanilltud Faerdre.

For the novel, this means the boy's religious world has **layers**. He does not live in an isolated pagan-ish valley that occasionally sees a priest. He belongs to a Christian parish landscape with local and wider centres.

---

## 2. Ystradyfodwg: the family's normal church centre

Heneb identifies Ton Pentre as a medieval and later parochial centre. The nineteenth-century church there was rebuilt on the site of the earlier medieval building.

A modern archaeological study of a small font associated with Penrhys states that the Penrhys grange lay within the huge Llantrisant parish in the part served by the **parochial chapelry of Ystradyfodwg**, and that the Ystradyfodwg church had the right to:

- baptise;
- marry;
- bury local people.

### Novel implication

Unless the protagonist's exact home is eventually placed nearer another legitimate church centre, **Ystradyfodwg should be the default place for his family's baptisms, marriages, funerals and ordinary parish obligations**.

The exact medieval dedication of the church requires caution. Later sources associate the site with St John the Baptist and local tradition with Tyfodwg. Do not casually write a definite fourteenth-century dedication until the evidence is stronger.

---

## 3. Penrhys is different

Penrhys was a Cistercian grange associated with Llantarnam Abbey and developed as a Marian religious site.

Documented/strongly supported features include:

- monastic grange;
- chapel;
- Marian shrine;
- Ffynnon Fair (St Mary's Well);
- hostelry/hospitality for travellers and pilgrims;
- ridgeway access;
- more than thirty leased holdings after the Cistercians reduced direct sheep farming in the early fourteenth century.

### Crucial distinction

The Penrhys chapel should **not** simply replace the parish church in the novel. Cistercian rules did not normally make an outlying grange chapel a parish baptismal centre.

Penrhys is instead ideal for:

- pilgrimage;
- prayer for healing;
- travellers bringing rumours;
- offerings;
- Marian devotion;
- desperate families seeking supernatural help;
- the collision between established religion and folk expectation during plague.

---

## 4. How certain is Penrhys pilgrimage in 1349?

The site was certainly a medieval religious centre and grange. Its great fame as a pilgrimage destination is especially well documented in the **fifteenth century**, when Welsh poets praised the shrine and pilgrims travelled there.

### Novel rule

We may use Penrhys as an existing sacred destination in 1349, but should be careful about writing it as already possessing the exact scale and celebrity documented a century later.

A few travellers or local supplicants are much safer than claiming crowds from across Britain unless further fourteenth-century evidence is found.

---

## 5. The well and healing

Ffynnon Fair's healing reputation makes it important to the plague story, but we must distinguish:

- medieval Christian devotion and healing belief;
- later folklore;
- our fictional characters' desperate interpretation of events.

A plague-stricken family might plausibly pray, make an offering, seek holy water or travel to a respected sacred place without the narrator declaring any remedy medically effective.

This gives the novel a powerful contrast:

**the reader understands infection; the characters understand sin, providence, saints, prayer, holy places and inherited remedies.**

Neither should be mocked by the narrative.

---

## 6. Clergy during plague

The family should know clergy through ordinary Christian life before the catastrophe. The plague then places priests and chaplains under the same mortality pressure as everybody else.

Do not write the local priest as a modern epidemiologist or as a caricatured fanatic.

Plausible priestly duties during crisis include:

- confession;
- Eucharist/last rites according to period practice;
- funerary prayer;
- organising or witnessing burial;
- preaching repentance and intercession;
- caring for frightened parishioners;
- becoming ill himself.

The horror is stronger if religious institutions continue trying to function and **fail through human exhaustion and death**, rather than everyone instantly abandoning ritual.

---

## 7. Saints and feast days: caution

Tyfodwg/Dyfodwg is strongly associated with the place-name and later local religious tradition.

However:

- the modern Cult of Saints in Wales database gives his feast day as **unknown**;
- later antiquarian tradition assigns **25 June**.

Therefore 25 June must not yet be treated as a securely observed local feast in 1349.

Likewise, later traditions concerning St Gwynno need medieval liturgical verification before becoming fixed calendar events.

Safe universal Western-Christian dates such as Christmas, Easter, Lent and major Marian feasts can be used once the **1349 Julian calendar dates** are calculated accurately.

---

## 8. Burial geography

If the family lives within the Ystradyfodwg parochial area, the local churchyard is the most natural initial burial focus.

As mortality rises, pressure can plausibly produce:

- graves opened faster;
- abbreviated attendance;
- fewer surviving neighbours to carry bodies;
- clergy overwhelmed or absent;
- multiple burials on the same day;
- families forced to improvise while still trying to remain within Christian expectations.

Do not default to anonymous mass pits without specific local evidence.

---

## 9. Story opportunities now grounded enough to use

### The first rumour
A traveller lodging or stopping near Penrhys carries frightening news from outside Glynrhondda.

### The first plea
When a family member becomes ill, somebody urges the household to seek help/prayer at Penrhys or Ffynnon Fair.

### The empty church
After repeated deaths, the boy visits the Ystradyfodwg church expecting the familiar priest/ritual and finds the system breaking down.

### The ridge
Later, alone, he passes Penrhys and remembers it first as a place adults believed could help them.

These are story uses, not claims about named historical incidents.
