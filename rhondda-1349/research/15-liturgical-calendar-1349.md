# Pass One: Liturgical Calendar for 1349

This file gives the protagonist's family a **real Christian year**, rather than generic medieval religious scenery.

Dates below use the **Julian calendar**, which was the calendar in use in fourteenth-century Wales.

The movable cycle is anchored by a securely calculated/independently tabulated Easter date:

> **Easter Sunday, 12 April 1349 (Julian calendar).**

A medieval regnal-calendar resource lists Easter in the 23rd regnal year of Edward III as 12 April 1349. This is also corroborated by independent historical material describing Easter 1349 on that date.

Sources:
- Medieval Genealogy, regnal calendar: https://www.medievalgenealogy.org.uk/cal/reg11.htm
- Historical discussion independently using Easter 1349 = 12 April.

Evidence rating: **A for the Easter date.**

# 2. Corpus Christi: usable, but with local caution

The Feast of Corpus Christi had been adopted in England before our story year.

Cambridge scholarship cites an English monastic record stating that in **1318** the feast was first celebrated by the whole English Church. Wider scholarship places its rapid adoption after the Council of Vienne (1311).

Therefore Corpus Christi in 1349 is not an anachronism.

However, we have **not** yet found evidence for the exact form of Corpus Christi observance at Ystradyfodwg in 1349.

### Novel rule

We may use the feast as part of the wider ecclesiastical calendar, but should not invent a huge local procession, elaborate guild pageant or drama cycle at Ystradyfodwg without evidence.

Source:
- Claire Kennan, Cambridge University Press, discussion of the earliest English celebration: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/creativity-contradictions-and-commemoration-in-the-reign-of-richard-ii/power-piety-and-presence-the-cult-of-corpus-christi-and-the-1389-guild-enquiry-in-lincolnshire/FBA6AA3A511E9C9D8D5D0D5F047866B8

# 4. The most important story consequence: one last Easter

Our current plague model has:

- rumours growing in **March–April 1349**;
- local outbreak planning default in **May 1349**.

Easter falls on **12 April**.

This gives us a historically natural emotional structure:

## Lent — February/March

Rumours of mortality elsewhere can arrive during a season already centred on:

- fasting;
- repentance;
- confession;
- mortality;
- Christ's suffering.

The characters do not need to interpret rumours scientifically. Their religious calendar already gives them a vocabulary for fear, sin, suffering and divine judgement/providence.

## Holy Week / Easter — early April

The family can attend the Ystradyfodwg church together for what will unknowingly be their final Easter as a complete family.

This should **not** be written as ominous prophecy from the narrator. To them it is simply Easter.

That ordinary happiness becomes tragic only in retrospect.

## May — mortality enters their immediate world

If first local cases emerge in May, they can collide with:

- Rogationtide;
- Ascension;
- Whitsun/Pentecost;

exactly while fields, cattle and spring work are accelerating.

A church calendar associated with renewal and resurrection becomes juxtaposed against increasingly visible death.

This is historically generated structure, not a fabricated symbolic calendar.

---

# 5. Rogationtide is especially useful

Rogation days immediately before Ascension traditionally involved prayers for God's protection and blessing upon crops/land.

In a rural community this can provide strong period texture.

If plague is appearing locally around **18–21 May**, a normal agricultural/religious observance can become emotionally transformed:

- prayer for crops coexists with prayer for sick neighbours;
- routes/boundaries familiar from ordinary life become associated with fear;
- the priest's workload begins escalating;
- some households may refuse or be unable to participate.

### Caution

The exact Ystradyfodwg Rogation route is not documented. Any detailed route/procession must be fictional reconstruction.

---

# 6. All Souls in the aftermath

If the protagonist survives into autumn, **2 November 1349** has enormous potential.

By then he may have lost:

- father;
- mother;
- sister;
- neighbours;
- possibly the familiar priest or other adults.

All Souls is therefore not merely atmospheric decoration. It belongs to a medieval religious culture of prayer for the departed and can reveal how the boy continues relating to his dead family.

Do not make him suddenly modern/secular because tragedy occurred. Trauma may deepen, distort, anger or complicate belief without erasing the cultural framework in which he understands death.

---

# 7. How medieval characters tell time

The novel does not need characters constantly saying modern-style dates such as “the twenty-first of May.”

More natural temporal anchors can include:

- before/after Easter;
- in Lent;
- Holy Week;
- after Whitsun;
- around Michaelmas;
- before All Saints;
- a certain number of days/weeks after a feast;
- agricultural tasks such as haymaking or harvest.

Official narration/chapter headings can still use modern-readable dates for clarity while characters inhabit a feast-and-season calendar.

# 8. Current proposed opening chronology using the liturgical year

This remains planning, not final chapter architecture.

### Late February 1349

Lent begins on **25 February**. Household life is still winter-lean.

### March

Rumours from outside Glamorgan become harder to ignore. The Annunciation falls on **25 March**.

### 5–12 April

Holy Week and Easter. The family is still intact.

### Late April / early May

News reaches closer communities; travel and market gossip turn fearful.

### Mid-May

First local sickness/death that the boy personally recognises as extraordinary.

### 18–21 May

Rogation/Ascension period. Community religious life is under pressure.

### Late May

Father's illness and the household catastrophe can begin here if Pass Two confirms the sequence.

### 31 May

Whitsunday. Depending on the final death chronology, this may fall during the family's collapse or immediately after it.

### June

Hay season approaches while labour disappears; the boy and sister's survival phase begins.

This sequence is powerful enough that **May 1349 is now a very strong candidate for formal story lock**, though Pass Two should make the final decision.

# 9. Evidence grades

### A

- Easter Sunday = 12 April 1349 (Julian).
- Fixed major feast dates.
- movable dates calculated from the Easter cycle.
- Corpus Christi was known/celebrated in England by the early fourteenth century.

### B

- these major observances structure an ordinary Welsh Christian household's year;
- seasonal religious language provides everyday date markers.

### C

- precise local ceremonies;
- exact attendance by the protagonist's family;
- exact Rogation route;
- any specific sermon, procession or community response.

Pass Two may use these C-level details conservatively once the family is built.
