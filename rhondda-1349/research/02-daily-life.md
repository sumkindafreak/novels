# Pass One: Daily Life

## 1. Household status

Current working model: the protagonist's family are **poor free Welsh tenants**, not destitute wanderers and not secret gentry.

This gives the family:

- obligations and rent;
- access to land and animals without making them wealthy;
- practical agricultural skills;
- possessions worth losing;
- a realistic reason for the boy to know livestock, tools, boundaries, weather and local routes.

This status remains provisional until the Glamorgan tenure evidence is pushed further.

## 2. Housing — what we know and what we do not

Direct evidence for the exact domestic form of a poor Glynrhondda household in 1349 is limited because later development destroyed much valley-floor archaeology.

Strong regional/period evidence does support:

- timber construction;
- wattle-and-daub walling in many Welsh buildings;
- open hearths;
- limited or unglazed openings;
- livestock kept very close to people, and in later documented Welsh longhouses under the same roof;
- smoke, soot and persistent damp as normal domestic realities.

Museum Wales preserves later Welsh longhouses such as Cilewent (first built 1470) and Hendre'r-ywydd Uchaf (1508), where human and animal spaces shared one structure. These are useful comparators, **not direct blueprints for a Rhondda house in 1349**.

### Safe fiction choice

Give the family a modest timber/wattle-and-daub dwelling with an open hearth and a very limited number of functional spaces, plus immediate animal/storage structures. The boy's separate sleeping place caused by bed-wetting can be an attached/byre-end space, loft or nearby outbuilding only after we choose a layout that fits the final local reconstruction.

Do not give the house modern bedrooms.

## 3. Bedding and sleep

Likely materials include:

- straw or other plant bedding;
- woollen blankets where affordable;
- shared sleeping spaces;
- clothing retained during cold weather;
- frequent presence of lice/fleas/other ectoparasites as a realistic risk.

The bed-wetting plot works best if soiled bedding is an expensive nuisance: straw needs changing, cloth needs washing/drying, and his accident can affect everyone in cramped sleeping conditions.

## 4. Food — staple logic

The family should eat from agriculture first and wild gathering second.

Comparable medieval archaeobotanical sites in Britain show crops including:

- wheat;
- barley;
- rye;
- oats;
- peas;
- field beans;
- vetches;
- hazelnuts.

The exact crop mix in upland Glamorgan requires caution. Oats/barley and hardy pulses may fit conditions better than portraying every household as awash with fine wheat bread.

Useful everyday foods for a modest household can include:

- coarse bread or grain-based pottage;
- oats/barley preparations;
- peas and beans;
- onions, leeks and garlic where available;
- dairy foods;
- eggs;
- bacon or other preserved meat when available;
- ale;
- seasonal nuts, berries, greens and crab apples or similar wild foods where locally appropriate.

A fourteenth-century shepherd-food description cited in Historic England's animal-remains review includes bread, bacon, onions, garlic, leeks, butter, green cheese, ale, meat, pudding, sheep's head and curds. This is useful for the texture of ordinary food, though it is not a Rhondda household inventory.

## 5. Plague survival food

After mass mortality, the boy's immediate survival should depend heavily on **stored and abandoned food**, not romantic year-round foraging.

Historically grounded opportunities include:

- grain left in deserted houses;
- dried peas/beans;
- dairy animals;
- loose chickens and eggs;
- livestock whose owners have died;
- preserved meat if he finds it before spoilage;
- abandoned tools, vessels and blankets.

Welsh Black Death court evidence records people entering houses after deaths and stealing household goods, three oxen and three cows. That strongly supports scavenging as part of the social reality of the plague.

The moral question matters: a fourteen-year-old Christian boy may understand that theft is sinful while also knowing the dead cannot eat the grain.

## 6. Water

Do not portray every stream as magically safe. The Rhondda gives abundant water, but the boy still needs practical judgement about:

- upstream animal contamination;
- stagnant versus moving water;
- flood water;
- proximity to corpses/settlement waste;
- boiling when fuel and vessels allow.

The novel should avoid modern germ-theory language in his thoughts.

## 7. Animals

Cattle should be central to the opening world. Sheep are also plausible and important, especially around Penrhys and upland estates. Poultry, pigs and working horses/ponies may appear depending on household wealth and local arrangements.

Animals are not scenery. They require:

- grazing;
- fodder;
- watering;
- milking;
- hoof/skin care;
- protection from straying/theft;
- slaughter/butchery knowledge.

A surviving child inheriting animals after adult deaths may suddenly possess wealth he cannot practically manage.

## 8. Clothing

By the mid-fourteenth century, ordinary clothing in Britain was dominated by wool, with linen used for shirts/undergarments where available. Museum Wales' reconstruction of a Welsh longbowman uses a linen shirt, woollen outer clothing and hose, hood/coif and leather ankle boots; its livery reference is tied to Welsh bowmen in Black Prince service in 1346.

For the fourteen-year-old rural boy, keep things simpler and poorer:

- linen or coarse under-shirt if affordable;
- woollen tunic;
- hose/leg coverings;
- hood;
- leather shoes/boots, repaired repeatedly;
- belt with small practical items.

Avoid pristine costume-shop clothing. Patches, grease, smoke, mud, sweat and repairs matter.

## 9. Money

The basic money of account is pounds, shillings and pence:

- 12 pence (d) = 1 shilling (s)
- 20 shillings = £1

Silver pennies remain the core everyday coin. The National Archives notes that Edward III introduced the gold noble in 1344 at 6s 8d and that the silver groat circulated in significant numbers from 1351 at 4d.

A poor rural teenager should usually think in very small sums and practical value rather than carrying purses of gold.

## 10. Work at fourteen

A fourteen-year-old in this world is not socially treated as a modern schoolchild. He can credibly have years of experience helping with:

- livestock;
- fetching water/fuel;
- field work;
- carrying and repair tasks;
- maintaining fire;
- food processing;
- watching younger children;
- walking substantial local distances.

This is important: plague survival should still overwhelm him, but he must begin with believable competence rather than becoming instantly skilled because the plot needs it.

## 11. Seasonal survival logic

The story should track the agricultural calendar. Future chapter planning must account for:

- lambing/calving where relevant;
- spring hunger before new harvests;
- hay/fodder preparation;
- harvest;
- autumn slaughter/preservation;
- winter fuel and feed;
- shorter daylight and wet/cold exposure.

Pass One will later convert this into a month-by-month calendar once the opening month of the local plague sequence is responsibly chosen.
