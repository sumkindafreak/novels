# Pass One: Travel, Routes and Isolation

The modern Rhondda trains the eye to think **along the valley floor**. The medieval landscape often worked differently. Ridgeways, steep cross-valley tracks and pack/foot routes matter much more to this novel than modern roads.

---

## 1. The basic rule

Do not draw medieval travel by tracing today's A-roads backwards.

Heneb identifies:

- long-lived upland/ridgeway routes;
- Penrhys positioned on a ridge route;
- Castell Nos overlooking a northern watershed approach toward Brecon;
- Ystradyfodwg as an important crossing point in the old route network.

Later accounts of the pre-industrial valley likewise remember goods being moved by packhorse and people taking direct mountain routes rather than following engineered valley roads.

Those later memories are **supporting texture**, not proof that every path existed in identical form in 1349.

---

## 2. Why the uplands matter

A valley floor can be:

- wet;
- flood-prone;
- wooded;
- cut by streams;
- awkward for carts;
- difficult to cross where bridges are absent.

A ridge route can be:

- exposed to wind and fog;
- steep to reach;
- physically punishing;
- but comparatively direct and easier to follow across country.

This gives the novel a distinctive movement language: **to escape the valley, the boy often has to climb first.**

---

## 3. Penrhys as a crossing point

Penrhys sits high between the Rhondda Fawr and Fach and had access to an established route network.

Safe uses:

- crossing between the two Rhondda valleys;
- meeting travellers;
- seeing smoke/weather over large areas;
- seeking the shrine/well;
- using the ridge as a navigation landmark.

Modern reconstructed pilgrimage routes from Llandaff/Cardiff to Penrhys are useful for understanding broad geographic relationships but **must not be copied wholesale as an exact 1349 path**.

---

## 4. Ystradyfodwg and the Rhondda Fawr

RCT heritage material describes Ystradyfodwg as an important crossing point for old Rhondda routes, including a route over Penrhys toward the Rhondda Fach.

For the protagonist, this supports a home area in the wider Ystradyfodwg/Gelli orbit because it combines:

- a church centre;
- river-terrace settlement;
- access to upland farms/hafodau;
- a cross-valley route;
- plausible contact with people travelling through rather than only along the settlement.

---

## 5. North: Castell Nos and the Brecon approach

Cadw describes Castell Nos at the head of the Rhondda Fach as commanding the Glamorgan side of a watershed route toward Brecon and one of the principal northern approaches into the uplands.

This does **not** mean there was a paved highway full of traffic.

It does mean the head of the Fach was strategically meaningful because movement over the watershed mattered.

Story uses later in the novel can include:

- rumours from the north;
- drovers/pack animals;
- armed men using known upland approaches;
- the boy learning that his valley is connected to a much larger world by paths he once considered local.

---

## 6. South: Llantrisant

Llantrisant was the administrative centre for Meisgyn and Glynrhondda and had a functioning medieval borough and castle.

This makes it the most important southern anchor before Cardiff.

A journey toward Llantrisant can plausibly mean contact with:

- borough inhabitants;
- traders;
- officials;
- castle personnel;
- legal/financial business;
- military news.

The exact route and travel time from the protagonist's final home site must be calculated **after the home site is locked**.

---

## 7. Cardiff and Llandaff

Cardiff is the major lordship centre further south. Llandaff is a major ecclesiastical centre.

They should feel psychologically far away to a fourteen-year-old whose normal world is a few miles of valley, upland and parish — even if experienced travellers can connect those places by established routes.

That difference between **actual distance** and **experienced distance** is important.

A boy who knows every stream and fold in a five-mile radius can still regard Cardiff as another world.

---

## 8. Travel speed — do not over-mechanise

Do not use a single universal 'medieval walking speed'.

Travel time changes with:

- gradient;
- mud;
- river crossings;
- fog;
- load carried;
- animal condition;
- whether the traveller knows the route;
- daylight;
- sickness/injury;
- fear of other people.

For plotting, calculate distances and elevation profiles later, then assign realistic **ranges**, not stopwatch-perfect times.

---

## 9. Isolation during plague

The Rhondda's scattered settlement pattern creates a different plague horror from a dense town.

Possible effects:

- a family can be dead for days before neighbours know;
- smoke from hearths becomes a visual sign of life;
- church bells carry farther than voices;
- a blocked/abandoned route can cut off information;
- a traveller can introduce terrifying news before visible local sickness appears;
- the boy may cross an apparently empty landscape containing occupied houses whose inhabitants are too ill or afraid to emerge.

This is one of the strongest reasons to keep the opening **rural and local** instead of beginning in Cardiff.

---

## 10. Mapping tasks still open

Before Pass One is complete we should build a working map containing:

- Ystradyfodwg medieval church site;
- Penrhys grange/shrine/well;
- Ynysygrug;
- Castell Nos;
- known platform-house/hafod concentrations;
- likely river crossings;
- Llantrisant;
- broad route corridors south and north;
- candidate fictional home zones.

The map should distinguish **known sites** from **reconstructed corridors**.
