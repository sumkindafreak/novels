# Pass One: Evidence-Graded Character Name Pool

This file does **not** choose the protagonist or family names.

Its purpose is to prevent us from reaching Pass Two and selecting names merely because they “sound Welsh” or “sound medieval.”

The pool below prioritises forms documented in medieval Welsh or South-East Welsh evidence close enough to the fourteenth century to be useful.

---

# 1. Naming system

A Welsh-speaking tenant family in Glynrhondda should normally be represented through **patronymic naming**, not a modern hereditary surname.

Examples:

- **Ieuan ap Dafydd** — Ieuan, son of Dafydd
- **Llywelyn ap Hywel** — Llywelyn, son of Hywel
- **Gwladus ferch Madog** — Gwladus, daughter of Madog

`ap` = son of.

`ferch` = daughter of.

Historical records may Latinise, Anglicise or vary spellings considerably. The form in a clerk's roll is not automatically how the person pronounced his or her name at home.

### Novel rule

Use readable Welsh forms in narrative/dialogue unless documentary spelling is itself relevant to a scene.

Do not give the family a modern inherited surname such as Jones, Davies or Williams.

# 2. Strong male name pool: South-East Welsh evidence

A 1317 Glamorgan political document preserves a group of Welsh witnesses whose names provide an excellent near-period South-East Welsh pattern.

Recorded forms include people represented as:

- **Llywelyn ap Ifor**
- **Hywel ap Dafydd**
- **Hywel ap Ieuan**
- **Meurig ap Madog ap Hywel**

These names are not Glynrhondda tenant names, and we must never claim they are.

# 3. Female name breakthrough: Wladus / Gwladus

William Rees's study of Black Death court records quotes a Ruthin court-roll case involving **Wladus**, widow of David ap Madoc.

David died in the pestilence after holding native land plus three acres of free land. Because he had no heirs, Wladus was permitted to retain the holding for life while performing the services due from it.

This is exceptionally useful for us because it proves:

- the female name in a genuine plague-era Welsh administrative record;
- a Welsh widow dealing directly with tenure after plague mortality;
- the sort of legal/economic disruption our fictional world is built around.

Source:
- William Rees, “The Black Death in Wales,” *Transactions of the Royal Historical Society*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/transactions-of-the-royal-historical-society/article/black-death-in-wales/9D28A0E282A273EC8DBF62FD05DD50B6

### Caution

This court was at Ruthin in north-east Wales, **not Glynrhondda**.

Therefore:

- **A** for the name existing in plague-era Wales;
- **B** for using Gwladus as a plausible Welsh woman’s name in Glamorgan;
- not evidence for a specific Glynrhondda woman.

### Normalised story form

**Gwladus** is a suitable readable Welsh form.

`Wladus` may appear in documentary/Latinised transcription.

# 4. Gwenllian

**Gwenllian** is securely medieval and remained a major Welsh female name.

The Dictionary of Medieval Names from European Sources notes both its Welsh origin and a famous fourteenth-century bearer: **Gwenllian ferch Llywelyn**, daughter of Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the last native Prince of Wales. She lived from 1282 to 1337, placing the name directly in the generation immediately preceding our novel.

Source:
- Dictionary of Medieval Names from European Sources: https://dmnes.org/name/Gwenllian

### Evidence grade

- **A** as an authentic medieval Welsh female name.
- **B** for an ordinary Glamorgan tenant family's use; popularity was broad, but we do not yet possess a 1349 Glynrhondda example.

This is a particularly strong candidate for the younger sister if we want a recognisably Welsh name without using a modern invention.

No choice is locked.

# 5. Angharad

**Angharad** is a long-established Welsh female name and is described by the Dictionary of Medieval Names from European Sources as a popular Welsh name with deep medieval literary and dynastic use.

Source:
- https://dmnes.org/name/Angharad

### Evidence grade

- **A** as a medieval Welsh name generally.
- **B/C** for a specific Glynrhondda tenant woman in 1349 until better local records appear.

It remains usable, but Gwladus currently has stronger direct fourteenth-century documentary anchoring.

# 6. Margred / Margaret

South Wales/Glamorgan evidence gives us a useful near-exact date.

A local-history transcription drawing on *Cartae et alia Munimenta quae ad Dominium de Glamorgan pertinent* records **Lady Margaret de Avene** alive in her widowhood in **1349–50**.

The de Avene/Afan family belonged to the Welsh-descended lordly society of Glamorgan.

The same material records **Maud**, wife of Thomas de Avene, around 1349–50.

Source:
- Llantrisant & District Local History Society material citing *Cartae Glamorgan*: https://www.talygarn.co.uk/onewebmedia/M%26G_Vol_I_No_4.pdf

### Caution

These women are socially elite and documentary forms are shaped by Anglo-Norman/Latin record culture.

Their names therefore prove period/local presence but do not tell us how frequently a poor Welsh tenant family used them.

### Welsh form

**Margred** is a historically Welsh form of Margaret and appears repeatedly in medieval Welsh genealogy/record traditions.

Evidence grade:

- **A/B** for Margaret/Maud in mid-fourteenth-century Glamorgan elite society;
- **B** for Margred as a medieval Welsh form;
- **C** for choosing it for this fictional tenant household without local tenant records.

# 8. Avoid accidental modern surname logic

If father is **Dafydd ap Hywel**, his children are not automatically “Dafydd” or “Hywel” as surnames.

For example:

Father:
> Dafydd ap Hywel

Son:
> Ieuan ap Dafydd

Daughter:
> Gwenllian ferch Dafydd

Mother may be:
> Gwladus ferch Madog

In intimate local speech, people may simply call them Ieuan, Gwenllian and Gwladus unless identification requires the patronymic.

This gives dialogue far less clumsy exposition than having everybody constantly recite a lineage.

---

# 9. Clerks may alter the names

The boy's identity can later change on the page as his world expands.

A Welsh-speaking child known locally as **Ieuan ap Dafydd** might encounter clerks who render Ieuan as forms corresponding to **John/Johannes**, or spell Welsh patronymics inconsistently.

This becomes especially useful when he enters marcher/English military society.

Do not imply that the Welsh form was merely a nickname for an English 'real name.'

Different linguistic systems are rendering the same person.

# 11. Naming-tone recommendation

Avoid making every character bear the most famous/romantic Welsh name available.

The protagonist should feel like **one ordinary fourteen-year-old among many**, before history destroys that ordinary life.

A name such as **Ieuan ap Dafydd** or **Madog ap Hywel** may serve the novel better than something selected merely for heroic resonance.

The eventual knightly form of his name can then become an interesting linguistic/social development rather than a fantasy-title reveal.
