# Rhys ap Llywelyn

## Core identity

**Name:** Rhys ap Llywelyn  
**Age in 1349:** about 34  
**Role:** practical stock/horse/transport servant connected to the wider Llantrisant estate-administration world  
**Status:** fictional character inside a historically grounded post-plague administrative/economic setting

Rhys is the first durable relationship Ieuan forms after the deaths of his family.

He is **not** a replacement father, knight, secret nobleman or master-at-arms.

His importance is that he gives Ieuan access to a larger working world.

---

# Background

Rhys comes from modest working stock himself and has spent years around:

- cattle;
- pack animals;
- riding horses;
- estate routes;
- markets;
- servants and officials;
- practical transport between upland districts and Llantrisant.

He is more travelled than Ieuan but not cosmopolitan.

He can function around clerks and minor officials without belonging to their social class.

---

# Losses

Rhys has already known grief before meeting Ieuan.

Working lock:

- his wife died several years before 1349, probably through ordinary illness/childbearing circumstances rather than plague;
- they had no surviving child;
- the 1349 mortality takes **Rhys's younger brother** and at least one close workmate/relative.

This gives him a personal reason to understand Ieuan's silence without making their tragedies identical.

Rhys does not narrate this history on first meeting.

Ieuan learns pieces over months.

---

# Why Rhys keeps working

Rhys is not emotionally untouched.

He continues because:

- animals still require handling;
- estate/transport work still pays;
- food and shelter remain tied to service;
- work gives structure to days that would otherwise be filled by grief;
- the death of so many workers makes somebody competent suddenly more valuable.

This mirrors one of the novel's central themes:

> ordinary labour continues while the world is broken.

---

# Temperament

Rhys is:

- patient with animals;
- less patient with people;
- observant;
- practical;
- capable of rough humour;
- unimpressed by self-pity, including his own;
- not casually violent;
- comfortable giving orders;
- suspicious of people who talk more than they work.

He has a strong sense of hierarchy but understands that usefulness often matters more day-to-day than birth within the servant world.

---

# Relationship with Ieuan

## First stage — useful boy

Rhys initially sees:

- local knowledge;
- cattle competence;
- hunger;
- stubbornness;
- a boy trying very hard to appear older than fourteen.

He does not ask Ieuan to tell him about his feelings.

This is probably one reason Ieuan can tolerate him.

## Second stage — worker

Rhys gives Ieuan tasks whose success can be judged clearly:

- hold this animal;
- bring that rope;
- take this track;
- watch the pack pony;
- count the cattle;
- clean this tack;
- be here before daylight.

Ieuan responds well because work restores structure.

## Third stage — trust

Rhys eventually gives Ieuan responsibility without standing over him.

That echoes Dafydd's old form of praise.

Rhys may never realise why this means so much.

---

# Horses

Rhys is the foundation of Ieuan's horse education.

His rules are practical:

- feed before vanity;
- feet matter;
- frightened animals hurt people;
- expensive tack does not fix bad handling;
- watch ears, weight and breathing;
- never assume a horse cares about your rank.

Ieuan's cattle instincts help, but Rhys repeatedly corrects him because horses are not cows.

This prevents Ieuan from acquiring implausible instant horsemanship.

---

# The bed-wetting discovery

Rhys should probably become the **first non-family person to discover Ieuan's wetting**.

This is an important Pass Three character beat.

Working approach:

- it happens after Ieuan has spent considerable energy hiding accidents in a shared stable/service environment;
- Rhys notices because he notices straw, smells, routines and the fact that Ieuan is cleaning bedding before dawn;
- Ieuan expects ridicule or dismissal;
- Rhys's response is practical rather than tender.

Something in the spirit of:

> Change it, then. The horse does not care.

Exact dialogue is not locked.

Rhys may quietly arrange a less exposed sleeping place without making a public issue of it.

This moment matters because it is the first evidence Ieuan receives that **another adult can know and still judge him by his work**.

Do not make Rhys unrealistically enlightened in modern language. He can still find the problem inconvenient or unusual while refusing cruelty.

---

# Conflict

Rhys and Ieuan should argue.

Main sources:

- Ieuan's pride;
- refusal to admit ignorance;
- resentment at being treated as a child;
- attempts to work while sick/injured;
- reluctance to abandon impossible responsibilities in Glynrhondda;
- later ambition when Ieuan begins seeing martial service as a route upward.

Rhys may eventually warn Ieuan that men in armour are not automatically better men.

Ieuan will need to learn that himself.

---

# Relationship to status

Rhys knows the social ceiling above him.

He is competent enough to move around people of higher rank but has no fantasy that competence makes him their equal.

Ieuan later begins to cross boundaries Rhys never expected to cross.

This can produce complicated pride:

- Rhys is genuinely pleased;
- slightly amused;
- sometimes irritated by Ieuan's new airs;
- aware that the boy may leave his world permanently.

No melodramatic jealousy is required.

---

# Long-term use

Rhys can plausibly remain important through the early/middle novel.

Possible functions:

- brings Ieuan into Llantrisant;
- teaches horse/stable work;
- recommends him into a stronger household/retinue when labour opportunity appears;
- becomes somebody Ieuan seeks out after his first military experiences;
- survives long enough to see that the starving fourteen-year-old became something neither expected.

Rhys does **not** need to accompany Ieuan to Poitiers.

Keeping him in Wales/Glamorgan may actually make the widening of Ieuan's world clearer.

---

# Memory object

Rhys does not need a sentimental heirloom equivalent to Dafydd's knife.

His recurring object can simply be a **short horse/stock rope** he automatically coils correctly whenever thinking.

Ieuan eventually copies the habit.

This is the kind of influence that happens without speeches.

---

# Evidence grading

- practical estate/transport servants and continued post-plague administration: **A/B historical foundation**
- specific role, age and biography: **C fiction**
- horse-handling apprenticeship: **C fiction, strong practical plausibility**
- bed-wetting discovery/reaction: **C character scene**
- relationship to Ieuan's social rise: **C story architecture**
