# Ieuan's First Month Alone — 7 June to Early July 1349

This file defines the survival and emotional progression immediately after Gwenllian's death. It is a **chapter-planning chronology**, not a claim that these exact events happened in historical Glynrhondda.

## Historical foundation

The survival model rests on:

- the real medieval upland cattle/hafod economy of South Wales;
- archaeological evidence for modest native Welsh platform-house communities;
- Welsh Black Death records showing abandoned tenements, dead tenants, livestock and theft/scavenging from plague-vacated houses;
- the early-summer agricultural calendar: grazing improves, dairying is active, haymaking becomes urgent, while the new cereal harvest is still some distance away.

Ieuan survives because he already possesses ordinary rural competencies. He does **not** suddenly become a wilderness expert.

---

# 7 June — Gwenllian dies

**Working age:** Ieuan 14, Gwenllian 8.

Gwenllian dies at or near the familiar upland cattle shelter where Ieuan took her after Angharad's death.

Ieuan has no adult help.

His first problem is not food. It is **what to do with his sister's body**.

## Burial choice

Ieuan understands that proper burial belongs in consecrated ground at Ystradyfodwg. He is also terrified of returning to the infected settlement, cannot safely carry an eight-year-old body down difficult ground alone for long, and has no guarantee that a priest or burial party remains available.

Working story choice:

- he wraps Gwenllian as carefully as he can;
- tries briefly to convince himself he can carry her toward the church;
- fails physically and emotionally;
- buries her near the upland refuge instead;
- marks the grave with stones and/or a simple wooden marker;
- says the prayers he knows imperfectly.

This is **C — fiction**, but psychologically and religiously plausible.

The important consequence is guilt:

> Ieuan believes he has failed his sister by leaving her outside consecrated ground.

That guilt can remain for years.

Do not turn the burial into an elegant ceremonial scene. A fourteen-year-old digging alone into difficult ground is exhausted, frightened and probably produces a grave he considers inadequate.

---

# 8–10 June — waiting to die

Ieuan does very little.

He has now watched the disease take:

1. Dafydd;
2. Angharad;
3. Gwenllian.

He assumes his own illness is merely late.

Behaviour:

- repeatedly feels his neck, armpits and groin for swellings;
- interprets ordinary aches as the beginning;
- sleeps badly;
- eats almost nothing;
- keeps close to Gwenllian's grave;
- avoids the lower valley;
- prays inconsistently — sometimes pleading, sometimes angry, sometimes unable to pray at all.

He is not calmly planning a new life.

He is **waiting for symptoms**.

## Bed-wetting beat

During one of these nights he wets his bedding again.

His first response is rage and humiliation even though nobody is there to see it.

Then comes the stranger realisation:

> his body is still doing the same stupid thing it did before everyone died.

The accident becomes a tiny, unwanted sign of continuity.

He does **not** conclude that urine has protected him at this point.

---

# 10–12 June — thirst and hunger win

Basic bodily need eventually overrides shock.

He already knows where reliable flowing water lies near the upland working landscape.

Food available to him may include:

- whatever bread/grain food was carried uphill;
- a little cheese/curd/butter if the family had provisions at the seasonal site;
- milk **only if** he can recover/manage a suitable cow;
- eggs only if he later returns lower and finds surviving poultry;
- stored food at the family holding, which requires him to go back.

Wild berries are **not** a June miracle food supply. Foraging remains supplemental.

## First major decision

He cannot remain beside Gwenllian's grave with the small amount of carried food.

He must return toward the human world.

This is the first thing he chooses after her death that is not simply grief.

---

# 12–14 June — return to the family holding

This should be one of the main horror sequences of the early novel.

Ieuan descends into a landscape he knows intimately, but ordinary signs of life are missing:

- no smoke from some holdings;
- doors standing open;
- loose animals;
- no voices from fields;
- church activity drastically reduced or silent;
- carrion/scavenging animals beginning to exploit what people have left behind.

Do not make every house visibly full of corpses. **Absence** is more frightening.

## His own house

Angharad died only days before the children fled.

Working choice:

Ieuan was unable to arrange her proper burial before leaving with Gwenllian. On returning he must confront the consequences.

This scene should be written with restraint rather than gore. Heat, flies, smell and the changed atmosphere of the dwelling do more work than explicit decomposition detail.

He cannot meaningfully resume living there.

He retrieves essentials and leaves.

### Things he takes

Priority items:

- Dafydd's work knife;
- a cooking vessel;
- grain/oats/barley or dried peas/beans if stores remain;
- salt if available;
- a blanket or woollen covering;
- cord/rope;
- fire-making materials;
- a small axe or other genuinely manageable tool if the household owned one;
- clothing;
- perhaps a wooden bowl/spoon;
- a sack or cloth for carrying food.

He cannot carry the entire household.

That limitation matters.

---

# 14–18 June — the first scavenging

Once Ieuan has taken from his own home, the next moral barrier is entering somebody else's.

The strongest candidate is **Madog ap Rhys's abandoned holding**.

Welsh Black Death court records explicitly document men entering houses after plague deaths and stealing household goods, oxen and cows. Scavenging abandoned property is therefore grounded in real plague-era behaviour.

Ieuan still understands it as theft.

He may initially justify taking only what will spoil:

- food;
- milk;
- eggs;
- a loose animal.

Then practicality expands the boundary.

He eventually takes an object that will not spoil — perhaps a whetstone, pot, cord, or tool — and knows he has crossed the line he set for himself.

That is a useful moral step.

---

# Livestock problem

Ieuan cannot realistically manage every abandoned animal he encounters.

He should face:

- cows needing milking;
- animals straying through damaged/open boundaries;
- cattle becoming difficult without routine handling;
- pigs/chickens scavenging freely;
- animals whose owners may return, making 'ownership' uncertain;
- fodder and future winter planning he cannot possibly manage alone.

## Working survival choice

He concentrates on **one manageable dairy cow** already familiar to him, rather than becoming lord of a giant post-apocalyptic herd.

Because medieval Welsh upland summer husbandry involved young people caring for cattle and producing butter/cheese at hafod sites, basic milking/dairy competence is plausible for Ieuan.

He can still make mistakes.

He knows more about tending the cow than preserving a large surplus of milk alone.

---

# 18–21 June — hay becomes a warning about the future

The grass is growing.

The days are long.

In any normal year adults would be thinking seriously about hay and winter fodder.

Ieuan realises something terrible:

**surviving June is not the same as surviving January.**

One fourteen-year-old cannot cut, dry, carry and stack enough hay for every loose animal.

This forces him to begin choosing:

- which animal to keep;
- which to release/abandon;
- what work is essential;
- what parts of his old life are impossible to preserve.

This is an important maturation step: survival means deliberately letting things go.

---

# 21–24 June — first human encounter after isolation

Do not leave Ieuan literally without seeing a person for months.

The plague world contains survivors, fugitives, opportunists and people moving between damaged communities.

His first encounter should frighten him more than comfort him.

Working options:

- a desperate survivor searching an abandoned holding;
- a frightened woman/child passing toward kin elsewhere;
- someone trying to claim livestock;
- an estate servant assessing what remains.

Current recommendation: **a stranger or near-stranger scavenging**, not yet the person who draws Ieuan into service.

This teaches Ieuan that other living people may now be competitors.

---

# 24–29 June — routine begins

For the first time, Ieuan develops a routine that belongs only to him.

Morning:

- check the cow/animals;
- water;
- fire;
- inspect food;
- check himself for signs of illness, even though this behaviour is slowly reducing.

Day:

- gather fuel;
- carry supplies;
- search known holdings cautiously;
- repair the upland shelter;
- begin limited hay/fodder work;
- retrieve useful items from lower ground.

Evening:

- cook;
- maintain knife/tools;
- listen for distant human activity;
- return psychologically to Gwenllian's grave.

He talks aloud more than he realises.

Sometimes to Gwenllian.

Sometimes to Dafydd.

Sometimes to the cow.

---

# 29 June–early July — the survivor changes

By the end of the first month, Ieuan is not 'healed'.

He has changed in smaller, more believable ways:

- he stops assuming every ache is plague;
- he knows exactly how much food he has;
- he sleeps lightly;
- he notices smoke immediately;
- he studies strangers before approaching;
- he has begun stealing/scavenging without pretending the distinction is simple;
- he can kill or butcher small food animals if absolutely necessary, but does not enjoy it;
- he keeps Dafydd's knife obsessively close;
- Gwenllian's grave remains his emotional centre.

Most importantly:

> he has stopped waiting to die.

That is the achievement of the first month.

Not bravery.

Not knighthood.

**The decision — mostly unconscious — to continue.**

---

# Things he must NOT be able to do yet

To preserve credibility, Ieuan should not suddenly:

- hunt deer expertly;
- make perfect cheese alone on first attempt;
- forge tools;
- fight trained men;
- read maps;
- know military tactics;
- identify plague scientifically;
- live indefinitely on berries;
- manage a large herd alone;
- become emotionally unaffected by corpses.

His competence is narrow, practical and earned from his upbringing.

# Evidence notes

**A/B foundation:**

- Cambridge / William Rees, *The Black Death in Wales*: abandoned holdings, dead tenants, livestock/property scavenging.
- Heneb/CPAT historic-landscape material: summer upland cattle tending, hafod occupation, dairy production.
- Coflein/Heneb Gelligaer evidence: small, materially poor medieval upland platform-house communities dated broadly to the 13th–14th centuries.

**C story construction:** all specific dates, Gwenllian's upland burial, Ieuan's movements, specific scavenged objects and the sequence of emotional decisions.
