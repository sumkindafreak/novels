# Pass Three: The Middle Rung — Household Servant to Man-at-Arms

This file solves the hardest social problem in the novel:

> How can Ieuan move from a low-status horse/transport servant into recognised armed service without pretending medieval society was meritocratic?

## Conclusion

The strongest route is **not** `groom -> squire overnight`.

It is:

**working boy -> groom/service lad -> valet/yeoman-level household servant -> armed household servant -> campaign-proven retainer -> esquire/man-at-arms**.

That progression is exceptional for Ieuan's birth but fits the structure of fourteenth-century households and armies far better than a conventional page-to-squire story.

---

# 1. Household grades provide a real ladder

Fourteenth-century elite households used graded lay service. Terminology varies by source and date, but the relevant hierarchy includes:

- groom (`garcio` / groom);
- valet/yeoman (`valettus`);
- esquire (`scutifer` / `armiger`);
- knight.

The categories were not simply modern job titles. They combined:

- social standing;
- household position;
- pay/allowances;
- proximity to the lord;
- military expectation.

Promotion between household ranks was possible.

This matters because Ieuan can rise **inside one service network** rather than being magically admitted into a separate 'knight school'.

### Historical comparisons

- Geoffrey Chaucer moved through household service from page/valet status into esquire status later in the fourteenth century.
- Roger Beauchamp spent years in royal service before being retained as a household knight.
- Peter de Brewes was knighted on campaign at Crecy after already belonging to the military-household world.

None is a direct social twin for Ieuan. They prove the importance of **service, familiarity and patronage** in advancement.

Evidence grade: **A/B for household structure; C for Ieuan's exact promotions.**

---

# 2. Men-at-arms were not synonymous with knights

By Edward III's reign, English armies were increasingly organised around paid retinues.

The two major fighting categories were:

- **men-at-arms** — normally mounted for movement and heavily armoured, commonly paid around 12d per day;
- **archers** — frequently mounted for movement, commonly paid around 6d per day, less for foot service in some contexts.

A man-at-arms could socially be:

- a knight;
- an esquire;
- another accepted armoured retainer functioning at that level.

Royal Armouries summarises the Hundred Years War man-at-arms as a full-armour close-combat soldier, with many holding the social status of esquire.

### Novel implication

Ieuan does **not** need to be knighted before he can fight beside knights.

He needs to become accepted as an armoured household retainer/man-at-arms first.

That distinction is the key to making 1356 work.

Evidence grade: **A/B.**

---

# 3. Social mobility through military service was possible — but not normal

Later-fourteenth-century career evidence gives examples of military promotion.

Robert de Fishlake, for example, served repeatedly as an archer before later appearing as a man-at-arms. The Medieval Soldier project explicitly treats his career as evidence that military social mobility was possible.

Andrew Ayton's work on the fourteenth-century 'military careerist' stresses that expanding war employment, retinue structures and the socio-economic consequences of the Black Death created fertile conditions for professional military careers.

### Caution

Fishlake's career is later than Ieuan's and took longer.

We use it to prove **possibility**, not to claim Ieuan's rapid rise was ordinary.

Ieuan still requires:

- an unusually committed patron;
- several years of household service;
- campaign experience;
- appropriate equipment;
- acceptance by other armed retainers.

Evidence grade: **B comparison.**

---

# 4. Final working promotion sequence

## 1349 — age 14

**Status:** labouring survivor / Rhys's stock helper.

No formal household rank.

He is useful because he understands cattle, terrain and work.

---

## 1350 — age 15

**Status:** groom/service boy in the Llantrisant administrative orbit.

Tasks:

- cattle and pack animals;
- stable cleaning;
- grooming;
- loading;
- leading animals;
- errands;
- sleeping with lower servants.

He is still socially ordinary.

---

## 1351–52 — ages 16–17

**Status:** groom moving toward **valet/yeoman-level household service** under a knightly patron.

He is trusted with:

- better horses;
- saddles/tack;
- travelling parties;
- messages;
- military equipment;
- helping an armed man prepare to ride.

His social improvement comes from **being retained and trusted**, not from his birth changing.

---

## 1353–54 — ages 18–19

**Status:** experienced `valettus` / mounted household servant.

This is where combat preparation becomes serious.

He learns:

- riding in formation/travel parties;
- spear/lance handling basics;
- sword as a sidearm;
- armour maintenance;
- helping a knight/esquire arm and disarm;
- guard and escort work;
- obedience under threat.

He may wear increasingly useful protective equipment supplied, loaned or enabled through the household.

He is **not yet described as an esquire** in planning.

---

## Spring–summer 1355 — age 20

**Status:** trusted mounted valet/armed household servant.

His patron is involved in the Welsh mobilisation and supply effort for the Gascony campaign.

Ieuan is selected because he has now spent years proving he can:

- handle horses;
- move supplies;
- work in poor weather;
- obey;
- travel long distances;
- remain useful when exhausted.

He goes to Gascony inside his patron's wider retinue network.

Crucially, he does **not** embark as a freshly minted glamorous esquire.

---

## Autumn 1355 campaign

**Status:** campaign household servant / armed retainer.

The grande chevauchee becomes his practical examination.

He experiences:

- extreme march distances;
- horse losses/injury;
- violent contact;
- burned settlements;
- guard duty;
- supply pressure;
- disorder;
- sleeping among armed strangers;
- maintaining equipment day after day.

If he performs well here, the patron has evidence for advancement that other men in the household can recognise.

---

## Winter 1355–56

**Promotion point.**

Ieuan is now about twenty.

The working story lock is that his patron promotes him into an **esquire/man-at-arms position** during the winter in Gascony.

This is the first point at which the prose may legitimately begin treating him as an esquire, subject to final terminology in the chapter plan.

The promotion must have practical consequences:

- improved pay/status;
- a suitable horse formally attached to his service;
- better armour;
- responsibility for weapons;
- expectation that he will fight in the men's battle, not merely serve behind it;
- changed seating/sleeping/social relationships;
- resentment from at least one man of better birth.

This is not a ceremonial dubbing.

It is a household/military regrading.

---

## 1356 campaign

**Status:** esquire / man-at-arms.

By Poitiers Ieuan has:

- seven years since the plague;
- roughly five years in structured household service;
- at least two years around armed duties;
- the entire 1355 campaign behind him;
- months in which his new status can become socially real.

Therefore Poitiers is **not his first day among men-at-arms**.

That is mandatory.

---

# 5. Why the Black Death matters without becoming a shortcut

Post-plague labour scarcity changes opportunity, but it does not abolish rank.

Ieuan can receive chances because:

- households need reliable servants;
- dead workers/retainers create vacancies;
- surviving young people can bargain indirectly through usefulness;
- military expansion creates additional employment.

But none of this means a tenant's son automatically becomes genteel.

The decisive force is **patronage accumulated over years**.

---

# 6. What the promotion should feel like in prose

Avoid:

> 'From this day, you are my squire.'

if it reads like a fantasy promotion scene.

Better:

- Ieuan discovers he has been entered among a different group of men;
- he receives responsibility for a fighting horse/equipment;
- somebody calls him `armiger`/esquire in a formal context before he thinks of himself that way;
- another servant resents that he now eats or sleeps with higher-status retainers;
- Matthew or the immediate patron makes clear that higher wages also mean **standing in the line when ordered**.

The promotion should initially feel more frightening than triumphant.

---

# 7. Pass Three lock

**LOCKED:** Ieuan's formal esquire/man-at-arms promotion occurs in **winter 1355–56 after the first Gascony campaign**, not several years earlier.

Before that he rises through groom/valet/armed-household service.

This is now the default route unless stronger evidence gives a better one.
