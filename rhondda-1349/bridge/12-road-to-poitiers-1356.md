# The Road to Poitiers — 1356

This file gives the final campaign movement a defensible chronological spine without pretending every mile of the army's route is certain.

Primary modern guide: Peter Hoskins, *In the Steps of the Black Prince: The Road to Poitiers, 1355–1356*.

---

# 1. Assembly after the winter

After the 1355 chevauchée, the Anglo-Gascon army spends months consolidating, resupplying, repairing equipment and conducting frontier operations.

By summer 1356 a new expedition is prepared.

## 6 July 1356

The Prince of Wales leaves **Bordeaux** for the army assembling at **La Réole**.

Hoskins estimates the assembled force at roughly **8,000–10,000** including its wider personnel, while all medieval army totals remain subject to source uncertainty.

For Ieuan, this is no longer his first campaign.

He enters the expedition as a recognised esquire/man-at-arms in Warwick's broader company.

---

# 2. La Réole to Bergerac — July

The prince reaches La Réole shortly after 6 July.

Around **22 July** the army moves north/north-east toward **Bergerac**, surprising French expectations that another southern Languedoc raid was coming.

The precise route between La Réole and Bergerac is not fully documented.

### Novel rule

Do not give Ieuan exact knowledge of an itinerary the sources themselves do not preserve.

This stage should feel like:

- assembly becoming movement;
- reconnaissance;
- supply strain returning;
- men realising the objective is not obvious.

---

# 3. Into Périgord/Limousin — late July to 14 August

From Bergerac the expedition drives north through Périgord/Limousin country.

Hoskins divides the first major 1356 movement as:

> **La Réole to Manot — 6 July to 14 August.**

The Prince later told London that his army had ridden through **Périgord and Limousin** toward Bourges, expecting to find the French king's son, the Count of Poitiers.

Ieuan's story emphasis remains:

- keeping his fighting horse sound;
- supervising/working with lower horse servants;
- water and fodder;
- destroyed countryside;
- growing confidence as a man-at-arms rather than a servant pretending to belong.

---

# 4. Across the Vienne — 14 August

Hoskins marks **Manot / the Vienne crossing** as a change in the character of the expedition.

Once the force crosses the Vienne, banners are unfurled and the campaign becomes more openly destructive/aggressive.

The army's broad objective points toward **Bourges** and the possibility of contact with French forces.

This is a good chapter-planning threshold:

> Before the Vienne: movement and uncertainty.  
> After the Vienne: everybody understands they are riding deep into hostile country.

---

# 5. Manot to Romorantin — 14 August to 5 September

Hoskins groups this phase as:

> **Manot → Romorantin, 14 August–5 September 1356.**

The route becomes a sequence of devastated settlements, captured/resisted places and manoeuvre through central France.

At **Romorantin**, the campaign pauses around a defended position and the different elements of the army regroup.

For Ieuan, this period should demonstrate that his promotion is no longer theoretical:

- he stands watch as a man-at-arms;
- serves beside Iorwerth rather than behind him;
- makes decisions affecting lower servants/horses;
- understands military orders faster than he did in 1355;
- still reacts viscerally to burned homes because of Glynrhondda.

---

# 6. Romorantin to Poitiers — 5 to 17 September

This is the final manoeuvre phase.

Hoskins deliberately frames the period as **manoeuvre**, because the strategic intentions of both sides are debated.

King Jean II is trying to bring the Anglo-Gascon army to battle under favourable conditions or cut its retreat.

Historians differ over whether the Prince is primarily:

- avoiding a pitched battle while trying to return toward Gascony; or
- willing to fight if he can obtain favourable ground.

### Novel rule

Ieuan does not know the answer.

That uncertainty belongs in the prose.

A low-ranking man-at-arms experiences:

- contradictory rumours;
- sudden route changes;
- forced marches;
- exhausted animals;
- concern over crossings;
- French forces appearing where they were not expected;
- senior decisions he does not hear explained.

---

# 7. The Loire problem and forced withdrawal

The expedition reaches the broader Tours/Loire theatre but cannot simply continue north without risk.

French forces concentrate rapidly.

The Anglo-Gascon army turns south again while Jean II attempts to cut it off.

This is the ideal point to show Ieuan realising that the army which spent weeks terrifying civilians is now itself afraid of being trapped.

---

# 8. Chauvigny / 17 September

By **17 September**, contact between forces is becoming unavoidable.

The Anglo-Gascon army moves through the **Chauvigny / Forest of Moulière** region and clashes with elements of the French army.

Exact details vary between reconstructions.

For the novel, this is the moment when Ieuan knows the coming battle is no longer a distant possibility.

---

# 9. Nouaillé-Maupertuis — 18–19 September

The Prince's force takes up the difficult position associated with the battle near **Nouaillé-Maupertuis**, south of Poitiers.

## 18 September

Negotiation and preparation.

Men:

- repair equipment;
- attend horses even though many will fight dismounted;
- confess/pray where possible;
- eat if they can;
- listen to rumours about French numbers;
- prepare to die.

This should be one of Ieuan's quietest scenes.

The plague memory can return strongly because the emotional posture is familiar:

> wait through the night to discover whether death has chosen you.

## 19 September

Battle of Poitiers.

Ieuan fights in Warwick's wider vanguard as already locked elsewhere in the bridge bible.

---

# 10. Return south — 20 September onward

The victory produces an enormous logistical burden:

- wounded men;
- prisoners;
- captured equipment;
- booty;
- horses;
- the French king himself within the Anglo-Gascon column.

Hoskins traces the return from Nouaillé-Maupertuis toward Bordeaux from **20 September to early October**.

For Ieuan, this means the story does not cut instantly from battle to ceremony.

He must:

- help keep Iorwerth alive;
- secure Guillaume's prisoner status/identity;
- manage movement south;
- live with what he has done;
- begin to understand that the prisoner may be worth more money than his family ever saw.

---

# 11. Working route anchors for Pass Four

Use these as the major geographic beats, not a claim that Ieuan visits only these places:

1. **Bordeaux** — winter base / Prince departs 6 July
2. **La Réole** — main assembly
3. **Bergerac** — northward turn
4. **Manot / Vienne region** — mid-August threshold
5. **Romorantin-Lanthenay** — early-September regrouping/operations
6. **Tours/Loire theatre** — strategic pressure / failed northern freedom
7. **Chauvigny** — French contact tightening
8. **Nouaillé-Maupertuis / Poitiers** — battle
9. **Bordeaux** — return with prisoners and wounded

The exact villages traversed between these anchors remain research-detail territory and are not necessary for every chapter.

---

# 12. Why this campaign works for Ieuan's arc

1355 taught him to function in an army.

1356 tests whether he has become part of the **armed social class** of that army.

His development is visible because:

- in 1355 he looks after other men's horses;
- in 1356 somebody else may look after his;
- in 1355 he waits behind the fighting line until ordered forward;
- in 1356 he begins in the fighting line;
- in 1355 survival earns promotion;
- in 1356 conduct determines whether he can become a knight.

# Evidence grades

- broad dates/segments from Hoskins: **A/B strong historical reconstruction from recorded itineraries**
- exact daily experience for Ieuan: **C**
- precise minor roads/camps not documented: remain intentionally unlocked.
