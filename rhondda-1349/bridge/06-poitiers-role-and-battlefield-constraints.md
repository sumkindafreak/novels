# Pass Three: Poitiers — Role and Battlefield Constraints

This file defines what the novel may safely say about the Battle of Poitiers and narrows Ieuan's likely battlefield role.

## Central rule

The battle is real and heavily documented, but the **precise deployment and sequence of some movements remain disputed** among chroniclers and modern reconstructions.

The novel should therefore build Ieuan's action around secure structural facts rather than claiming false cartographic precision.

---

# 1. Secure historical anchors

- Date: **19 September 1356**.
- Edward of Woodstock's Anglo-Gascon army was intercepted near Poitiers by King John II's substantially larger French army.
- Edward selected a strong defensive position involving hedges/ditches and difficult approaches.
- English/Welsh archers played a major role in breaking the opening French mounted attacks, including shooting horses from vulnerable angles.
- Successive French assaults became intense hand-to-hand fighting.
- King John II was captured.
- Thomas Beauchamp, Earl of Warwick, was a major commander and led the **vanguard / first battle**.

Evidence grade: **A/B depending on tactical detail.**

---

# 2. What is uncertain

Chronicle evidence does not produce one perfectly agreed modern battle map.

Modern reconstructions differ over:

- exact orientation of the hedge and road;
- precise location of every gap;
- sequence/timing of Warwick's movement across a narrow marsh/causeway;
- exact placement of reserves and divisions at different moments;
- how much of later Froissart-style detail should be accepted literally.

### Novel rule

Do not write lines such as:

> 'Ieuan stood precisely eighty yards north of the gap.'

Do not make the plot depend on one disputed modern reconstruction being certain.

Instead use sensory/local facts visible to Ieuan:

- hedge;
- ditch;
- churned earth;
- restricted gaps;
- horses struggling in bad ground;
- archers firing at close/oblique angles;
- press of bodies;
- orders passing imperfectly;
- men losing sight of commanders.

---

# 3. Warwick's vanguard is the best home for Ieuan

Ieuan enters the 1355 army through Sir Matthew de Soor's connection to **Thomas Beauchamp, Earl of Warwick**.

Matthew is securely reconstructed in Warwick's vanguard in 1355.

Warwick is securely present and commands the vanguard at Poitiers in 1356.

### Important caveat

We have **not yet found direct evidence proving Matthew de Soor himself remained with Warwick at Poitiers**.

Therefore:

- Matthew may remain Ieuan's formative patron from 1352–55;
- Ieuan's continued service in **Warwick's broader retinue/affinity** carries him into 1356;
- finished prose should not state Matthew's documented presence at Poitiers unless new evidence is found.

This is historically cleaner than turning absence of evidence into certainty.

---

# 4. Ieuan's status on 19 September 1356

By battle morning Ieuan is **not a groom**.

Pass Three currently locks him as:

- about 21;
- campaign-proven from 1355;
- promoted during winter 1355–56;
- functioning as an **esquire/man-at-arms**;
- supplied with suitable armour, horse and weapons through accumulated wages/patronage/household resources;
- recognised within Warwick's armed retinue.

He may still perform horse/equipment work because all fighting men live inside logistical reality, but his battle obligation is now to fight.

---

# 5. Best battlefield role

## Working choice: Warwick's vanguard, fighting dismounted

This is the strongest option.

Ieuan begins the main engagement **on foot among men-at-arms attached to Warwick's first battle/vanguard**.

Why it works:

- English men-at-arms frequently fought set-piece battles dismounted;
- Warwick definitely commands this formation;
- the vanguard is involved in the opening crisis and subsequent close fighting;
- Ieuan's years of service place him naturally here;
- it avoids making him an ahistorical free-roaming hero.

---

# 6. How his horse skill can still matter

Do **not** invent a moment where Ieuan personally remounts the entire army or wins Poitiers because he understands horses.

His animal skill can matter more modestly and credibly:

### Before the clash

- calming/positioning mounts during confused movement;
- getting a valuable horse through congested ground;
- noticing when frightened horses are about to break order;
- helping household men dismount and secure mounts before joining the line.

### During/after a mounted crisis

If the final battle reconstruction supports it, Ieuan may:

- retrieve a loose or wounded patron's horse;
- lead remounts through restricted ground;
- prevent an injured horse from blocking a route;
- move a wounded man away using a horse after the close fighting.

These are supporting actions, not superhero tactics.

---

# 7. The heroic deed — current strongest design

The climax should combine **three versions of Ieuan**:

1. the cattle boy who watches animals/terrain;
2. the plague survivor who acts while terrified;
3. the trained man-at-arms who now understands military discipline.

## Recommended sequence

During the violent close fighting around Warwick's division:

- a senior knight/retainer in Ieuan's immediate affinity is wounded or unhorsed/cut off;
- Ieuan stays in formation long enough not to endanger everyone merely to 'save' him;
- when ordered/when an opening develops, Ieuan and one or two others recover/protect the wounded man;
- a French knight presses the group;
- Ieuan fights him at close quarters and **takes him prisoner rather than killing him once surrender is possible**;
- Ieuan then completes the practical task of getting his wounded man/prisoner clear rather than posing over a victory.

This gives him:

- conspicuous loyalty;
- legitimate combat achievement;
- a prisoner/ransom mechanism;
- witnesses;
- behaviour his patron/Warwick network can actually reward.

It avoids a huge kill count and avoids giving him a famous historical prisoner.

---

# 8. Candidate person Ieuan saves

## Do not use King John or the Prince

Too historically central.

## Do not currently use Matthew de Soor as the wounded man

Until Matthew's 1356 presence is proven, making his injury the climax would silently claim he was at Poitiers.

## Safest solution

Create a **fictional esquire/knight inside Warwick's company** who has already been introduced during the 1355 campaign and winter.

Alternatively, use a real lesser retainer only after checking that the novel's action does not contradict known biography.

This fictional immediate superior can be the man who inherited Matthew's day-to-day patron role once Ieuan entered Warwick's broader company.

---

# 9. The prisoner

Use a **fictional French knight of respectable but not famous standing**.

The prisoner should be valuable enough that ransom materially changes Ieuan's finances, but not so valuable that history would certainly record the capture.

The prisoner plot also tests Ieuan morally:

- killing an incapacitated enemy might be easier;
- taking surrender requires restraint in chaos;
- keeping a prisoner alive creates responsibility;
- ransom transforms a human being into wealth.

This echoes the plague chapters, where Ieuan learned to see abandoned objects and livestock as survival resources, but now the 'valuable thing' is a person.

---

# 10. Knighthood must not occur in the middle of the melee

Popular traditions of spontaneous battlefield dubbing can be unreliable; later stories about Welsh figures such as Dafydd Gam demonstrate how easily knighting legends grow after the fact.

For Ieuan, the cleaner ending is **after the battle**, when:

- witnesses can describe his conduct;
- his prisoner/ransom rights can be assessed;
- patronage can operate;
- his previous esquire/man-at-arms status is recognised;
- a senior knight/noble can decide the accolade is socially supportable.

The emotional scene is stronger if he has time to understand that the victory has created a new problem: somebody now thinks he should become a knight.

---

# 11. Edward le Despenser

Edward le Despenser is independently recorded as present at Poitiers in biographical/chronicle-derived material.

He remains an excellent **higher-level thematic connection** because:

- he is lord of Glamorgan;
- he is almost Ieuan's age;
- both began 1349 as boys in the same lordship at radically different social extremes;
- he is in the same army in 1355–56.

But his precise battlefield position/action should not be invented beyond evidence.

### Working ending possibility

Ieuan's immediate Warwick patron and/or Warwick himself recommends him.

Edward le Despenser, already aware of the Welsh Glamorgan esquire through the campaign, may participate in or perform the accolade **after** Poitiers.

This remains a story option, not yet final lock.

---

# 12. Visual rule

Later medieval illuminations of Poitiers are useful for understanding how later medieval artists imagined battle and armour, but they are **not eyewitness photographs of 1356**.

Do not copy a fifteenth-century illumination's armour or layout uncritically into the novel.

---

# Pass Three lock

**LOCKED:** Ieuan fights at Poitiers as a dismounted esquire/man-at-arms in the wider **Warwick vanguard**.

**WORKING:** his conspicuous deed combines protecting/recovering a wounded superior with taking a non-famous French knight prisoner.

**UNLOCKED:**

- exact fictional immediate superior;
- exact prisoner;
- exact tactical location within Warwick's formation;
- who performs the final knighting;
- whether Matthew de Soor can be independently proven at Poitiers.
