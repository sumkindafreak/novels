# Pass Three Character: Iorwerth ap Morgan

**Status:** fictional  
**Role:** Ieuan's immediate armed-service superior / professional mentor in Warwick's company  
**Age in winter 1355–56:** about 30  
**Social origin:** minor Welsh uchelwr / landed family from elsewhere in Glamorgan  
**Rank:** established esquire / man-at-arms

Iorwerth exists to solve a structural problem without hijacking real biography.

Sir Matthew de Soor is the historically anchored man who opens the military network to Ieuan. Iorwerth is the fictional man who can safely occupy the intimate day-to-day role across 1355–56 and be wounded at Poitiers without falsifying a real person's history.

---

# 1. Background

Iorwerth comes from a **minor Welsh landed family within the wider Glamorgan lordship**, socially well above Ieuan's tenant household but far below magnates such as Despenser or Warwick.

Do not give him a famous pedigree or secretly make him prince-descended unless later story needs it.

His position comes from:

- family standing;
- patronage;
- prior armed service;
- horse/equipment resources;
- familiarity with marcher/English military structures.

By 1355 he has already served enough to be treated without question as an esquire/man-at-arms.

This makes him an important contrast:

> He and Ieuan are both Welsh, but medieval class still places a large distance between them.

---

# 2. Language

Iorwerth speaks Welsh naturally.

He is also much more comfortable than Ieuan with:

- English household speech;
- Anglo-French / French military and elite vocabulary;
- formal rank etiquette.

He can switch language according to audience almost without thinking.

Ieuan initially finds this irritating because it exposes how provincial he still feels.

---

# 3. Temperament

Iorwerth is:

- controlled;
- dry;
- difficult to impress;
- attentive to equipment;
- better at reading men than Ieuan is;
- entirely willing to retreat when ordered;
- contemptuous of pointless showing-off;
- aware that being brave and being useful are not identical.

He is not especially warm.

He does not become Ieuan's substitute father.

Their eventual affection is mostly disguised as work criticism.

---

# 4. First impression of Ieuan

Iorwerth initially sees Ieuan as:

> Matthew's very capable horse boy who has begun carrying weapons.

He dislikes Ieuan's sensitivity about status.

He notices that Ieuan:

- works harder when he thinks somebody doubts him;
- refuses help too often;
- watches animals before people;
- becomes dangerously stubborn when embarrassed.

He deliberately orders Ieuan to perform ordinary horse work even after armed duties begin, partly because it is necessary and partly because he wants to know whether Ieuan believes promotion has made him too important to work.

Ieuan passes that test while resenting every second of it.

---

# 5. 1355 campaign

Iorwerth is attached to the same broader Warwick company as Matthew de Soor.

He becomes Ieuan's immediate armed supervisor during the grande chevauchee.

His rules are simple:

- do not chase plunder beyond orders;
- do not abandon the horse you are responsible for;
- do not break formation to prove bravery;
- do not draw a weapon unless willing to finish what follows;
- do not lie about equipment damage.

## Late-November incident

Iorwerth is one of the witnesses to Ieuan's promotion-defining return-march incident.

He sees that Ieuan:

- solves the horse/route blockage;
- obeys under pressure;
- fights when necessary;
- does not pursue after the threat;
- returns immediately to the job.

His report/support helps make Ieuan's winter promotion possible.

---

# 6. Winter 1355–56

Iorwerth becomes formally responsible for part of Ieuan's transition into man-at-arms service.

He helps with:

- armour fit;
- lance handling;
- mounted movement;
- when to dismount;
- formation discipline;
- treatment of prisoners;
- household etiquette among armed retainers.

He refuses to teach Ieuan as though the boy knows nothing.

If Ieuan already knows a practical solution, Iorwerth lets him use it.

That mutual respect is important.

---

# 7. The wetting discovery

During winter quarters Ieuan wets his bedding after a long dry period.

Iorwerth discovers the situation before Ieuan can fully conceal it.

Ieuan expects:

- ridicule;
- gossip;
- loss of status;
- the destruction of six years of advancement.

Iorwerth instead treats it as a logistical nuisance.

Working tone:

> 'Is that why you're standing there like you've murdered somebody?'
>
> Ieuan says nothing.
>
> Iorwerth looks at the bedding.
>
> 'Change it before it freezes.'

Then he moves on to the day's work.

### Rule

Do not turn this into a modern reassurance speech.

The power is that **Iorwerth does not consider the information relevant to whether Ieuan can do his job**.

He also does not tell others.

This changes something fundamental in Ieuan:

For the first time, a man outside his family knows the secret and Ieuan's life continues.

---

# 8. Early 1356

Iorwerth and Ieuan serve together in smaller frontier/garrison operations.

This establishes that they can fight/work together before Poitiers.

Iorwerth begins to use Ieuan for:

- independent short tasks;
- supervising a groom;
- prisoner escort;
- watches;
- remount decisions;
- situations where he needs someone who will not panic when confronted with bodies/illness.

He eventually stops thinking of Ieuan as Matthew's horse servant.

He thinks of him as **one of his men**.

---

# 9. Poitiers

Iorwerth fights with Ieuan in Warwick's wider vanguard.

During the close fighting he is **seriously wounded**, but not instantly incapacitated in a melodramatic fashion.

Ieuan's climax includes:

- maintaining formation rather than immediately abandoning his place;
- reaching Iorwerth when an opportunity/order permits;
- protecting him with one or two others;
- engaging a French knight who threatens/cuts into the group;
- accepting that knight's surrender;
- getting Iorwerth and the prisoner clear.

Iorwerth is therefore a witness to Ieuan's behaviour but may be too badly hurt to do much immediately afterward.

His testimony later matters.

---

# 10. After Poitiers

Iorwerth should **survive**.

Killing him after using him as the wounded superior is emotionally predictable and would repeat too much of Ieuan's earlier loss pattern.

Instead:

- his recovery is uncertain for a while;
- he remains alive to describe what Ieuan did;
- he finds Ieuan's potential knighthood faintly absurd and entirely deserved at the same time.

He may also be one of the first people to make Ieuan understand that becoming a knight creates financial/social obligations he has not yet considered.

---

# 11. Why Iorwerth should remain an esquire

Iorwerth does not need to become a knight during this novel.

That is useful thematically.

He can be:

- better born;
- more experienced;
- socially polished;

and still remain an esquire while Ieuan, through an extraordinary combination of Poitiers service, ransom wealth and high patronage, is elevated.

This prevents the book from implying that knighthood is simply the next automatic military promotion.

It may also create a moment of discomfort for Ieuan:

> Why him, when Iorwerth has been this man for years?

Iorwerth's answer need not be philosophical.

Something like:

> 'Because you have the money now, you fool.'

captures the material reality better.

---

# 12. Relationship rule

Iorwerth and Ieuan never become openly sentimental.

Their strongest expressions of trust are practical:

- handing over a horse without checking Ieuan's work;
- sleeping while Ieuan is on watch;
- giving Ieuan responsibility for another man;
- keeping the wetting secret without ever discussing the promise;
- Ieuan refusing to leave him at Poitiers.

---
