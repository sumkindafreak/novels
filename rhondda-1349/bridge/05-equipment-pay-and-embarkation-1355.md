# Pass Three: Equipment, Pay and Embarkation — 1355

This file turns Ieuan's social promotion into practical military reality.

A man does not become a man-at-arms merely because somebody calls him one. He needs the horse, armour, weapons, wages and household backing to function in that rank.

---

# 1. Pay grades

English armies under Edward III increasingly distinguish two principal paid combat categories:

## Man-at-arms

Typical later-Hundred-Years-War rate rooted in Edward III's system:

- about **12d (1 shilling) per day**.

Men-at-arms were normally mounted for strategic movement and fought in substantial armour, frequently dismounting for battle.

Socially they included knights and esquires.

## Archer

Typical mounted-archer rate:

- about **6d per day**;

with lower rates possible for foot service in some contexts.

### Story implication

When Ieuan moves into man-at-arms status in winter 1355–56, the change has measurable financial consequences.

It is not merely a nicer title.

Evidence grade: **A/B general Edward III system.**

---

# 2. The horse problem

Men-at-arms require appropriate horses and are expected to operate as mounted soldiers even when a set battle is fought on foot.

Royal Armouries notes that fighting men were responsible for obtaining/maintaining expensive horses; fourteenth-century royal service also used **restaurum equorum** — horse restoration/compensation — where qualifying military horses were appraised and losses could be reimbursed.

The Gascon Roll introduction for 1355 explicitly notes that horses were appraised before departure so that their cost could be repaid if lost during the expedition.

### Novel solution

Ieuan does not save enough pennies as a groom to walk into a horse market and purchase an elite destrier.

Instead:

1. his knightly patron advances/rewards him with a suitable campaign horse from the household network;
2. the horse is good enough for mounted man-at-arms service but need not be a fabulously expensive tournament animal;
3. Ieuan's promotion therefore creates a **financial debt/obligation to patronage** as well as honour;
4. by 1356 the animal must be treated as military property/wealth, not a pet.

The exact ownership mechanism remains C-level fiction, but gifts, allowances, saddles and horse replacement were real forms of household/military reward.

---

# 3. Armour progression

Do not give Ieuan a complete late-fifteenth-century white harness.

Mid-fourteenth-century equipment can plausibly build around:

- padded textile armour / arming garment;
- mail protection;
- bascinet or similar helmet, increasingly favoured during this period;
- mail aventail protecting neck/throat;
- plate reinforcement at vulnerable limbs/joints where status/resources permit;
- gauntlets;
- practical sword and lance/spear;
- dagger/knife.

Royal Armouries holds/cites mid-fourteenth-century mail, gauntlets and poleyns and describes the bascinet's rise during the Hundred Years War.

### Ieuan's equipment arc

## 1352

He maintains other men's equipment.

## 1353–54

He receives/uses basic protective kit for escort work.

## 1355 campaign

As an armed valet he has enough protection to survive duties around a military retinue, but is not equipped like a wealthy knight.

## Winter 1355–56

Promotion to man-at-arms requires a real upgrade:

- better helmet;
- meaningful mail/plate protection;
- appropriate fighting horse;
- lance/spear and sword.

Some may be second-hand household equipment.

This is more plausible and emotionally richer than presenting him with a gleaming matched suit.

---

# 4. The 1355 horse-transport system

The Gascon Rolls preserve extremely concrete preparation for the expedition.

On **6 May 1355** sheriffs were ordered to provide thousands of wooden hurdles and long gangplanks for the transport of the Prince's and other soldiers' horses.

These hurdles created shipboard horse stalls.

This gives us an excellent scene texture:

- horses led up gangplanks;
- temporary wooden partitions;
- frightened animals on moving decks;
- manure and urine below deck/deck level;
- constant watering/feeding problems;
- men assigned to horses throughout the passage.

Ieuan's entire upbringing makes him particularly useful here.

---

# 5. Departure and crossing

Modern scholarship gives a strong chronology for the Prince's main force:

- departure from **Plymouth: 9 September 1355**;
- arrival in the Gironde: **20 September**;
- passage: about **eleven days**.

Several horses were lost during the voyage and compensation was paid in at least some cases.

### Warwick complication

Some evidence indicates that Warwick/Suffolk retinue shipping also used Southampton, while the Prince's main fleet gathered at Plymouth.

Because Matthew de Soor is associated with Warwick's vanguard, the **exact embarkation port for Ieuan must remain unlocked** until the retinue shipping evidence is reconciled.

Do not automatically put every soldier at Plymouth simply because the Prince sailed from there.

### Story lock for now

- September 1355 sea passage to Bordeaux/Gironde;
- exact English port for Matthew/Ieuan: **research open**.

---

# 6. Why the sea voyage matters to Ieuan

Ieuan has grown up in an inland Welsh valley.

The sea should therefore be genuinely alien.

His first great journey contains an irony:

**he is less frightened of France than of the ship carrying him there.**

Plausible reactions:

- seasickness;
- inability to sleep because horses move/panic around him;
- instinctively prioritising a horse's footing before his own comfort;
- disgust at wasted drinking water/fodder;
- surprise at the scale of the fleet;
- no romantic 'first sight of the ocean' speech — he is working.

His experience managing frightened cattle/animals under bad conditions gives him value even before combat begins.

---

# 7. Bordeaux recovery

The army did not immediately launch the chevauchee after landing.

Madden notes that the horses needed time to recover after the voyage and final preparations were made rapidly in/around Bordeaux.

This gives Ieuan:

- first foreign-city experience;
- exhausted horses to walk/feed/check;
- exposure to Gascon speech and unfamiliar food/wine culture;
- an early lesson that military glory begins with sore hooves, damaged tack and accounting.

---

# 8. Story use of pay and equipment

Ieuan should remain obsessive about the value of things.

He grew up in a household where a knife, blanket or cow mattered.

Therefore military wealth shocks him:

- one horse may represent more wealth than his family ever controlled;
- armour requires continuous work;
- a lost saddle matters;
- dead men leave equipment worth fortunes to somebody of Ieuan's background;
- ransom/booty later becomes comprehensible because he already understands objects as survival value.

His father's little work knife remains emotionally more important than the expensive sword he eventually carries.

---

# 9. Pass Three equipment lock

By September 1356, Ieuan must possess **the functional package of a man-at-arms** through patronage, wages and accumulated service:

- suitable campaign horse;
- substantial mid-fourteenth-century armour;
- lance/spear;
- sword/sidearm;
- recognised place in an armed retinue.

The novel should show the gradual acquisition rather than reveal it all at Poitiers.
