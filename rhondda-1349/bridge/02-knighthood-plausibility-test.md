# Knighthood Plausibility Test

The novel's ending works only if Ieuan's knighthood feels **exceptional but possible**.

This file defines the conditions that must be met before the story is allowed to knight him.

---

# 1. The problem

Ieuan is born the son of a modest Welsh tenant.

Fourteenth-century knighthood is not simply a military qualification.

It carries:

- social/genteel status;
- expectations of military equipment and horses;
- patronage;
- financial cost;
- public reputation;
- association with landholding/wealth and the upper ranks of armed society.

Scholarship on medieval English knighthood warns that men of genuinely low birth were **rarely** elevated.

Therefore 'he was brave in battle' is not enough.

---

# 2. What helps us

## Military service is changing

By the mid-fourteenth century English warfare increasingly uses contracted retinues and professional/semiprofessional soldiers.

Men-at-arms include both knights and esquires, while household service is a major route into military networks.

## Esquire is becoming a recognised grade

The fourteenth century sees clearer distinction between knights and esquires.

Household/military association could make an individual's social identity more genteel over time even when his origins were humbler than the traditional knightly class.

Some secondary scholarship explicitly notes that squires' backgrounds could range from landed to urban or even peasant, though this should be treated cautiously and tested against stronger specialist work before final prose claims it as normal.

## War can transform wealth

Poitiers produced large quantities of booty and prisoners.

Froissart gives an especially useful example of a squire who became a knight after the ransom/profit from a prisoner at Poitiers transformed his means.

This shows that battlefield success could change the economic conditions surrounding knighthood.

---

# 3. Mandatory conditions for Ieuan

Ieuan may be knighted only if **all** of the following are true in the final outline.

## Condition A — years of household service

Minimum working period:

**1349–1355: roughly six years** of increasingly responsible service before Poitiers.

He cannot be a recent recruit.

## Condition B — horse competence

Ieuan must be a genuinely competent rider/horse servant by 1355.

This is earned through:

- cattle background;
- Rhys's training;
- pack/stable work;
- years of daily horse care;
- campaign experience.

No instant warhorse mastery.

## Condition C — armed service before 1356

Ieuan must have served in situations where he is expected to carry/use weapons **before** Poitiers.

The battle cannot be his first real fight.

## Condition D — recognised patron

A knight or high-status retainer must have publicly taken responsibility for Ieuan's advancement.

This patron provides:

- reputation;
- access;
- equipment;
- the social claim that Ieuan belongs among armed household men.

Without patronage, talent alone is not enough.

## Condition E — esquire/man-at-arms status before Poitiers

By September 1356 Ieuan must already be treated as more than a groom.

The final terminology must be research-checked, but functionally he needs to:

- attend a knight in war;
- possess/use appropriate armour and horse(s) through household resources;
- fight as part of the armed retinue;
- be recognised by other men-at-arms as belonging in that layer.

## Condition F — exceptional Poitiers service

Ieuan must perform something memorable enough that his patron can justify elevation.

It should use skills built across the whole novel.

Good possibilities:

- keeps/remounts horses under dangerous conditions at the decisive moment;
- rescues a wounded patron while still completing a military task;
- protects a banner/standard rather than seeking personal glory;
- captures an important prisoner;
- holds a gap/route long enough for others to act;
- combines animal skill, terrain awareness and combat discipline.

Avoid superhero kill-count logic.

## Condition G — economic support

Ieuan needs a believable means of bearing knightly costs after the accolade.

Possible combination:

- share of booty;
- prisoner ransom;
- reward from patron;
- wages/annuity;
- later land/office opportunity.

The ending should make clear that becoming a knight gives him **new obligations**, not merely a cool title.

---

# 4. Who should knight him?

## Option A — immediate patron

**Most socially intimate.**

The man who has watched Ieuan rise formally recognises the achievement.

Problem: the patron must have sufficient standing/resources for the elevation to carry weight.

## Option B — Edward le Despenser

**Strongest thematic circle.**

Edward is the lord of Ieuan's home region, almost his contemporary, and historically fights at Poitiers.

This can work if Ieuan has served within Despenser's wider military/household affinity long enough before the battle.

It does **not** work if Edward first learns Ieuan's name on the battlefield.

## Option C — Prince of Wales

Historically powerful but narratively dangerous.

This risks making Ieuan unrealistically central to famous history.

Use only if the later outline creates sustained direct household contact.

### Current preference

**Edward le Despenser or Ieuan's immediate senior patron, with Despenser present/approving.**

This closes the Glamorgan-to-Poitiers circle without turning Ieuan into the Prince's personal favourite.

---

# 5. Ransom as the missing economic key

The Poitiers battlefield generated extraordinary opportunities for taking prisoners.

A useful potential ending mechanism:

1. Ieuan saves/captures a French knight of meaningful but not absurd rank.
2. He protects the prisoner rather than murdering him for equipment.
3. The resulting ransom is shared/regulated according to his retinue obligations.
4. Ieuan's resulting share is enough to materially change his economic position.
5. His patron can now argue that knighthood is not only deserved by service but financially supportable.

This ties chivalric behaviour, practical economics and social advancement together.

Do **not** give Ieuan King John II or another famous prisoner.

---

# 6. Thematic requirement

The accolade must not read as:

> The poor boy finally escaped being poor.

It should feel more complicated.

At fourteen Ieuan wanted only:

- his family alive;
- nobody to know he wet the bed;
- his father to trust him.

At twenty-one/twenty-two he may kneel to receive knighthood after helping an army burn, plunder and kill its way through France.

The achievement should therefore carry pride **and unease**.

The last pages must remember the boy from Glynrhondda.

---

# 7. Current verdict

**Plausible only if the intermediate social promotion is shown on-page.**

The ending is not historically ordinary, but it is not automatically impossible.

The main remaining research task is not Poitiers itself.

It is the middle rung:

> **How exactly does Ieuan move from useful mounted servant to socially recognised esquire/man-at-arms between roughly 1352 and 1355?**

Pass Three should concentrate there next.
