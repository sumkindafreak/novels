# Poitiers Prisoner, Ransom and War Profit

This file solves the economic half of Ieuan's final rise.

## Core rule

Poitiers can make Ieuan **worthy** of knighthood, but battlefield bravery alone does not make knightly status financially sustainable.

The novel therefore uses a historically grounded combination of:

- a captive French knight;
- negotiated ransom;
- retinue/customary shares;
- Ieuan's accumulated wages;
- later patronage and income.

The ransom is a **bridge**, not a magic fortune.

---

# 1. Historical ransom culture

Prisoners were a central part of Hundred Years War profit.

Froissart's Poitiers narrative describes large numbers of French prisoners being taken and many being put to ransom or released on parole to return with payment.

The famous Thomas/Maurice Berkeley episode describes a French squire receiving **6,000 nobles** for a very valuable captive and becoming a knight because of the resulting profit.

That is deliberately treated as an **extreme aristocratic example**, not our model for Ieuan.

Modern scholarship on ransom culture shows that military indentures and customary practice could require a captor to pass a proportion of winnings upward to his commander. A later commonly documented pattern is a **third** to the captor's superior, with further superior/Crown claims possible.

We do not possess a surviving Warwick-specific 1356 agreement telling us the exact percentage that Ieuan would owe.

### Novel rule

Do not state a universal legal formula such as 'every captor always kept exactly two thirds'.

Instead show that:

- Ieuan has a valid personal claim to the prisoner because he accepted the surrender;
- Iorwerth/Warwick's retinue recognises that claim;
- the company also has claims on war profit;
- costs and obligations reduce what Ieuan ultimately receives.

Sources:
- Rémy Ambühl, *Prisoners of War in the Hundred Years War: Ransom Culture in the Late Middle Ages*
- Reviews in History review of Ambühl
- Froissart, Poitiers narrative

# 2. The fictional prisoner

**Working name:** Sir Guillaume de la Motte  
**Status:** fictional minor French knight  
**Age:** early-to-mid 30s  
**Region:** Poitou / wider Poitiers military world  
**Rank:** landed knight of respectable but non-famous standing

Guillaume must not be:

- a count/baron;
- a royal officer of major fame;
- connected to King John's capture;
- worth an absurd fortune;
- written as a coward merely because he surrenders.

He is a professional/gentle warrior whose defeat creates an obligation under the law/custom of arms.

### Character use

Guillaume is most useful if Ieuan recognises something uncomfortable in him:

- he is frightened;
- concerned for his household/lands;
- worries what ransom will do to his family;
- has fought well;
- is not a monster.

That prevents Poitiers becoming a simple English-good/French-bad climax.

# 3. Working ransom scale

## Locked planning figure

**100 marks**.

100 marks = **£66 13s 4d** sterling.

This is deliberately modest compared with elite ransoms.

Comparative evidence from medieval warfare shows knightly ransoms ranging around £100, 200 marks, £200 and far higher depending on wealth/status; scholarship also notes a convention that ransom should relate to a captive's means/revenue rather than simply an arbitrary maximum.

Therefore **100 marks for a respectable minor knight is a defensible fictional figure**, not a claimed historical ransom for a named Poitiers captive.

Evidence grade: **B/C**.

---

# 4. Why 100 marks matters to Ieuan

Standard mid-fourteenth-century military wage benchmarks:

- ordinary man-at-arms/esquire: about **1 shilling per day**;
- knight: about **2 shillings per day**.

At 1s/day, a full year of man-at-arms wages is about **£18 5s**.

So a nominal 100-mark ransom equals more than three years of Ieuan's basic man-at-arms wage.

But he does **not** simply put £66 in a bag.

Likely reductions include:

- retinue/superior share;
- any agreed share to companions involved in the capture/recovery;
- prisoner food/guard/travel costs;
- delayed or staged payment;
- possible debt/pledge arrangements.

## Working net to Ieuan

For planning, assume Ieuan ultimately controls roughly **40–50 marks** of value/profit after obligations and costs.

That is approximately:

- 40 marks = £26 13s 4d
- 50 marks = £33 6s 8d

This is a life-changing amount for Ieuan.

It is **not** enough by itself to sustain permanent knightly status.

That distinction is critical.

---

# 5. The ransom should not be instantly paid

Historically ransom could involve:

- parole;
- pledges/hostages;
- instalments;
- written obligations;
- delays while property/family raised funds.

Therefore the novel should not have Guillaume hand Ieuan a chest of coins beside the battlefield.

## Working story sequence

1. Guillaume yields to Ieuan at Poitiers.
2. His identity/status is established after the fighting.
3. A ransom is negotiated at roughly 100 marks.
4. Guillaume or his representatives provide obligation/security.
5. Ieuan receives only part immediately, with the remainder becoming a valuable claim.
6. The recognised claim itself improves Ieuan's credit/status even before every penny is collected.

This makes the economics much more believable.

---

# 6. Why the capture matters socially

Taking a prisoner does more than produce money.

Ieuan has:

- fought successfully as a man-at-arms;
- accepted surrender under the law/custom of arms;
- protected a prisoner instead of killing him for equipment;
- produced a recognised economic asset for himself and his military superiors;
- gained witnesses to his conduct.

That is exactly the sort of event a patron can use to argue that Ieuan has crossed from useful low-born fighting man into genuinely knightly behaviour.

---

# 7. What the ransom does NOT do

The ransom does not:

- erase Ieuan's birth;
- make him a rich baron;
- automatically make him a landowner;
- automatically make him a knight;
- give him a castle;
- resolve his shame/grief.

It gives him **capital and credibility**.

The final requirement remains recurring income/patronage.

# 8. Evidence notes

### A / direct

- Poitiers produced enormous numbers of prisoners and ransom opportunities.
- Froissart preserves a Poitiers example where ransom wealth enables a squire's later knighthood.
- English military pay in this period places ordinary men-at-arms around 1s/day and knights around 2s/day.

### B

- ransom obligations passing shares up through retinue hierarchies;
- using around one year's means/revenue as a broad guide to a captive's sustainable ransom.

### C

- Guillaume de la Motte;
- 100-mark negotiated figure;
- Ieuan's exact net share;
- exact wording/timing of the ransom agreement.
